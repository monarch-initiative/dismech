Subtypes

5
Sensitizer-induced occupational asthma
Asthma caused by immunological sensitization to an agent encountered at work, after a latency period, and reproduced on re-exposure to that agent. The causative agents split into high molecular weight protein allergens (flour, enzymes, latex, animal proteins), which sensitize through conventional IgE-mediated routes, and low molecular weight chemicals (diisocyanates, acid anhydrides, persulfate salts, reactive dyes), which must first haptenate a carrier protein. Only the chemical arm conforms to the `chemical_respiratory_allergy` module, so the two arms are separate rows below rather than being collapsed into this one.
  • LMW-chemical
  • HMW-protein
Low molecular weight chemical sensitizer-induced
Sensitization to a reactive low molecular weight chemical, which must haptenate a carrier protein before the immune system can recognize it. This is the arm the `chemical_respiratory_allergy` module models, and the haptenation chain in this entry is scoped to it.
  • Isocyanate-induced

High molecular weight protein allergen-induced
Sensitization to an inhaled protein allergen — flour, enzymes, latex, or laboratory animal proteins — which is immunogenic as supplied and needs no haptenation step. Conventional IgE-mediated sensitization, with specific IgE usually detectable, unlike the chemical arm. Its mechanism is not curated here and it does not conform to `chemical_respiratory_allergy`, whose trigger node requires a reactive chemical; see `notes`.

Isocyanate-induced occupational asthma MONDO:0025556
The single largest cause of sensitizer-induced occupational asthma, from toluene, methylene diphenyl and hexamethylene diisocyanate used in polyurethane manufacture, spray painting and foam production. Specific IgE is detectable in only a minority of affected workers, so diagnosis rests on the work-related pattern and specific inhalation challenge rather than on serology.
Irritant-induced occupational asthma
Asthma caused by a non-immunological irritant injury to the airway, with no latency and no agent-specific immune memory. Includes reactive airways dysfunction syndrome after a single high-dose exposure, and the lower-intensity repeated-exposure form. Conforms to the `inhaled_irritant_airway_injury` module rather than to `chemical_respiratory_allergy`. No MONDO term covers this stratum: OLS returns only `MONDO:0004979` asthma for `irritant induced asthma` and nothing at all for `reactive airways dysfunction syndrome`, and `MONDO:0022742` has no children, so the row carries no `subtype_term`.

Pathophysiology

Workplace Sensitizer Haptenation of Airway Proteins
A reactive low molecular weight chemical inhaled at the workplace binds nucleophilic residues on airway and serum proteins, generating the hapten-protein adduct that the immune system subsequently recognizes. This is the step that requires the agent to be chemically reactive, and it is why the low molecular weight agents behave differently from the protein allergens that sensitize directly.

Airway Epithelial Danger Signal Release on Workplace Exposure
The bronchial epithelium, lung fibroblasts and endothelium exposed to the sensitizer release IL-8, IL-6 and IL-1beta, generate reactive oxygen species, lose MUC1, and upregulate endothelial adhesion molecules. This innate response licenses dendritic cell maturation but is not by itself specific to a sensitizer, which is why an irritant exposure can produce a similar picture without sensitization following.

Hapten-Specific T Cell Memory
Hapten-specific T cells expand and polarize toward a type 2 profile, with IL-4, IL-5 and IL-13 and airway eosinophilia. This establishes the immunological memory that gives sensitizer-induced occupational asthma its defining clinical features: a latency period before onset, and reproducible symptoms on re-exposure to that agent and not to others. Specific IgE may accompany it but frequently does not with low molecular weight agents.

Irritant Injury of the Airway Epithelial Barrier
In the irritant-induced stratum there is no haptenation and no agent-specific memory. A reactive inhaled irritant injures the epithelium directly, disrupting tight junctions and raising paracellular permeability, and the resulting inflammation produces asthma without a latency period. This is why irritant-induced occupational asthma begins within hours of a high-dose exposure rather than after months of low-level exposure.

Work-Related Airway Hyperresponsiveness and Variable Airflow Limitation
The convergence point of both strata and the clinical disease. Airway smooth muscle contraction, airway hyperresponsiveness and variable airflow limitation, with the temporal pattern that defines the diagnosis: symptoms worse at work and improving away from it. Removal from exposure does not reliably resolve it, so this node represents a persistent state rather than a transient response to exposure. Both strata converge here by different routes: the sensitizer arm through hapten-specific memory, the irritant arm through direct epithelial injury. The `conforms_to` link is true of the sensitizer route; the irritant module's own consequence is mucociliary clearance failure rather than asthma, so it is deliberately not named as a second target.

Workplace exposure elimination as primary prevention
Action: Therapeutic ProcedureNCI Thesaurus (NCIT) Relation: this treatment is this clinical intervention This treatment is Therapeutic Procedure (NCIT:C49236). NCIT:C49236 is a clinical intervention from the NCI Thesaurus. NCIT:C49236
Platform: Behavioral / lifestyle
Removing or substituting the sensitizer in the workplace so that workers are never exposed. This is a different intervention from the removal row above, which withdraws an already-affected worker: this one prevents the disease arising at all, and the evidence for it is the strongest in the field. Powder-free low-protein natural rubber latex gloves are the worked case, where substitution was followed by a sharp fall in new sensitization.
Mechanism Target:
INHIBITS Workplace Sensitizer Haptenation of Airway Proteins — Eliminating the agent from the workplace removes the reactive chemical that haptenates airway protein, so the chain never starts. This is why the intervention is preventive rather than therapeutic.

Occupational Asthma (MONDO:0022742): A Comprehensive Disease Characteristics Report

MONDO:0022742 · ICD-11 CA23 (asthma, occupational) · ICD-10 J45 (+ external-cause codes; J68.3 for irritant/RADS) · MeSH "Asthma, Occupational" · no OMIM / no single Mendelian causal gene

Causal gene — stated up front, per instructions: Occupational asthma has no single Mendelian causal gene. It is a complex, polygenic, environmentally-caused disease in which an occupational inhalational exposure (sensitizer or irritant) is the necessary cause and host genetics act as susceptibility modifiers. The dominant susceptibility loci are the HLA class II genes on chromosome 6p21 — principally HLA-DQB1 (with agent-specific haplotypes such as HLA-DRB1*15–DPB1*05 for isocyanates and HLA-DQ5/DQB1*0501 + DR1 for acid anhydrides), together with non-HLA modifiers in oxidative-stress and antigen-processing pathways (GSTM1, GSTP1, CTNNA3, NAT1/2, TNF). This is a disease defined by its environmental cause plus a polygenic HLA-centred susceptibility background — not by a causal gene.

Summary

Occupational asthma (OA) is a complex, environmentally-caused, adult-onset form of asthma defined by variable airflow limitation and/or airway hyperresponsiveness attributable to causes in the working environment. The necessary cause of OA is workplace exposure — more than 400 agents are documented — acting upon a polygenic susceptibility background centred on the HLA class II region (principally HLA-DQB1, with agent-specific haplotypes), supplemented by non-HLA modifier genes in antigen-processing, lymphocyte-activation, cytokine-regulation, and oxidative-stress pathways (e.g., GSTM1/GSTP1, CTNNA3, NAT, TNF). OA is therefore categorized as a complex gene–environment disease, not a monogenic disorder.

OA is epidemiologically substantial: pooled longitudinal data attribute ~16–17% of adult-onset asthma to occupational exposures (population attributable fraction). It presents in two mechanistically distinct forms. Sensitizer-induced (allergic) OA follows a latency period of exposure and is driven either by high-molecular-weight (HMW, >10 kDa) complete protein allergens (flour/grain glycoproteins, enzymes/α-amylase, latex, animal danders, molds) or by low-molecular-weight (LMW) chemical haptens (isocyanates, acid anhydrides, platinum salts, persulfates, quaternary ammonium compounds) that conjugate to airway proteins to form neoantigens; both converge on an HLA class II → CD4⁺ Th2 (IL-4/IL-5/IL-13) → IgE / eosinophilic airway inflammation axis. Irritant-induced asthma, including reactive airways dysfunction syndrome (RADS), is a separate non-immunologic entity caused by direct epithelial injury after high-level irritant exposure (classically chlorine, ammonia, acid fumes), characteristically without a latency period or specific immune sensitization.

Diagnosis is confirmed by specific inhalation challenge (SIC), the reference standard, supported by spirometry, non-specific bronchial hyperresponsiveness testing, serial peak flow (OASYS), specific IgE, and the complementary inflammatory biomarkers FeNO and sputum eosinophils. Prognosis is frequently poor: only ~32% symptomatic recovery after exposure cessation, with persistent non-specific bronchial hyperresponsiveness in ~73%. Complete removal from exposure gives the best clinical outcome but the worst socioeconomic outcome (income/job loss), the central management trade-off. Primary prevention by exposure elimination/reduction is the single most effective intervention, with powder-free low-protein latex gloves providing landmark proof-of-principle. This report synthesizes 12 confirmed findings and 53 reviewed papers across all 15 requested disease-characteristic domains.

Key Findings

Finding 1 — OA is a complex, environmentally-caused disease with three main phenotypes and no single causal gene

Occupational asthma is asthma caused by workplace conditions. It is subdivided into sensitizer-induced (allergic) OA and irritant-induced (non-allergic) OA; together with work-exacerbated asthma (WEA) — pre-existing asthma made worse but not initially caused by work — these constitute the umbrella category work-related asthma (WRA). More than 400 workplace agents are implicated. The recognized disease phenotypes are: OA caused by HMW agents, OA caused by LMW agents, irritant-induced asthma, and an OA–COPD overlap phenotype.

As the review by Lemière and colleagues states: "OA is asthma caused by workplace conditions, and is subdivided into sensitizer-induced (allergic) OA and irritant-induced (nonallergic) OA. WEA is asthma that is made worse, but was not initially caused, by workplace conditions" (PMID: 26024347). The phenotypic subdivision is confirmed by Vandenplas et al.: "The main phenotypes of occupational asthma are: occupational asthma caused by high-molecular-weight (HMW) or low-molecular-weight (LMW) agents, irritant-induced asthma and occupational asthma-chronic obstructive pulmonary disease overlap" (PMID: 33141780). Evidence source: human clinical / aggregated disease-level reviews.

Finding 2 — Occupational exposures account for ~16–17% of adult-onset asthma

Pooled data from nine longitudinal studies yielded a population attributable fraction (PAF) of 16% for the occupational contribution to incident asthma: "Data from nine longitudinal studies showed a population attributable fraction for the occupational contribution to incident asthma of 16%" (PMID: 33141780). This is independently corroborated by the population-based Telemark 5-year cohort (n=7120), in which new-onset asthma incidence was 7.5 per 1000 person-years, ever exposure to vapour/gas/dust/fumes (VGDF) carried an odds ratio of 1.49 (95% CI 1.15–1.94) with a clear exposure–response (daily exposure OR 2.46, 95% CI 1.39–4.35): "The corresponding PAF for ever exposed to VGDF was 17% (95% CI 5.4% to 27.8%) and the risk of asthma onset increased with frequent VGDF exposure" (PMID: 39277201). GBD-2021 analysis separately attributed 13.9% of young-adult asthma DALYs to environmental/occupational risks (PMID: 42323982). Evidence source: human epidemiological cohorts.

Finding 3 — Causative agents divide into HMW complete allergens and LMW haptens; genetic susceptibility modifies risk

HMW proteins (>10 kDa) — flour/grain glycoproteins, fungal α-amylase and other enzymes, natural rubber latex, animal danders, avian and insect proteins, molds — act as complete allergens driving IgE sensitization. LMW agents — isocyanates, acid anhydrides, metals/platinum salts, quaternary ammonium compounds, persulfates — act as haptens that bind airway proteins. Only a subset of exposed workers develop disease, implicating genetic susceptibility. As Cannon et al. summarize: "OA can be triggered by high-molecular-weight (HMW) proteins that act as complete allergens or by low-molecular-weight (LMW) sensitizers that act as haptens" (PMID: 31690384). A systematic review/meta-analysis of diisocyanate asthma found: "only a subset of workers exposed to diisocyanates develop diisocyanate-induced occupational asthma (diisocyanate asthma, DA), indicating that genetic susceptibility may play a role", with associated gene functions spanning "antigen processing, lymphocyte activation, cytokine production regulation, and response to oxidative stress" (PMID: 36276967). Evidence source: human clinical + genetic meta-analysis.

Finding 4 — Specific inhalation challenge is the diagnostic gold standard; prognosis is often poor

Diagnosis combines clinical history, spirometry/bronchodilator responsiveness, non-specific bronchial hyperresponsiveness testing, specific IgE, serial peak-flow analysis (OASYS), and SIC as the reference standard: "specific inhalation challenge (SIC) is considered as the reference standard and represents the best way to confirm the specific cause of WRA" (PMID: 33652998). Specific IgE performance differs by agent class — for HMW agents sensitivity ~0.74/specificity ~0.71, for LMW agents sensitivity 0.28/specificity 0.89. A systematic review of 39 studies (1681 patients) quantified the poor prognosis: "Reported rates of symptomatic recovery varied from 0% to 100%, with a pooled estimate of 32% (95% CI 26% to 38%)" and "The pooled prevalence of persistent NSBHR at follow-up was 73% (95% CI 66% to 79%)" (PMID: 17040933). Severity at diagnosis and airway inflammation predict persistence; early diagnosis and removal improve outcome. Evidence source: human clinical / systematic review.

HLA-DQB1 is the haplotype most associated with occupational asthma, in contrast to HLA-DRB1 in general allergic asthma and HLA-DPB1 in aspirin-sensitive asthma: "The most common HLA haplotypes in the different asthma phenotypes are HLA-DRB1 in allergic asthma, HLA-DQB1 in occupational asthma and HLA-DPB1 in aspirin-sensitive asthma" (PMID: 24994462). Finding 6 — Immunopathogenesis: hapten–protein conjugates drive CD4⁺ Th2 responses with eosinophilic airway inflammation

LMW agents such as diisocyanates react with biological nucleophiles to form diisocyanate-adducted (hapten–carrier) biomolecules that act as neoantigens: "The importance of diisocyanate-adducted biomolecules will be emphasized and their potential contributions to pathogenesis discussed" (PMID: 11811926). Sensitization is associated with recruitment of CD4⁺ T lymphocytes and Th2 cytokine production: "The diisocyanate-induced sensitization, is associated with the recruitment of CD4 T lymphocytes to the lungs and the production of Th2-type cytokines, including IL-4, IL-5 and IL-13" (PMID: 22774468). A Wistar rat TDI-vapor model confirmed eosinophilic Th2 inflammation: "marked infiltration of eosinophils in the TDI-exposed rats. The cytokine assay showed that IL-4 and IL-5 were predominantly produced in the BAL fluid in vivo" (PMID: 24570807). Prevalence of diisocyanate asthma among exposed workers is ~5–15%. Evidence source: human clinical mechanism + rodent model.

Finding 7 — Primary prevention by exposure control is the most effective intervention

A systematic review of 29 studies concluded: "Exposure elimination is the strongest and preferred primary preventive approach to reduce the burden of occupational asthma" and "There is strong evidence to recommend not using powdered allergen-rich natural rubber latex gloves" (PMID: 22654083). Interventional proof-of-principle comes from a health-care-worker cohort: "After the intervention, new latex sensitization rates declined 16-fold, and 25% of previously sensitized employees reverted to negative skin tests" (PMID: 21775898); pre-intervention sensitization was dose-dependent on airborne latex antigen (low 2%, intermediate 8%, high 16%, P<0.0001). Germany's nationwide switch to low-protein powder-free gloves produced a steady decline in reported occupational latex allergy cases after 1998 (PMID: 12170275). Management of established disease centres on complete cessation of exposure plus standard asthma pharmacotherapy (inhaled corticosteroids ± bronchodilators; type-2 biologics — omalizumab, mepolizumab, dupilumab, tezepelumab — by extrapolation from severe type-2 asthma). Evidence source: human clinical / interventional + ecological.

Finding 8 — Animal and transgenic models recapitulate sensitizer-induced OA

Validated models include the Wistar rat exposed to TDI vapor (airway hyperresponsiveness, eosinophilic inflammation, IL-4/IL-5 in BAL); murine models of diisocyanate asthma, preferred for defined genome and reagent availability ("Murine models of diisocyanate-induced asthma have proved to be the most useful", PMID: 22774468); guinea pig and swine models of trimellitic anhydride ("Animal models revealed sIgG, sIgE, sIgA, and sIgM along with pulmonary lesions, cellular infiltrates, alveolar hemorrhage, and pneumonitis associated with TMA exposure", PMID: 32594360); and HLA-DQ8 / HLA class II transgenic mice reproducing detergent-enzyme hypersensitivity: "The DQ8 mice also developed allergic eosinophilic inflammatory reactions in the airways following intranasal instillations of this enzyme" (PMID: 16185928). Evidence source: model organism studies.

Finding 9 — Dominant risk factors are exposure intensity/duration, atopy, and concomitant rhinitis/conjunctivitis

Risk of sensitizer-induced OA rises with exposure intensity and duration and with host atopy. A florist study (n=128) found work-related asthma-like symptoms in 14.1%, with "The most prominent risk factors in florists were work intensity, work duration, and specific atopy" (PMID: 15189959); specific odds ratios were high work intensity OR 7.3 (1.1–51.8), long duration OR 5.1 (1.2–21.6), specific flower atopy OR 5.9 (1.4–24.3), and notably "excess risk for work-related asthma-like symptoms among those with allergic rhinitis (OR, 13.2; 95% CI, 3.1 to 56.4)" and conjunctivitis OR 8.4 (2.4–29.2). A pooled laboratory-animal-worker study (n=650) demonstrated the exposure–response for sensitization: "the likelihood of IgE-mediated sensitization increases with increasing exposure" (PMID: 10200019). Rhinitis frequently precedes/accompanies OA. Smoking is a risk factor for IgE sensitization to some HMW agents (platinum salts, anhydrides). Evidence source: human epidemiological studies.

Finding 10 — FeNO and sputum eosinophils are complementary biomarkers during SIC

A multicenter study (n=321 undergoing SIC, 156 positive) found post-challenge rises in FeNO and sputum eosinophils had similar diagnostic accuracy (AUC 0.78 and 0.81). Optimal thresholds were defined: "Increases in FeNO level ≥ 13 ppb and sputum eosinophils ≥ 1.25% were identified as the optimal threshold values for differentiating positive from negative SICs" — each ≥95% specific but of low sensitivity (55% and 62%) — and "Combining either a rise in FeNO ≥ 13 ppb or an increase in sputum eosinophils ≥ 1.25% increased the sensitivity to 77%" (PMID: 39726396). Increases were discordant in 38% of positives, indicating complementary information. Evidence source: human clinical / diagnostic accuracy study.

Finding 11 — Irritant-induced asthma / RADS is a distinct non-immunologic phenotype with rapid onset

Irritant-induced OA is a separate non-immunologic entity. RADS is an asthma-like syndrome persisting >3 months, beginning within minutes to hours after a single high-concentration irritant exposure: "RADS is defined as an asthma-like syndrome that persists for >3 months and occurs within minutes to hours after a single exposure to a high concentration of a respiratory irritant" (PMID: 31690384). Chlorine is the most common cause: "A variety of agents were implicated, with chlorine or chlorine-releasing molecules most frequently reported" (PMID: 33063819); other documented causes include ammonia, acid fumes, lithium hydroxide dust, and β-mercaptoethanol. Unlike sensitizer-induced OA, there is no latency period and no IgE/specific immune sensitization. Chronic moderate irritant exposure can also cause irritant-induced asthma "with latency": "there is epidemiological evidence for irritant-induced asthma with latency from chronic moderate exposure" (PMID: 30661311). Diagnosis uses Brooks (1985)/ACCP 2008 criteria with methacholine challenge (e.g., PC20 1.36–6.74 mg/mL). Evidence source: human clinical / case series + reviews.

Finding 12 — Complete removal gives best clinical but worst socioeconomic outcome; OA imposes major burden

A Cochrane review of workplace interventions found: "complete removal from exposure resulted in the best outcome in terms of symptoms, lung function, and airway hyper-responsiveness. Reduction of exposure appeared to be less effective in terms of improving asthma but was also less likely to result in loss of income or unemployment" (PMID: 23407122). A follow-up of 125 sensitizer-induced OA patients (median 18 months symptom duration before referral; 79% no longer exposed at 1 year) found unexposed patients had better symptoms (82% vs 53% improved, P=0.023) but "poorer outcomes in terms of career, income and how they felt treated by their employer" (PMID: 32307530). Diagnostic delay worsens prognosis: "In adult asthmatics, work-relatedness can account for 15%-33% of cases, but delays in diagnosis remain common and lead to worse outcomes" (PMID: 30661311). Evidence source: human clinical / systematic review + cohort.

Full Section-by-Section Report

1. Disease Information

Overview. Occupational asthma is adult-onset asthma — variable airflow obstruction, airway hyperresponsiveness, and airway inflammation — caused by agents or conditions in the workplace. It is the most common occupational lung disease in industrialized countries and belongs to the broader category of work-related asthma (WRA), which comprises OA (caused by work) and work-exacerbated asthma (worsened by, but not caused by, work) (PMID: 26024347).

Key identifiers. - Mondo: MONDO:0022742 (occupational asthma) - MeSH: "Asthma, Occupational" - ICD-10: J45 (asthma) with occupational specification; J68.3 (airway disease due to chemical fumes/vapors, for irritant/RADS) - ICD-11: CA23 (asthma) with external-cause coding for occupational origin - OMIM: Not applicable — no single Mendelian causal gene (complex trait) - Orphanet: Not a classic rare-disease entry; captured under occupational respiratory disorders

Synonyms: work-related asthma; occupational allergic asthma; sensitizer-induced occupational asthma; irritant-induced asthma; reactive airways dysfunction syndrome (RADS, for acute irritant form).

Data source type. Information is derived predominantly from aggregated disease-level resources — systematic reviews, occupational cohorts, registries (e.g., SHIELD, SWORD), and challenge-test case series — rather than from individual EHR records.

2. Etiology

Causal factors. The primary cause is environmental (occupational inhalational exposure), acting on a genetic susceptibility background. There is no infectious etiology. More than 400 agents are implicated, divided into HMW complete allergens and LMW chemical haptens, plus irritant agents (PMID: 34031758, PMID: 31690384).

Genetic risk factors. HLA class II alleles, principally HLA-DQB1, are the dominant susceptibility loci, with agent-specific haplotypes (Finding 5). Non-HLA modifiers: GSTM1/GSTP1 (oxidative stress), CTNNA3, NAT, TNF (PMID: 36276967, PMID: 24994462).

Environmental/occupational risk factors. Exposure intensity and duration (exposure–response relationship), high-risk occupations (bakers, farmers, spray painters, isocyanate/plastics workers, health-care workers, cleaners, woodworkers, laboratory-animal workers), atopy, and smoking (for some HMW agents) (PMID: 15189959, PMID: 10200019, PMID: 22794690). Exposure–response thresholds exist for specific allergens (wheat flour 1–2.4 mg/m³, fungal α-amylase 0.25 ng/m³, latex 0.6 ng/m³, rat allergen 0.7 µg/m³) (PMID: 9645589).

Protective factors. Reduction/elimination of exposure; engineering controls; respiratory protection (limited evidence); powder-free low-protein latex gloves (PMID: 22654083). HLA-DQB1*0501 is protective for isocyanate/plicatic-acid asthma. No well-established dietary protective factor is specific to OA.

Gene–environment interactions. The defining feature of OA: identical exposures produce disease only in genetically susceptible (e.g., HLA-DQB1-defined) individuals, and only a subset of exposed workers (~5–15% for diisocyanates) develop disease (PMID: 36276967).

4. Genetic / Molecular Information

Causal genes: None (complex disease). Susceptibility genes: HLA-DQB1 (HGNC:4944), HLA-DRB1 (HGNC:4948), HLA-DPB1 (HGNC:4940), HLA-E, HLA-DOA, HLA-DQA2; modifiers GSTM1 (HGNC:4632), GSTP1 (HGNC:4638), CTNNA3, NAT1/NAT2, TNF. Variants are germline common polymorphisms/SNPs and HLA haplotypes (not classic pathogenic Mendelian variants), classified as susceptibility alleles, not ACMG pathogenic variants. Allele frequencies are population-level HLA frequencies. Functional consequences relate to altered antigen presentation (HLA) and reduced detoxification capacity (GST null genotypes → oxidative stress). Epigenetic/chromosomal abnormalities: No recurrent chromosomal abnormality; epigenetic contributions are plausible but not yet firmly established (PMID: 36276967, PMID: 24709764).

5. Environmental Information

Environmental/occupational factors. HMW: wheat/grain flour and glycoproteins, fungal/bacterial enzymes (α-amylase, subtilisin), natural rubber latex, animal danders/urinary proteins, avian proteins, molds. LMW haptens: diisocyanates (TDI, MDI, HDI; CHEBI toluene diisocyanate), acid anhydrides (trimellitic, phthalic, HHPA/MHHPA), platinum salts, persulfates (hairdressing), quaternary ammonium compounds, cleaning/disinfectant chemicals (chloramine, benzalkonium chloride). Irritants: chlorine (CHEBI:17997), ammonia (CHEBI:16134), acid fumes, lithium hydroxide dust, β-mercaptoethanol (PMID: 34031758, PMID: 34247391, PMID: 42427009).

Lifestyle factors. Smoking increases IgE sensitization to some HMW agents (platinum salts, anhydrides). Infectious agents: Not applicable — OA is non-infectious (molds and bacterial-derived enzymes act as allergens, not as infections).

6. Mechanism / Pathophysiology

Ordered causal chain — sensitizer-induced (allergic) OA:

  1. Workplace inhalation exposure to an HMW allergen or LMW chemical → deposition on the airway epithelium.
  2. (LMW branch) The hapten reacts with biological nucleophiles (airway/serum proteins, e.g., albumin) → forms a hapten–carrier conjugate (neoantigen) [demonstrated for diisocyanates; PMID: 11811926]. (HMW branch) The intact protein is a complete allergen requiring no conjugation.
  3. Antigen uptake by dendritic/antigen-presenting cells and presentation on HLA class II molecules (principally HLA-DQ) → activates CD4⁺ T lymphocytes in genetically susceptible (HLA-DQB1-defined) hosts [inferred from HLA association + demonstrated CD4⁺ recruitment; PMID: 24994462, PMID: 22774468].
  4. CD4⁺ T cells polarize to Th2 → secrete IL-4, IL-5, IL-13 → drive B-cell IgE class-switching and eosinophil recruitment/activation [demonstrated; PMID: 22774468, PMID: 24570807].
  5. Re-exposure → IgE-mediated mast-cell degranulation + eosinophilic inflammation → airway smooth-muscle contraction, mucosal edema, mucus hypersecretion → variable airflow obstruction and bronchial hyperresponsiveness (clinical asthma).
  6. Chronic inflammation → airway remodeling → persistent, often irreversible hyperresponsiveness even after exposure cessation [demonstrated; PMID: 17040933, PMID: 18394133].

Ordered causal chain — irritant-induced asthma / RADS:

  1. Single high-concentration irritant exposure (e.g., chlorine, ammonia, LiOH dust) → direct toxic epithelial injury (no immune sensitization, no latency).
  2. Epithelial denudation → neurogenic inflammation, oxidative stress, innate immune activation → bronchial hyperresponsiveness.
  3. Airway injury/repair → persistent asthma-like syndrome (>3 months) with bronchial hyperresponsiveness [demonstrated clinically; PMID: 31690384, PMID: 42427009].

Molecular pathways / GO terms: antigen processing and presentation via MHC class II (GO:0019886), T-helper 2 cell differentiation (GO:0045064), IL-4/IL-5/IL-13 cytokine signaling, immune response (GO:0006955), eosinophil chemotaxis (GO:0048245), response to oxidative stress (GO:0006979), inflammatory response (GO:0006954). Cell types / CL terms: CD4⁺ Th2 lymphocyte (CL:0000899), eosinophil (CL:0000771), mast cell (CL:0000097), dendritic cell (CL:0000451), bronchial epithelial cell (CL:0002328), airway smooth muscle cell (CL:0000192), B cell/plasma cell (CL:0000786). Upstream: exposure, hapten conjugation, HLA presentation. Downstream: Th2 cytokines, IgE, eosinophilia, remodeling.

7. Anatomical Structures Affected

  • Primary organ: lung / bronchi (UBERON:0002048 lung; UBERON:0002185 bronchus); body system: respiratory system (UBERON:0001004).
  • Secondary involvement: upper airway — nasal mucosa/nose (UBERON:0001707) with occupational rhinitis; conjunctiva (UBERON:0001811) with conjunctivitis.
  • Tissue level: bronchial/airway epithelium, airway smooth muscle, lamina propria/submucosa.
  • Cell populations (CL): bronchial epithelial cell (CL:0002328), airway smooth muscle cell (CL:0000192), eosinophil (CL:0000771), CD4⁺ Th2 cell (CL:0000899), mast cell (CL:0000097).
  • Subcellular (GO CC): MHC class II protein complex (GO:0042613); secretory granules of eosinophils/mast cells.
  • Localization/lateralization: bilateral, diffuse airway involvement (PMID: 22794690, PMID: 31690384).

8. Temporal Development

  • Onset: adult-onset, work-age. Sensitizer-induced OA requires a latency period (weeks to years) of exposure for sensitization (insidious/subacute). RADS is acute, within minutes–hours of a single exposure.
  • Progression: episodic initially (worse at work, better away), may become persistent/progressive with ongoing exposure and remodeling.
  • Course/duration: often chronic and lifelong; persistent bronchial hyperresponsiveness in ~73% after cessation (PMID: 17040933).
  • Patterns: partial remission possible after cessation (~32% symptomatic recovery); critical period — early diagnosis and prompt removal markedly improve prognosis (PMID: 9383254, PMID: 30661311).

9. Inheritance and Population

  • Epidemiology: OA accounts for ~16–17% of adult-onset asthma (PAF) (PMID: 33141780, PMID: 39277201); work-relatedness 15–33% of adult asthma (PMID: 30661311). New-onset asthma incidence ~7.5/1000 person-years in an exposed population cohort.
  • Inheritance pattern: multifactorial/polygenic (not Mendelian). No single-gene inheritance, penetrance, expressivity, anticipation, mosaicism, founder effect, consanguinity, or carrier-frequency metrics apply classically; "penetrance" of HLA risk alleles is low and exposure-dependent.
  • Demographics: occupation-determined rather than ethnicity-determined. Sex ratio reflects workforce composition of exposed industries; age distribution: working adults; geographic distribution follows industrial/agricultural activity (PMID: 42323982, PMID: 39212906).

10. Diagnostics

  • Functional tests: spirometry with bronchodilator responsiveness; non-specific bronchial hyperresponsiveness (methacholine/histamine, PC20); serial peak expiratory flow at/away from work (OASYS).
  • Immunologic tests: specific IgE / skin-prick tests (HMW sensitivity ~0.74, specificity ~0.71; LMW sensitivity 0.28, specificity 0.89).
  • Inflammatory biomarkers: FeNO rise ≥13 ppb and sputum eosinophils ≥1.25% during SIC (each ≥95% specific; combined sensitivity 77%) (PMID: 39726396).
  • Reference standard: specific inhalation challenge (SIC) (PMID: 33652998, PMID: 22191204).
  • Genetic/omics testing: Not used diagnostically — HLA typing is a research tool.
  • Clinical criteria: work-relatedness by history + objective evidence; RADS by Brooks (1985)/ACCP 2008 criteria.
  • Differential diagnosis: work-exacerbated (pre-existing) asthma, hypersensitivity pneumonitis (CD4/CD8 <1 on BAL), COPD, eosinophilic bronchitis, vocal cord dysfunction (PMID: 22794690).

11. Outcome / Prognosis

  • Mortality: low; OA is rarely directly fatal (though fatal asthma attacks and fatal anaphylaxis to occupational allergens occur).
  • Morbidity/function: substantial — persistent airflow obstruction, chronic bronchial hyperresponsiveness (~73%), long-term FEV1 decline (median ~79 mL/year in one diisocyanate cohort) (PMID: 18394133).
  • Recovery: only ~32% symptomatic recovery after exposure cessation (PMID: 17040933).
  • Prognostic factors: severity at diagnosis, degree of airway inflammation, duration of symptoms before diagnosis, and promptness of removal. Favourable prognosis is associated with short symptom duration, immediate cessation, and milder hyperresponsiveness at diagnosis (PMID: 9383254, PMID: 15764901).
  • Socioeconomic burden: career disruption, income/job loss, especially after complete removal (PMID: 32307530).

12. Treatment

  • Cornerstone: removal from / avoidance of the causative exposure (NCIT: avoidance). Complete removal gives best clinical outcome; reduction is a clinical–socioeconomic compromise (PMID: 23407122).
  • Pharmacotherapy (NCIT): inhaled corticosteroids (NCIT:C1130), short-/long-acting β2-agonists / bronchodilators, leukotriene receptor antagonists, antihistamines — standard asthma therapy extrapolated to OA.
  • Advanced/biologic therapy: type-2 biologics — omalizumab (anti-IgE), mepolizumab (anti-IL-5), dupilumab (anti-IL-4Rα), tezepelumab (anti-TSLP) — effective in severe type-2 asthma and applied by extrapolation to severe type-2 OA (PMID: 32007172, PMID: 38878250).
  • No gene/cell/RNA therapy applies (no monogenic target).
  • Treatment strategy: accurate diagnosis → exposure management → pharmacotherapy → monitoring; personalized by phenotype (type-2 vs non-type-2) (PMID: 33141780, PMID: 34031758).

13. Prevention

  • Primary prevention (most effective): exposure elimination > reduction; substitution of safer materials; engineering controls; powder-free low-protein latex gloves (PMID: 22654083, PMID: 21775898, PMID: 12170275); enforceable exposure thresholds (TLVs) for allergens (PMID: 9645589).
  • Secondary prevention: medical surveillance of exposed workers; early symptom/sensitization detection; prompt removal (PMID: 40306950).
  • Tertiary prevention: optimizing asthma control, preventing irreversible remodeling.
  • Respiratory protection (PPE): limited supporting evidence; HEPA-filtered helmets reduce latex symptoms (PMID: 9847441).
  • Immunization / genetic counseling: not applicable.

14. Other Species / Natural Disease

  • Species used as models/affected: rat (Rattus norvegicus, NCBITaxon:10116), mouse (Mus musculus, NCBITaxon:10090), guinea pig (Cavia porcellus, NCBITaxon:10141), swine (Sus scrofa, NCBITaxon:9823) — all as experimental models (PMID: 24570807, PMID: 32594360).
  • Natural disease: OA is fundamentally a human occupational disease; no established naturally-occurring companion-animal equivalent. Orthologous susceptibility genes (HLA orthologs — murine H2/I-A/I-E; GST orthologs) exist. Zoonotic potential: none (non-infectious).

15. Model Organisms

  • Mammalian models: Wistar rat (TDI vapor) — airway hyperresponsiveness, eosinophilic inflammation, IL-4/IL-5 BAL (PMID: 24570807); murine diisocyanate models (preferred for defined genome/reagents) (PMID: 22774468); guinea pig/swine trimellitic-anhydride models (PMID: 32594360).
  • Genetic/humanized models: HLA-DQ8 and HLA class II transgenic mice reproducing detergent-enzyme (subtilisin BPN') hypersensitivity with antigen-specific T-cell responses, airway eosinophilia, and IgG1/IgE (PMID: 16185928).
  • Phenotype recapitulation: models reproduce Th2/eosinophilic airway inflammation, hyperresponsiveness, and HLA-dependent sensitization central to human OA.
  • Limitations: rodent airway anatomy/immunology differs from human; models capture sensitization/acute inflammation better than chronic remodeling and socioeconomic/persistence aspects.

The unifying interpretation is that OA is the paradigmatic gene–environment respiratory disease: an environmental exposure is necessary but not sufficient, with HLA-class-II-centred genetic susceptibility determining which exposed workers progress to sensitization and disease. The two sensitizer branches (HMW complete allergen; LMW hapten–carrier neoantigen) converge on a shared HLA-II → Th2 → IgE/eosinophil effector axis, while the irritant branch bypasses immunity entirely via direct epithelial toxicity. The therapeutic and preventive corollary follows directly from the causal chain: because the initiating lesion is the exposure, removing the exposure is both the most effective treatment and the only true primary prevention, yet it generates a clinical–socioeconomic trade-off that dominates real-world management.

Concordance and tension. The evidence base is strongly concordant across human cohorts, systematic reviews, and animal/transgenic models. The principal internal tension is the clinical–socioeconomic trade-off (Finding 12): the intervention with the best biological outcome (complete removal) produces the worst livelihood outcome, which the literature resolves pragmatically rather than definitively. A second nuance is the agent-specific directionality of HLA alleles (DQB1*0501 protective for isocyanates yet predisposing for anhydrides), cautioning against treating "HLA susceptibility" as a single uniform risk factor.

Limitations and Knowledge Gaps

  1. No single causal gene / no Mendelian model. OA is polygenic; HLA and modifier-gene effects are modest, population-dependent, and not clinically actionable for prediction. Genetic testing has no current diagnostic role.
  2. Mechanistic detail is richest for diisocyanates. The hapten–carrier → HLA-II → Th2 pathway is best demonstrated for isocyanates; mechanisms for many of the 400+ agents are inferred by analogy rather than directly demonstrated.
  3. Irritant-induced asthma / RADS mechanisms (oxidative/neurogenic epithelial injury) are less molecularly resolved than the allergic pathway, and the boundary between acute RADS and "irritant-induced asthma with latency" is not sharply defined.
  4. Biologic therapy evidence in OA specifically is extrapolated from severe type-2 asthma trials; no large OA-specific randomized trials of type-2 biologics exist.
  5. Epigenetics, single-cell, and spatial transcriptomics of occupational airway tissue are largely unexplored — a genuine data gap.
  6. Prognostic prediction remains imprecise; no validated molecular biomarker predicts who will recover versus develop persistent disease after cessation.
  7. Health-equity dimension: occupational exposures and OA burden fall disproportionately on low-income and minority workers, and surveillance under-counts these risks (PMID: 39212906).

Proposed Follow-up Experiments / Actions

  1. Agent-stratified GWAS / HLA fine-mapping across multiple sensitizers (isocyanates, anhydrides, flour, enzymes, latex) in harmonized cohorts to resolve shared vs agent-specific susceptibility loci and formalize the HLA-DQB1 signal.
  2. Single-cell and spatial transcriptomics of bronchial biopsies pre/post specific inhalation challenge to map the dendritic → CD4⁺ Th2 → eosinophil axis in situ and identify remodeling drivers that predict persistence.
  3. Prospective biomarker validation combining FeNO, sputum eosinophils, periostin, and serum specific-IgE as a multivariate predictor of (a) SIC positivity and (b) post-cessation recovery, enabling risk-stratified removal decisions.
  4. Randomized or pragmatic trials of type-2 biologics specifically in severe sensitizer-induced OA, including workers unable to fully avoid exposure, to replace current extrapolation with direct evidence.
