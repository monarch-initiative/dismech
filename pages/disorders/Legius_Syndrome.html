Legius Syndrome

Genetic MONDO:0012669 Pathograph 7 Show in embeddings browser RASopathy

Legius syndrome (LGSS; formerly "neurofibromatosis type 1-like syndrome", NFLS) is an autosomal dominant RASopathy caused by heterozygous germline loss-of-function variants in SPRED1. SPRED1 encodes a Sprouty-related, EVH1 domain-containing negative regulator of RAS-MAPK signaling whose principal function is to recruit neurofibromin — the RAS-GTPase-activating protein encoded by NF1 — to activated RAS at the plasma membrane. Loss of SPRED1 therefore phenocopies the pigmentary arm of neurofibromatosis type 1: multiple bilateral café-au-lait macules with or without axillary/inguinal freckling, macrocephaly, lipomas, and a mild neurocognitive profile of learning disability, ADHD, and developmental delay. Critically, and unlike NF1, Legius syndrome is not associated with neurofibromas, Lisch nodules, symptomatic optic pathway gliomas, or the characteristic NF1 osseous lesions, which is why it is classified as a RASopathy rather than a form of neurofibromatosis. Because the two conditions are clinically indistinguishable in early childhood when only pigmentary findings are present, molecular confirmation of a SPRED1 variant is the defining diagnostic criterion in the 2021 international consensus revision.

Definitions

1
2021 international consensus diagnostic criteria for Legius syndrome
The International Consensus Group on Neurofibromatosis Diagnostic Criteria (I-NF-DC) established the first formal diagnostic criteria for Legius syndrome in 2021. Unlike the NF1 criteria, which can be met clinically, the Legius syndrome criteria require molecular confirmation: a heterozygous pathogenic SPRED1 variant at a ~50% variant allele fraction in apparently normal tissue, together with six or more bilaterally distributed café-au-lait macules and no NF1-related diagnostic criteria other than axillary or inguinal freckling. A child of an affected parent needs only one of these. Fewer than six café-au-lait macules does not exclude the diagnosis.

Discussions and Knowledge Gaps

2
Is the tumor risk in Legius syndrome truly at population baseline, or does the absence of tumors in published cohorts reflect limited sample size and incomplete adult follow-up?
KNOWLEDGE GAP OPEN gap_legius_residual_tumor_risk
Attached to
pathophysiology#RAS-MAPK Pathway Hyperactivation
The clinical management of Legius syndrome — developmental rather than oncologic surveillance — rests on the claim that SPRED1 loss does not confer the tumor predisposition of NF1. The strongest supporting observation is nonetheless a zero count in a modest cohort: 0 of 42 SPRED1-positive individuals, with a 95% confidence interval extending to 7%. A tumor risk anywhere below that upper bound is statistically compatible with the data, and the cohorts are weighted toward children, so adult-onset risk is especially poorly constrained. Because SPRED1 and NF1 converge on the same RAS-inactivation step, a mechanistic prior for some residual risk is not unreasonable, and resolving this determines whether any oncologic surveillance is warranted.
Proposed experiments
Genomic-ascertainment cohort of adult SPRED1 variant carriers

Identify carriers of pathogenic and likely pathogenic germline SPRED1 variants in large unselected biobank/health-system genomic datasets rather than in neurofibromatosis clinics, and compare age-stratified adult cancer incidence against non-carrier controls. Genomic rather than clinical ascertainment removes the referral bias that inflates the apparent specificity of the pigmentary phenotype, and biobank scale provides the person-years needed to tighten the upper confidence bound well below 7%.

The mechanistic account of the neurocognitive phenotype rests entirely on a mouse model, and two gaps separate it from the human disease. First is a dosage mismatch: the electrophysiology and behavioral deficits are reported in homozygous Spred1(-/-) animals, whereas humans with Legius syndrome are heterozygous. Second, no human study has demonstrated a corresponding hippocampal plasticity or ERK-signaling abnormality, so the inference from rodent CA1 physiology to human learning disability remains an extrapolation rather than a demonstrated mechanism. This matters therapeutically, because MEK-inhibitor-based cognitive rescue strategies proposed for RASopathies presuppose that the human deficit is ERK-driven in the same way.

Germline SPRED1 Loss of Function
Legius syndrome is caused by heterozygous germline loss-of-function variants in SPRED1, a member of the SPROUTY/SPRED family of proteins that act as negative regulators of the RAS-RAF interaction and downstream MAPK signaling. This was the first human disease attributed to the SPRY/SPRED gene family and placed the disorder within the RASopathy group of RAS-MAPK pathway disorders.

Impaired Neurofibromin Recruitment to Activated RAS
SPRED1 suppresses RAS-ERK signaling not by acting on RAS directly but by serving as a membrane-targeting adaptor for neurofibromin, the RAS-GAP encoded by NF1. The SPRED1 EVH1 domain binds short N- and C-terminal helical extensions flanking the GAP-related domain (GRD) of neurofibromin — regions dispensable for GAP catalysis and absent from p120GAP, which explains the selectivity of SPRED1 for neurofibromin among RasGAPs. Pathogenic EVH1-domain missense variants found in Legius syndrome reduce GRD binding affinity and correspondingly abolish the ERK-suppressing activity of SPRED1, providing a direct molecular link between genotype and pathway dysregulation. Structural work confirms that SPRED1-mediated membrane targeting allows neurofibromin to engage activated KRAS simultaneously.

RAS-MAPK Pathway Hyperactivation
Like the other RASopathies, Legius syndrome results from germline dysregulation of the RAS-MAPK (RAS/ERK) signal transduction cascade. The shared pathway lesion places the disorder in the same phenotypically overlapping family as neurofibromatosis type 1, Noonan syndrome, Costello syndrome, and cardiofaciocutaneous syndrome, and accounts for the macrocephaly, pigmentary, and neurocognitive features shared across the group. In Legius syndrome the dysregulation is comparatively mild and does not reach the threshold required for neurofibroma or other NF1 tumor formation.

Biallelic SPRED1 Inactivation in Melanocytes
Melanocytes isolated from a café-au-lait macule of an affected individual carried, in addition to the germline SPRED1 variant, an acquired somatic mutation in the wild-type SPRED1 allele. Café-au-lait macule formation in Legius syndrome therefore follows a two-hit, mosaic-somatic logic in the melanocyte lineage, mirroring the second-hit requirement described for NF1 pigmentary lesions, rather than arising from germline haploinsufficiency alone.

Cafe-au-lait Macule Formation
The cardinal and often sole objective finding of Legius syndrome: multiple, bilaterally distributed café-au-lait macules, with or without skinfold (axillary or inguinal) freckling, clinically indistinguishable from those of neurofibromatosis type 1. Because the pigmentary phenotype is shared, roughly half of individuals with a SPRED1 variant satisfy the older NIH clinical criteria for NF1 despite having a different natural history — the observation that drove the 2021 consensus revision to make a molecular SPRED1 result a required diagnostic criterion.

Hippocampal Synaptic Plasticity Impairment
Spred1-null mice show impaired performance in the Morris water maze and visual-discrimination T-maze with normal basic neuromotor and sensory abilities, together with defects in short- and long-term hippocampal synaptic plasticity including a disequilibrium between long-term potentiation and long-term depression in CA1. Increased ERK phosphorylation is measurable in Spred1-null slices after LTP induction, linking the plasticity defect to Ras/ERK hyperactivation. This is the candidate substrate for the learning disability, ADHD, and developmental delay reported in affected individuals, but the translational step from mouse hippocampal physiology to the human cognitive phenotype has not been directly demonstrated (see the HUMAN_MODEL_MISMATCH discussion).

Neurofibromatosis type 1 MONDO:0018975
Overlapping Features The principal differential and the reason the disorder was originally named "NF1-like syndrome". Read the other way round, 1.9% (95% CI 1.2%-2.9%) of individuals carrying a clinical NF1 diagnosis by the NIH criteria in fact have Legius syndrome — the diagnostic yield of SPRED1 testing in that ascertained population, which is not a population prevalence and is recorded here rather than in `prevalence:`.

notes: >-
  Naming: the disorder was described in 2007 as an "NF1-like syndrome" (NFLS).
  The eponym "Legius syndrome" was subsequently proposed and widely adopted
  because reference to NF1 in the name was thought to confuse families about
  prognosis. The 2021 international consensus group considered classifying it as
  an NF1 subtype and explicitly rejected this, reasoning that in the absence of
  neurofibromas it is inappropriate to consider it a type of neurofibromatosis;
  the group did, however, note that a more appropriate nomenclature for the
  neurofibromatosis family as a whole remains a future task.

  Relationship to NF1 at the mechanistic level: Legius syndrome and NF1 are not
  merely phenotypic look-alikes but converge on the same molecular step. SPRED1
  recruits neurofibromin to activated RAS at the plasma membrane; NF1 encodes
  neurofibromin itself. Losing either protein impairs GAP-mediated RAS
  inactivation. The clinical divergence — pigmentary-only versus tumor-prone —
  therefore reflects a quantitative and cell-type-dependent difference in the
  severity of RAS dysregulation rather than involvement of a different pathway.
