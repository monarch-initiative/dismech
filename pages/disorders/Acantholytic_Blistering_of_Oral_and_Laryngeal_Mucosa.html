Acantholytic Blistering of Oral and Laryngeal Mucosa

Mendelian MONDO:0030986 Pathograph 13 Show in embeddings browser Desmosomal Disease Inherited Mucosal Blistering Disorder

Acantholytic blistering of the oral and laryngeal mucosa (ABOLM; OMIM 619226) is a very rare autosomal recessive mucosal blistering disorder caused by biallelic loss-of-function variants in DSG3, which encodes the desmosomal cadherin desmoglein 3. It is the genetic counterpart of the mucosal form of pemphigus vulgaris: the protein that pemphigus autoantibodies block is instead absent, so the disease reproduces a pemphigus-like mucosal phenotype without evidence of autoimmunity. The restriction to mucosa follows from the desmoglein compensation principle: in squamous mucosa desmoglein 1 is expressed at much lower levels than desmoglein 3 and cannot replace it, whereas in epidermis desmoglein 1 maintains adhesion. Only a few families have been reported, carrying a homozygous nonsense variant, a homozygous deletion of the DSG3 terminal exon, and a homozygous missense variant.

Pathophysiology

4
Biallelic DSG3 Loss of Function
Biallelic DSG3 variants remove desmoglein 3 from the desmosomes of stratified squamous epithelia. Reported alleles are a homozygous nonsense variant, a homozygous 49.6 kb deletion at 18q12.1 that removes the last exon of DSG3 and also involves DSG2, and a homozygous missense variant. This is the structural-gene route of the desmosomal adhesion failure module: the protein that IgG autoantibodies block in pemphigus vulgaris is here genetically absent.

Insufficient Desmoglein 1 Compensation in Squamous Mucosa
Desmoglein 1 can substitute for desmoglein 3 where it is expressed at sufficient levels. In epidermis it is abundant, but in oral and other squamous mucosa it is expressed at much lower levels than desmoglein 3. The lack of compensation explains why loss of desmoglein 3 presents as a mucosal rather than a cutaneous disease. This node is inferred from pemphigus and mouse genetic studies; desmoglein 1 expression has not been measured in ABOLM patient tissue.

1
Genetic confirmation and exclusion of autoimmune pemphigus
The mucosal lesion resembles pemphigus vulgaris, so the diagnosis rests on finding biallelic DSG3 loss-of-function variants in a patient without evidence of autoimmunity. One reported allele is a homozygous 49.6 kb deletion at 18q12.1, a copy-number loss that sequence-only testing may not detect.

Acantholytic Blistering of Oral and Laryngeal Mucosa (ABOLM) — Comprehensive Disease Characterization Report

Target disease: Acantholytic Blistering of Oral and Laryngeal Mucosa (ABOLM) MONDO ID: MONDO:0030986 · Category: Mendelian (monogenic, autosomal recessive) Causal gene: DSG3 (desmoglein 3), 18q12.1, HGNC:3050, OMIM gene 605595

Summary

Acantholytic Blistering of Oral and Laryngeal Mucosa (ABOLM) is an ultra-rare, autosomal-recessive, non-autoimmune mucosal blistering disorder caused by biallelic loss-of-function of DSG3 (desmoglein 3). It is, in essence, the human genetic null equivalent of the autoimmune disease pemphigus vulgaris (PV): the target antigen that is attacked by autoantibodies in PV is simply absent from birth in ABOLM. The disorder has been reported in only two families worldwide — a consanguineous family with a homozygous DSG3 nonsense mutation (PMID: 30528827, 2019) and dizygotic twins with a homozygous 49.6-kb deletion of chromosome 18q12.1 removing the terminal exon of DSG3 (PMID: 37850634, 2024). As of this report, it is one of the rarest monogenic desmosomal diseases described.

The central mechanism is loss of desmoglein-3–mediated cell–cell adhesion in stratified squamous epithelia. DSG3 is a calcium-dependent desmosomal cadherin whose N-terminal (EC1) adhesive interface mediates trans-adhesion between adjacent keratinocytes. When DSG3 is absent, desmosomes in the deep (suprabasal/basal) layers of mucosal epithelium fail, producing suprabasal acantholysis — the same histology ("tombstone" basal cells) seen in pemphigus vulgaris. Critically, the disease is mucosa-dominant and largely skin-sparing, a pattern explained by the desmoglein compensation theory: in skin, DSG1 is broadly expressed and compensates for DSG3 loss, whereas in oral and laryngeal mucosa DSG1 expression is weak and cannot compensate, so DSG3 loss produces full mucosal blistering. The Dsg3-knockout mouse (and its coallelic spontaneous mutant "balding") faithfully recapitulates the oral acantholytic phenotype, runting from impaired feeding, and cyclical telogen hair loss, providing strong cross-species validation.

For a knowledge base, the actionable clinical message is diagnostic and therapeutic distinction from pemphigus. ABOLM shows PV-like histology but negative direct/indirect immunofluorescence and negative DSG1/DSG3 autoantibody ELISA; molecular confirmation is biallelic DSG3 loss-of-function. Because the disorder is non-autoimmune, the immunosuppressive therapies that anchor pemphigus management (corticosteroids, rituximab) are not indicated; care is supportive, focused on airway protection, nutrition, pain control, and avoidance of mucosal trauma. The 2024 twins presented predominantly with laryngeal/respiratory mucosal friability, underscoring airway involvement as a defining and clinically important feature.

Key Findings

Finding 1 — ABOLM is caused by biallelic loss-of-function of DSG3

ABOLM is an autosomal-recessive mucosal blistering disorder caused by complete loss of desmoglein 3 function from both DSG3 alleles. Two independent human reports establish causation across two distinct mutational mechanisms. The first family carried a homozygous nonsense mutation in DSG3 (PMID: 30528827, 2019). The second report described a homozygous 49.6-kb deletion of chromosome 18q12.1 removing the last exon of DSG3 in dizygotic twins (PMID: 37850634, 2024). The authors of the 2024 report state: "We report a novel homozygous 49.6 kb deletion of chromosome 18q12.1 involving the last exon of DSG3 in dizygotic twins with phenotype consistent with acantholytic blistering of the oral and laryngeal mucosa (ABOLM)," and note this is "only the second report in the literature of this unusual autosomal recessive blistering disorder." The phenotype is acantholytic blistering and erosions of the oral and laryngeal/respiratory mucosa without autoantibodies — "The diagnosis explains the mucosal phenotype of a pemphigus-like disorder without evidence of autoimmune dysfunction." Two convergent loss-of-function mechanisms (nonsense; terminal-exon deletion) producing the same phenotype strongly support DSG3 null as the definitive cause. Evidence type: human clinical/genetic.

Finding 2 — The Dsg3-null mouse (and coallelic "balding" mutant) recapitulates the disease

The DSG3-knockout mouse provides a faithful animal model and demonstrates the direct adhesive function of DSG3. The authors engineered mice with targeted disruption of DSG3 specifically "to test whether Dsg3 mediates adhesion" (PMID: 9166409). DSG3−/− mice are normal at birth but become runted around day 18 as oral lesions impair feeding. Oropharyngeal biopsies reproduce the human histology: "oropharyngeal biopsies showed erosions with histology typical of PV, including suprabasilar acantholysis and 'tombstoning' of basal cells. EM showed separation of desmosomes." The phenotype is identical to the spontaneous mouse mutant "balding" (bal): "The runting and hair loss phenotype of DSG3 −/− mice is identical to that of a previously reported mouse mutant, balding (bal)," establishing bal as a naturally occurring DSG3-null model. This cross-species concordance — same gene, same suprabasal acantholytic histology, same desmosomal ultrastructural separation — is a cornerstone of the causal argument. Evidence type: model organism (mouse).

Finding 3 — The desmoglein compensation theory explains mucosa-dominant, skin-sparing disease

Why does loss of a protein expressed throughout stratified epithelia produce disease predominantly in mucosa and hair while largely sparing skin? The desmoglein compensation theory (Stanley & Amagai) provides the mechanism. DSG1 and DSG3 have complementary tissue distributions. In oral mucosa, DSG3 is expressed throughout the epithelium and DSG1 is weak and limited, so DSG3 loss cannot be compensated and acantholysis results. In skin, DSG1 is expressed across all layers and compensates for DSG3 loss in the superficial layers, sparing most skin (acantholysis occurs only with added mechanical trauma). The theory was established through anti-desmoglein autoantibody profiling in pemphigus: "The desmoglein compensation theory posits that this rare phenotype can be produced by pathogenically weak anti-Dsg3 IgG in the presence of potent anti-Dsg1 IgG autoantibodies" (PMID: 15858475). It explains lesion localization directly: "the Dsg compensation theory proposed by Stanley and Amagai can reasonably explain the different depths of skin lesions and the different occurrences of skin and oral mucosal lesions between PV and PF" (PMID: 12677426). Applied to ABOLM, the complete genetic absence of DSG3 is phenotypically equivalent to the "mucosal-dominant" autoantibody profile of PV, but constitutively and from birth. Evidence type: human clinical + mechanistic inference.

Finding 4 — DSG3 adhesion operates through a calcium-dependent N-terminal (EC1) interface

The molecular basis of DSG3-mediated adhesion is a calcium-dependent, conformational, N-terminal trans-adhesive interface, and its disruption alone is sufficient to cause acantholysis. The pathogenic monoclonal antibody AK23 reproduces the full Dsg3−/−/PV phenotype by binding this interface: "AK23 recognized a calcium-dependent conformational epitope on Dsg3, which consisted of the V3, K7, P8, and D59 Dsg3-specific residues that formed the adhesive interface between juxtaposed Dsg, as predicted by the crystal structure" (PMID: 12574390). Antibodies binding middle or C-terminal extracellular-cadherin (EC) regions are non-pathogenic, localizing the critical adhesive function to the EC1 amino terminus. The same report concludes these data "suggest the direct inhibition of adhesive interaction of Dsg as an initial molecular event of blister formation in pemphigus." DSG3 is a type-1 transmembrane desmosomal cadherin with four extracellular cadherin domains (EC1–EC4) plus an EC-anchor domain, a single transmembrane segment, and a cytoplasmic tail that binds plakoglobin. In ABOLM, genetic ablation removes the entire protein — including this interface — so the proximal adhesive lesion is identical to (and more complete than) antibody-mediated interface blockade. Evidence type: in vitro / structural / model organism.

Finding 5 — DSG3 anchors telogen-phase hair; its loss causes cyclical acantholytic hair loss

Beyond mucosa, DSG3 has a specialized role anchoring resting-phase (telogen) hair in the follicle, explaining the hair phenotype of the null state. In DSG3−/− mice, hair grows normally through the first anagen (growth) cycle, but "when the hair follicles entered the resting phase of the hair growth cycle (telogen), mice with a targeted disruption of the desmoglein 3 gene (DSG3−/−) lost hair in a wave-like pattern from the head to the tail" (PMID: 9701552). Telogen (not anagen) hairs pull out in clumps. Histology shows cystic telogen follicles lacking shafts, and the mechanism is again acantholytic: the study "showed loss of cell adhesion (acantholysis) between the cells surrounding the telogen club and the basal layer of the outer root sheath epithelium," with "half-desmosomes" on EM. This generalizes the DSG3 adhesion defect from interfollicular mucosa to the follicular outer root sheath. (Human ABOLM reports emphasize the mucosal/airway phenotype; hair involvement in humans is less documented and may be subtler, but the mouse clarifies the full biological role of DSG3.) Evidence type: model organism (mouse).

Finding 6 — Diagnosis: suprabasal acantholysis + NEGATIVE immunofluorescence/serology, confirmed genetically; management is supportive

The diagnostic signature of ABOLM is PV-like histology combined with the absence of autoimmune markers, confirmed by DSG3 genetic testing. Biopsy shows suprabasilar acantholysis and "tombstone" basal cells, but — unlike pemphigus — there are no circulating or tissue-bound anti-desmoglein autoantibodies: direct immunofluorescence (DIF), indirect immunofluorescence (IIF), and DSG1/DSG3 ELISA are all negative. Molecular confirmation is biallelic DSG3 loss-of-function (homozygous nonsense, PMID: 30528827; homozygous 18q12.1 deletion detectable by chromosomal microarray/sequencing, PMID: 37850634). This distinction is not academic: "The exclusion of an autoimmune basis has management implications" (PMID: 37850634) — meaning immunosuppressants and rituximab used for pemphigus are not indicated, and care is supportive. Airway management is central because "The twin siblings presented predominantly with friability of the laryngeal and respiratory mucosa." Evidence type: human clinical.

It is essentially a genetic phenocopy of pemphigus vulgaris lacking the autoimmune component.

Epidemiology: ultra-rare — only two families reported worldwide; prevalence/incidence not estimable (far below 1/1,000,000). Inheritance: autosomal recessive ("only the second report in the literature of this unusual autosomal recessive blistering disorder," PMID: 37850634). 10. Diagnostics

Histopathology (biopsy): suprabasal/suprabasilar acantholysis with "tombstone" basal cells — indistinguishable from pemphigus vulgaris on H&E. Immunofluorescence/serology — the decisive discriminator: DIF and IIF negative; DSG1/DSG3 autoantibody ELISA negative (no autoantibodies). Electron microscopy: desmosome separation / "half-desmosomes." Genetic testing (confirmatory): biallelic DSG3 loss-of-function. Recommended approach: single-gene DSG3 sequencing or a blistering/epidermolysis gene panel; chromosomal microarray (CMA) or CNV-aware sequencing is required to detect structural deletions such as the 49.6-kb 18q12.1 deletion (PMID: 37850634). WES/WGS are useful when panels are negative or to detect CNVs. Differential diagnosis: pemphigus vulgaris (autoantibody-positive), paraneoplastic pemphigus, epidermolysis bullosa, Hailey-Hailey/Darier disease, and other acantholytic dermatoses — all distinguished by immunofluorescence status, autoantibody profile, and DSG3 genetics. Screening: cascade/carrier testing within affected families; prenatal/preimplantation testing possible once the familial variant is known.

11. Outcome / Prognosis

Survival/mortality: no formal survival data; the chief life-threatening risk is airway compromise from laryngeal/respiratory mucosal friability, plus nutritional failure from oral disease (mouse runting is a direct analog). Morbidity: chronic oral pain, dysphagia/odynophagia, feeding difficulty, hoarseness, and recurrent mucosal erosions dominate. Disease course: chronic/lifelong with trauma-provoked flares. Recovery potential: the underlying genetic defect is not reversible with current therapy; supportive care can substantially mitigate morbidity. Prognostic factors: extent of laryngeal/respiratory involvement (airway risk) and feeding status are the key determinants of outcome. Prognostic biomarkers: none established beyond genotype.

Mechanistic Model / Interpretation

ABOLM should be understood as "pemphigus vulgaris minus the autoantibody." Pemphigus and ABOLM converge on an identical effector lesion — suprabasal acantholysis from DSG3 adhesive failure — but arrive there from opposite directions:

PEMPHIGUS VULGARIS (acquired, autoimmune)
  Loss of immune tolerance → anti-DSG3 (±anti-DSG1) IgG
     → antibody binds EC1 interface / steric + signaling (RhoA↓, tension loss)
→ desmosome disassembly → suprabasal acantholysis

ABOLM (congenital, genetic)
  Biallelic DSG3-null genotype → no DSG3 protein at all
     → EC1 adhesive interface never forms
→ desmosome adhesion absent → suprabasal acantholysis

Both are then filtered by the same tissue rule — the desmoglein compensation theory. Wherever DSG1 can substitute (most skin), the lesion is masked; wherever it cannot (oral and laryngeal mucosa; telogen follicle), the lesion is expressed. This is why an autoimmune disease and a recessive genetic disease share the same target-tissue signature. The genetic disease is, in a sense, the cleanest possible proof of the compensation model: it removes the confound of variable autoantibody titers and shows that absence of DSG3 alone, interpreted through tissue-specific DSG1 availability, predicts exactly where blisters form.

The practical corollary — and the single most important clinical takeaway — is therapeutic: the two diseases look identical on H&E but require opposite management. Pemphigus demands immunosuppression; ABOLM would be harmed by it (immunosuppression cannot replace a missing adhesion protein and exposes an infant to needless risk). The diagnostic fork is immunofluorescence/serology (negative in ABOLM) plus DSG3 genetics.
