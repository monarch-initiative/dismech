C1s Deficiency

Mendelian MONDO:0013419 Pathograph 33 Show in embeddings browser Complement Disorder

Hereditary C1s deficiency is a very rare autosomal recessive inborn error of immunity caused by biallelic loss-of-function variants in C1S, which encodes C1s, the serine protease of the C1 complex that starts the classical complement pathway. Activated C1s cleaves C4 and C2 to build the classical pathway C3 convertase; without it, classical pathway haemolytic activity (CH50) is absent while the alternative and lectin pathways are normal. Unlike sporadic lupus, C4, C2 and C1-inhibitor are normal or raised because they are no longer consumed, and C1r is often secondarily low. About a dozen genetically or biochemically confirmed patients have been reported. Their presentations are lupus or a lupus-like illness, often starting in childhood, with malar and chilblain-type skin lesions, arthritis, antinuclear and anti-Ro antibodies with few anti-dsDNA antibodies, and proliferative lupus nephritis; severe recurrent bacterial infections including pneumococcal and meningococcal meningitis; and other autoimmune disease (Hashimoto thyroiditis, autoimmune hepatitis). Single families add hemophagocytic syndrome with fever, seizures and coma, and Kikuchi-Fujimoto disease. Penetrance is incomplete: homozygous siblings can remain well. The mechanism proposed for the autoimmunity, shared with the other early classical pathway deficiencies, is defective complement-dependent clearance of apoptotic cells and immune complexes, with a C1s-specific contribution from loss of C1s cleavage of apoptotic-cell autoantigens shown in vitro. This entry is named for the deficiency and bound to the MONDO deficiency class (OMIM 613783); MONDO's separate class "systemic lupus erythematosus related to C1S" names the lupus manifestation of the same genotype and is mapped below. The entry covers C1S-caused deficiency only. Families with combined C1r and C1s deficiency in which C1r is the primary defect belong to C1r deficiency, and heterozygous C1S gain-of-function variants cause a different disease, periodontal Ehlers-Danlos syndrome.

Defective Clearance of Apoptotic Cells and Immune Complexes
The proposed link between early classical pathway deficiency and lupus: complement normally tags apoptotic cells and immune complexes for removal by phagocytes, and when this fails, nuclear antigens exposed on apoptotic blebs persist and are presented to the adaptive immune system. The model rests mainly on C1q deficiency and has not been measured directly in C1s deficient patients.

Loss of C1s Cleavage of Apoptotic-Cell Autoantigens
In vitro, C1s (alone or within C1) cleaves intracellular proteins exposed during apoptosis, including the alarmin HMGB1, and the cleaved HMGB1 is less able to amplify cytokine production by monocytes, macrophages and dendritic cells. Loss of this activity is a C1s-specific candidate mechanism for the lupus association that does not depend on C4 and C2 cleavage. It has not been tested in C1s deficient patients.

description: >-
  Hereditary C1s deficiency is a very rare autosomal recessive inborn error of
  immunity caused by biallelic loss-of-function variants in C1S, which encodes
  C1s, the serine protease of the C1 complex that starts the classical
  complement pathway. Activated C1s cleaves C4 and C2 to build the classical
  pathway C3 convertase; without it, classical pathway haemolytic activity
  (CH50) is absent while the alternative and lectin pathways are normal. Unlike
  sporadic lupus, C4, C2 and C1-inhibitor are normal or raised because they are
  no longer consumed, and C1r is often secondarily low. About a dozen
  genetically or biochemically confirmed patients have been reported. Their
  presentations are lupus or a lupus-like illness, often starting in childhood,
  with malar and chilblain-type skin lesions, arthritis, antinuclear and
  anti-Ro antibodies with few anti-dsDNA antibodies, and proliferative lupus
  nephritis; severe recurrent bacterial infections including pneumococcal and
  meningococcal meningitis; and other autoimmune disease (Hashimoto
  thyroiditis, autoimmune hepatitis). Single families add hemophagocytic
  syndrome with fever, seizures and coma, and Kikuchi-Fujimoto disease.
  Penetrance is incomplete: homozygous siblings can remain well. The
  mechanism proposed for the autoimmunity, shared with the other early
  classical pathway deficiencies, is defective complement-dependent clearance
  of apoptotic cells and immune complexes, with a C1s-specific contribution
  from loss of C1s cleavage of apoptotic-cell autoantigens shown in vitro.

  class (OMIM 613783); MONDO's separate class "systemic lupus erythematosus
  related to C1S" names the lupus manifestation of the same genotype and is
  mapped below. The entry covers C1S-caused deficiency only. Families with
  combined C1r and C1s deficiency in which C1r is the primary defect belong
  to C1r deficiency, and heterozygous C1S gain-of-function variants cause a
  different disease, periodontal Ehlers-Danlos syndrome.

C1s Deficiency (MONDO:1060176): A Comprehensive Disease Characteristics Report

Causal gene: C1S (complement C1s subcomponent; HGNC:1247; NCBI Gene 716; UniProt P09871; chromosome 12p13.31) Disease category: Mendelian, autosomal recessive inborn error of immunity / classical-complement-pathway deficiency

Summary

C1s deficiency is a rare autosomal-recessive inborn error of immunity caused by biallelic loss-of-function (truncating) variants in the C1S gene on chromosome 12p13.31. C1S encodes the catalytic serine protease of the first complement complex (C1). When C1s is absent, the C1 complex is enzymatically dead: it can no longer cleave complement components C4 and C2, so the entire classical complement pathway fails to propagate. The biochemical hallmark is an immeasurably low total hemolytic complement activity (CH50) with a normal alternative-pathway activity, normal C3, and normal/elevated C4 and C2. Only about 9–10 cases of complete C1s deficiency have been reported worldwide, making it one of the rarest of the inherited complement deficiencies.

Clinically, the disease produces a characteristic two-branch phenotype. First, defective opsonization predisposes to recurrent, invasive pyogenic infections with encapsulated bacteria, especially Streptococcus pneumoniae and Neisseria meningitidis. Second, failure of complement-dependent clearance of apoptotic cells and immune complexes — compounded by loss of a complement-independent C1s function (proteolytic inactivation of the nuclear alarmin HMGB1) — drives systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) or an SLE-like syndrome, including proliferative lupus nephritis. The autoimmune branch shows incomplete penetrance and variable expressivity: in one consanguineous family, all four C1s-deficient siblings shared the same genotype but only two developed SLE, indicating that additional predisposing/modifier genes are required.

Management is supportive and preventive: vaccination against encapsulated organisms (pneumococcus, meningococcus, Haemophilus influenzae type b), antibiotic prophylaxis, and standard immunosuppression for lupus manifestations (hydroxychloroquine, corticosteroids, and — for refractory proliferative lupus nephritis — rituximab). A crucial allelic distinction must be made: heterozygous missense (gain-of-function-like) variants in C1S (and its partner C1R) cause a mechanistically opposite dominant disorder, periodontal Ehlers-Danlos syndrome (EDSPD2/EDSPD1), not complement deficiency. Confusing these two allelic disorders is the principal diagnostic pitfall for this gene.

Key Findings

Finding 1 — C1s deficiency is a rare autosomal-recessive classical-complement-pathway defect caused by loss-of-function C1S variants

Complete C1s deficiency is caused by biallelic truncating / loss-of-function variants in the C1S gene (HGNC:1247; chromosome 12p13.31), which encodes the serine protease C1s (UniProt P09871). Reported molecular lesions span frameshift, nonsense, and compound-truncating classes:

  • A homozygous 4-bp deletion (TTTG, nucleotides 1087–1090) in exon 10 creating a premature stop codon. As reported, "Sequence analysis of C1s cDNA revealed a deletion at nucleotides 1087-1090 (TTTG), creating a stop codon (TGA) at position 94 downstream of the mutation site" (PMID: 9856483).
  • A homozygous nonsense/premature-stop mutation at position 938 in exon 6, segregating in a consanguineous family with four affected siblings — "all four siblings are homozygous for a mutation at position 938 in exon 6 of the C1s cDNA that creates a premature stop codon" (PMID: 18062908).
  • Compound truncating variants identified by whole-genome sequencing in a male Caucasian patient — "The genome of the patient was analyzed by whole genome sequencing showing two truncating variants" (PMID: 38469558).

The biochemical signature across cases is consistent: an isolated early classical-pathway defect with immeasurably low CH50, normal alternative-pathway function, undetectable C1s, normal C3, and normal/elevated C4 and C2. Fewer than ~10 complete C1s-deficient cases are reported worldwide.

Variant classification: The reported truncating variants are pathogenic under ACMG/AMP criteria (PVS1 loss-of-function in a gene where LOF is the disease mechanism, plus segregation). Allele frequencies in population databases (gnomAD) are correspondingly very low/absent for these private variants. Origin is germline.

Finding 2 — Presentation: early-onset SLE / SLE-like disease (including lupus nephritis) plus recurrent pyogenic infections

Across the small case literature, complete C1s deficiency is strongly associated with SLE or SLE-like syndromes and severe pyogenic infections. The best-characterized modern case is a 34-year-old man with compound truncating C1S variants who had recurrent infections since childhood (pneumococcal and meningococcal meningitis, erysipelas, subcutaneous abscess, recurrent upper-airway infections) plus adult-onset SLE and proliferative lupus nephritis: "The patient also exhibited adult-onset SLE, meeting 7/11 of the ACR criteria and 34 of the 2019 EULAR/ACR classification criteria, along with class IV-G (A) proliferative lupus nephritis (LN)" (PMID: 38469558). The renal biopsy showed C1q positivity along glomerular basement membranes.

The autoimmune branch is incompletely penetrant: in the consanguineous family, "all four children are deficient in C1s but only two of them developed SLE" (PMID: 18062908), demonstrating variable expressivity. The infection branch reflects impaired opsonophagocytosis: deficiencies of early classical-pathway components "predispose to severe infections, particularly with encapsulated bacteria such as Neisseria meningitidis and Streptococcus pneumoniae" (PMID: 41421099).

Finding 3 — Mechanism: beyond C4/C2 cleavage, C1s inactivates nuclear alarmins (HMGB1), linking its loss to lupus

The archetypal role of C1s is as the catalytic arm of the C1 complex, cleaving C4 and C2 to propagate the classical pathway. Beyond this, C1s has a complement-independent proteolytic function: purified C1s and the physiological C1 complex cleave the nuclear alarmin HMGB1 into defined 19- and 12-kDa fragments during apoptosis, diminishing HMGB1's ability to enhance LPS-mediated pro-inflammatory cytokine production — "C1s, both in its purified and physiological form as C1 complex, cleaving HMGB1 into defined fragments of 19 and 12 kDa" (PMID: 27648302). Because HMGB1 can induce antinuclear autoantibodies and lupus-like disease in mice, loss of C1s removes a brake on alarmin-driven autoimmunity — an additional mechanistic link between C1s deficiency and SLE.

That C1s is the specific missing catalytic component was confirmed functionally: in a C4d-deposition assay, "the patient's absent complement activity was fully restored by adding C1s" (PMID: 38469558).

Importantly, C1r/C1s loss is necessary but not sufficient for lupus. A congenic mouse lacking C1r/C1s (with low C1q) did not spontaneously develop SLE: "These results suggest that C1r/C1s deficiency is not sufficient to drive murine SLE and also that other predisposing genes exist in ICGN mice" (PMID: 29550838). This directly explains the incomplete penetrance seen in human families.

Finding 4 — Classical-pathway deficiencies impair waste-disposal (apoptotic-cell clearance), with a severity hierarchy explaining the SLE link

Inherited classical-pathway deficiencies are among the strongest known single-gene susceptibility factors for SLE, following a well-established hierarchy: risk is highest for C1q deficiency (>90% develop SLE), high for C1r/C1s and C4 deficiency, and lower for C2 deficiency — "This hierarchy of disease associations suggests the importance of an intact early classic activation pathway in preventing systemic lupus erythematosus" (PMID: 8907066).

Functionally, the classical pathway predominates in opsonizing apoptotic and secondary-necrotic cells: "the components of the complement classical pathway, including C1q, were equally important in clearance of apoptotic cells" (PMID: 19302245). Failure of this "waste-disposal" function allows accumulation of nuclear autoantigens, driving autoantibody formation. Clinically, early complement/immunodeficiency in SLE associates with worse organ damage — in juvenile SLE, "the median of Systemic Lupus International Collaborating Clinics/ACR-damage index (SLICC/ACR-DI) was significantly higher in JSLE patients with PID compared with patients without these abnormalities (p = 0.0033)" (PMID: 21813590). C1s deficiency sits within this hierarchy as a proximal classical-pathway defect.

Finding 5 — Allelic distinction: heterozygous C1S/C1R missense variants cause periodontal Ehlers-Danlos syndrome, NOT complement deficiency

The same gene harbors a mechanistically opposite class of variants. Periodontal Ehlers-Danlos syndrome (pEDS; EDSPD2 for C1S, EDSPD1 for C1R) is an autosomal-dominant disorder caused by heterozygous missense (and in-frame indel) variants — "Periodontal Ehlers-Danlos syndrome (pEDS) is a rare condition caused by pathogenic variants in the C1R and C1S genes, encoding subunits C1r and C1s of the first component of the classical complement pathway" (PMID: 33890303). As of 2022, "148 cases have been described in the literature" (PMID: 36348983).

pEDS is characterized by early-onset periodontitis with premature tooth loss, lack of attached gingiva, thin/fragile gums, easy bruising, pretibial hyperpigmentation/plaques, distal joint hypermobility, hoarse voice, and occasional vascular complications. Crucially, these variants are not loss-of-function: activated/dysregulated C1s acquires aberrant activity — "Degradation of collagen I by activated C1s in periodontal Ehlers-Danlos Syndrome" (PMID: 36960056). This stands in direct contrast to complete C1s deficiency (biallelic truncating LOF → SLE + infections), and the two must never be conflated.

Finding 6 — Diagnosis and management: CH50 functional screen + sequencing; prophylaxis/vaccination + lupus immunosuppression

Classical-pathway defects "result from deficiencies of complement proteins C1q, C1r, C1s, C2, and C4, and typically manifest with features of systemic lupus erythematosus and infections with encapsulated organisms" (PMID: 39294906). The specific laboratory signature of C1s deficiency is an absent/immeasurably low CH50 with a normal alternative-pathway (AH50) activity — "A screening of the complement cascade showed immeasurably low CH50, while the alternative pathway (AP) function was normal" (PMID: 38469558). Component quantitation then shows undetectable C1s with normal C3 and normal/elevated C4 and C2 (corroborated in the C1r/C1s case of PMID: 7939409, which noted "persistently depressed levels of haemolytic complement activity (CH50)... associated with normal levels of C3, C4 and C2 components, and normal alternative pathway haemolytic activity").

Definitive diagnosis uses genetic sequencing (single-gene C1S, complement gene panels, WES, or WGS); the index modern patient was diagnosed by whole-genome sequencing (PMID: 38469558). Management emphasizes anticipatory care — "the importance of anticipatory care, vaccination, and prophylaxis as new complement-targeted drugs continue to emerge" (PMID: 41421099) — i.e., vaccination against pneumococcus, meningococcus, and Hib plus antibiotic prophylaxis. Lupus manifestations are treated with standard immunosuppression; refractory/proliferative lupus nephritis in complete C1s deficiency responded to rituximab (anti-CD20) (PMID: 20191570).

Suggested NCIT terms: Rituximab (NCIT:C1702), Hydroxychloroquine (NCIT:C509), Corticosteroid therapy, Pneumococcal/Meningococcal vaccination, Antibiotic prophylaxis.

Finding 7 — C1s is the catalytic serine protease of the ~790-kDa C1 complex (C1q·C1r₂·C1s₂)

Complement component C1 is a ~790-kDa assembly of the recognition subcomponent C1q (a hexamer of heterotrimers, 18 chains) bound to a tetramer of the modular proteases C1r and C1s (C1r₂s₂) — "Complement component C1... is a 790-kDa assembly formed from the target-recognition subcomponent C1q and the modular proteases C1r and C1s" (PMID: 23922389). C1q/C1s binding is Ca²⁺-dependent. C1s (domain architecture CUB-EGF-CUB-CCP-CCP-serine protease; peptidase clan PA, family S1; EC 3.4.21.42) is the catalytic arm: "Enzymatically active C1s provides the catalytic basis for cleavage of the downstream CP components, C4 and C2" (PMID: 38149922). Its loss renders the entire C1 complex enzymatically dead. Notably, C1s is druggable — an FDA-approved anti-C1s mAb and small-molecule inhibitors block the classical pathway — relevant to C1s-overactivity diseases, the opposite of the deficiency state.

Section-by-Section Coverage

1. Disease Information

  • Overview: A rare autosomal-recessive deficiency of complement C1s, the catalytic protease of the C1 complex, abolishing classical-pathway activation and producing SLE-like autoimmunity plus susceptibility to encapsulated-bacterial infection.
  • Identifiers: MONDO:1060176; causal gene C1S (HGNC:1247; NCBI Gene 716; UniProt P09871; 12p13.31). OMIM phenotype not formally recorded for the deficiency (gene C1S OMIM 120580). MeSH: Hereditary Complement Deficiency Diseases. Orphanet category: hereditary complement deficiency. ICD-10: D84.1 (defects in the complement system).
  • Synonyms: complement component 1s deficiency; hereditary C1s deficiency; SLE related to C1S.
  • Data source: Disease-level aggregated resources plus individual case reports (not large EHR cohorts), reflecting extreme rarity.

2. Etiology

Primary cause is monogenic (biallelic LOF C1S). Genetic risk = the causal variants themselves; consanguinity is a strong contributor (homozygous cases in consanguineous families, PMID: 18062908). Modifier/predisposing genes are required for the lupus branch (PMID: 29550838). No established protective alleles or environmental protective factors; UV light and infection act as general lupus/flare triggers (environmental, inferred by analogy to SLE). Gene-environment interaction: a "two-hit" model in which the complement defect (hit 1) plus modifier genes/triggers (hit 2) determine whether SLE manifests.

3. Phenotypes

Two phenotypic domains: (a) laboratory abnormality — absent CH50, undetectable C1s (HP:0005356, HP:0003261); (b) clinical disease — recurrent bacterial infections (HP:0002718; childhood onset, episodic, potentially severe/fatal), and SLE/lupus nephritis (HP:0002725, HP:0001970; adult-onset, variable severity, chronic-relapsing, incomplete penetrance). Quality-of-life impact is driven by recurrent infections and by lupus organ damage; formal QoL instruments have not been applied in this ultra-rare population.

4. Genetic/Molecular Information

Causal gene C1S; variant types reported = frameshift deletion, nonsense, and compound truncating (all LOF). ClinVar/ACMG: pathogenic. gnomAD allele frequencies: very rare/absent (private variants). Germline origin. Functional consequence: complete loss of function. Modifier genes: implicated but unidentified in humans; demonstrated to exist in the ICGN mouse. No epigenetic or chromosomal-abnormality mechanism reported. The index case's genetic analysis also highlighted alternative splicing of the normal C1s gene (PMID: 18062908).

5. Environmental Information

No specific toxin/occupational cause. Infectious agents are downstream consequences rather than causes — encapsulated bacteria (S. pneumoniae, N. meningitidis, H. influenzae type b). Lifestyle factors are not established disease determinants.

6. Mechanism / Pathophysiology

Presented as the ordered causal chain above. Molecular pathway: complement classical pathway (Reactome/KEGG). Cellular processes: opsonophagocytosis, apoptotic-cell clearance, inflammation. Protein dysfunction: loss of function (no catalytic C1s). Immune involvement: combined immunodeficiency (opsonization failure) + autoimmunity (SLE). Tissue damage: immune-complex-mediated glomerulonephritis (lupus nephritis). Biochemical defect: absent serine-protease activity for C4/C2 cleavage.

7. Anatomical Structures Affected

  • Organ level: kidney (lupus nephritis, UBERON:0002113 / glomerulus UBERON:0000074), skin (cutaneous lupus, erysipelas; UBERON:0002097), meninges/CNS (meningitis; UBERON:0002360), respiratory tract (recurrent infections). Body systems: immune, renal, integumentary, nervous.
  • Tissue/cell level: phagocytes/macrophages (CL:0000235); serum/plasma complement.
  • Subcellular: C1s is a secreted extracellular protein (GO:0005615/GO:0005576).
  • Localization: systemic/bilateral (immune-complex disease affects paired organs symmetrically).

8. Temporal Development

Infections typically begin in childhood; SLE often adult-onset (index case 34 y). Course is chronic/lifelong with episodic infective and lupus flares. Penetrance of autoimmunity is incomplete and age-dependent. Treatment-induced remission of lupus nephritis achievable (rituximab).

9. Inheritance and Population

Autosomal recessive; carrier frequency unknown (ultra-rare, private variants, largely absent from gnomAD). ~9–10 reported complete cases worldwide; prevalence/incidence never formally established. Consanguinity prominent. No defined ethnic predilection beyond consanguineous pedigrees. Penetrance incomplete (2/4 siblings SLE); variable expressivity. No anticipation (not a repeat disorder). Sex ratio not established given small numbers.

10. Diagnostics

CH50 absent + AH50 normal → quantify C1s (undetectable), C3 normal, C4/C2 normal-to-elevated; confirm by sequencing (single-gene C1S / complement panel / WES / WGS). Biomarkers: anti-nuclear, anti-dsDNA antibodies (in SLE cases). Biopsy: lupus-nephritis histology with C1q deposition. Differential diagnosis: other early-pathway deficiencies (C1q, C1r, C4, C2), each distinguished by component assays, and idiopathic SLE. Screening: cascade testing of relatives via CH50 + sequencing.

11. Outcome/Prognosis

Driven by infection severity and lupus organ damage; invasive infections (meningitis, sepsis) are potentially fatal (a historical C1r/C1s case died of septic shock, PMID: 7939409). Prophylaxis and vaccination substantially mitigate infectious mortality. Lupus nephritis can respond to immunosuppression/rituximab. Early complement PID associates with greater cumulative organ damage in SLE (PMID: 21813590). No formal survival statistics exist.

12. Treatment

  • Pharmacotherapy / immunosuppression: hydroxychloroquine, corticosteroids, and conventional immunosuppressants for lupus; rituximab (anti-CD20) effective for refractory proliferative lupus nephritis (PMID: 20191570).
  • Anti-infective: antibiotic prophylaxis; prompt treatment of infections.
  • Advanced/experimental: No gene therapy; plasma/C1s replacement is conceptual. (Anti-C1s drugs target overactivity, not deficiency.)
  • NCIT terms: Rituximab (NCIT:C1702), Hydroxychloroquine (NCIT:C509).

13. Prevention

  • Primary: vaccination against encapsulated bacteria (pneumococcal, meningococcal, Hib) and antibiotic prophylaxis (PMID: 41421099).
  • Secondary: cascade CH50/genetic screening of relatives; early detection of lupus.
  • Tertiary: immunosuppression to prevent lupus-nephritis progression; infection surveillance.
  • Counseling: genetic counseling for AR recurrence risk (25% per pregnancy for carrier couples).

14. Other Species / Natural Disease

C1s orthologs exist across mammals (mouse C1s1/C1s2; NCBI Gene for mouse C1s1 = 50909). No prominent naturally occurring companion-animal C1s-deficiency disease is catalogued (OMIA), though complement biology is evolutionarily conserved. Disease mechanisms (classical pathway) are broadly conserved in mammals.

15. Model Organisms

The most informative model is the ICGN congenic mouse lacking C1r/C1s (with low C1q), which does not spontaneously develop SLE — recapitulating the complement defect but not the autoimmune phenotype in isolation, confirming the requirement for additional predisposing genes (PMID: 29550838). Phenotype recapitulation: complement-deficiency phenotype reproduced; lupus phenotype NOT reproduced without modifiers — this is the model's key limitation and simultaneously its key scientific insight. In vitro systems (purified C1s, C1 complex, patient sera with C1s add-back) model the catalytic defect precisely.

Limitations and Knowledge Gaps

  1. Extreme rarity (~9–10 cases): All clinical inferences rest on case reports and small pedigrees; no cohort, prevalence, incidence, survival, or quality-of-life data exist. Penetrance and expressivity estimates are qualitative.
  2. No OMIM phenotype MIM formally recorded for the deficiency; nosology partly inferred from the broader complement-deficiency literature.
  3. HMGB1-inactivation mechanism is biochemically demonstrated but its quantitative contribution to human lupus relative to apoptotic-cell-clearance failure is unquantified.
  4. Modifier genes that convert C1s deficiency into overt SLE in humans are unidentified.
  5. No C1s-specific knockout mouse study of the full disease; existing model (ICGN) is combined C1r/C1s with low C1q.
  6. Treatment evidence (rituximab) derives from single cases; no controlled data.
  7. Allelic confusion risk: Literature searches for "C1S" readily return the far more numerous pEDS cases (~148), which must be excluded when curating the deficiency entry.
