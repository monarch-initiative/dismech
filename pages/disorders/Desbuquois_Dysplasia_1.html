Desbuquois Dysplasia 1

Mendelian MONDO:0009629 Pathograph 30 Show in embeddings browser osteochondrodysplasia dysplasia with multiple joint dislocations

Desbuquois dysplasia 1 (DBQD1) is an autosomal recessive osteochondrodysplasia of the multiple-dislocation group, caused by biallelic loss-of-function variants in CANT1, which encodes an ER/Golgi calcium-activated nucleotidase that hydrolyses UDP. Loss of this activity impairs glycosaminoglycan chain synthesis on cartilage proteoglycans, derailing the growth plate and producing severe prenatal-onset short stature with short limbs, generalized joint laxity with multiple dislocations, a "monkey-wrench" proximal femur, advanced carpal ossification, and characteristic hand anomalies.

Desbuquois dysplasia, Kim variant (DDKV)
A CANT1-related presentation with almost normal hands apart from short metacarpals and elongated phalanges (no accessory metacarpal ossification center), rather than the type-1 hand anomalies. Originally reported in Japanese and Korean patients carrying the c.676G>A (p.Val226Met) founder allele, but subsequently found in other populations. Mechanistic Hypotheses

1
Canonical CANT1 Loss / Impaired GAG Synthesis / Growth-Plate Failure Model
canonical_cant1_gag_synthesis_growth_plate_model CANONICAL
Evidence balance 1 support
Biallelic loss-of-function CANT1 variants abolish the ER/Golgi calcium-activated nucleotidase that hydrolyses UDP to UMP and phosphate. The inferred proximal consequence is that undischarged UDP accumulates in the Golgi lumen, product-inhibiting the glycosyltransferases that elongate glycosaminoglycan chains and reducing the UMP available for exchange of cytosolic UDP-sugars through the nucleotide-sugar antiporter. The measured downstream consequence is impaired glycosaminoglycan synthesis on cartilage proteoglycans (aggrecan, decorin), with shorter GAG chains, increased sulfation and delayed proteoglycan secretion. The depleted, abnormal growth-plate matrix deregulates chondrocyte proliferation and impairs terminal hypertrophic differentiation, so endochondral ossification fails and the skeleton is short with the DBQD1 signature. Patient fibroblasts and two independent Cant1 knock-out mouse lines reproduce the GAG-synthesis defect and skeletal phenotype, corroborating the axis.
The upstream enzymology (UDP hydrolysis, loss of activity/dimerization for missense alleles) and the downstream GAG-synthesis defect are both experimentally established; the intervening step linking a Golgi nucleotidase to a glycosyltransferase product-inhibition/antiporter defect is inferred from enzyme function and substrate preference and has not been measured directly in patient chondrocytes. The extension of the same proteoglycan defect to joint laxity (via tendon decorin) and to aortopathy is a reasonable but still inferential branch, recorded in the discussions.

Discussions and Knowledge Gaps

5
How exactly does loss of a Golgi UDP-hydrolysing nucleotidase cause a glycosaminoglycan-synthesis defect in DBQD1 chondrocytes?
KNOWLEDGE GAP OPEN cant1_udp_to_proteoglycan_mechanism
Attached to
pathophysiology#Impaired Golgi UDP Hydrolysis pathophysiology#Impaired Glycosaminoglycan Chain Synthesis mechanistic_hypotheses#canonical_cant1_gag_synthesis_growth_plate_model
The upstream enzymology (CANT1 hydrolyses UDP to UMP and phosphate) and the downstream readout (reduced GAG synthesis, shorter chains) are both established, but the step linking them is inferred rather than measured. The proposed logic is that undischarged UDP product-inhibits the glycosyltransferases that elongate GAG chains and that reduced UMP starves the nucleotide-sugar antiporter that imports cytosolic UDP-sugar donors. This has not been demonstrated by measuring Golgi UDP, UDP-sugar pools, or glycosyltransferase activity in patient or knock-out chondrocytes, and the enzyme's exact subcellular site of action (ER versus Golgi) is itself still debated. Settling it would convert the central node of the canonical model from inference to measurement and identify where a therapeutic bypass could act.

Do the Cant1 mouse models and the inferred tendon/cardiovascular branches faithfully represent the human DBQD1 mechanism, given the unexplained ER dilation and the lack of human-tissue confirmation for joint laxity and aortopathy?
HUMAN MODEL MISMATCH OPEN cant1_mouse_fidelity_and_inferred_branches

Two independent Cant1 knock-out mouse lines reproduce the skeletal and GAG-synthesis phenotype well, which is strong support for the core axis. But two translational gaps remain. First, knock-out chondrocytes show dilated ER cisternae yet no canonical ER stress/unfolded-protein response, so the cellular-stress step is observed in mouse without a known mechanism. Second, the joint-laxity branch is inferred from mouse tendon decorin data and the aortopathy branch from a human echocardiographic cohort, neither of which directly measures the proteoglycan defect in human tendon, ligament, valve, or aortic tissue. The mechanism connecting the molecular defect to the two most disabling non-skeletal features is therefore model- or association-based, not demonstrated in the relevant human tissue.

Does DBQD1 share a final common mechanism with other glycosaminoglycan biosynthesis chondrodysplasias, and what distinguishes the chain-elongation defect of CANT1 from the sulfation and linker defects of related genes?
KNOWLEDGE GAP OPEN cant1_shared_gag_biosynthesis_family
Attached to
pathophysiology#Impaired Glycosaminoglycan Chain Synthesis disease#Desbuquois Dysplasia 1
DBQD1 belongs to a family of skeletal dysplasias caused by defective glycosaminoglycan biosynthesis, alongside XYLT1 (DBQD2), the sulfation defects (SLC26A2 diastrophic dysplasia, CHST3), and the linkeropathies (B3GAT3 and others). These converge on an undersulfated or undersized proteoglycan in cartilage matrix, which is why DBQD overlaps clinically and radiographically with CHST3-related spondyloepiphyseal dysplasia with joint dislocations. What is not resolved is how a chain-elongation defect (CANT1) produces a phenotype distinct from, yet overlapping with, the sulfation and linker defects, and whether the shared matrix endpoint offers a common therapeutic target. A shared mechanism module for proteoglycan-synthesis chondrodysplasia is a candidate for factoring this out.

CANT1 Loss-of-Function
Biallelic pathogenic CANT1 variants (nonsense, frameshift and missense) abolish or severely reduce the activity of calcium-activated nucleotidase 1, a soluble UDP-preferring nucleotidase resident in the endoplasmic reticulum and Golgi. Missense alleles reduce enzyme activity and, for some, abolish the calcium-induced dimerization required for full activity.

Growth Plate Chondrocyte Dysfunction and Loss of Zonal Organisation
In the growth plate, GAG content and extracellular matrix space are reduced, proliferating chondrocytes lose their flat columnar shape, and chondrocyte proliferation and maturation are deregulated. Terminal differentiation into hypertrophic chondrocytes is specifically impaired, so the physis stalls at the step CANT1-deficient cartilage cannot complete. This is the chondrocyte-biosynthetic-input instance of the shared growth-plate failure mechanism, with the program stalling at terminal hypertrophic differentiation.

1. Disease information

Overview. Desbuquois dysplasia (DBQD) is an autosomal recessive osteochondrodysplasia in the multiple-dislocation group. Its main features are: - severe short stature that starts before birth and gives rhizomelic or mesomelic shortening; - marked joint laxity with multiple dislocations; - a round, flat face with a flat nasal bridge, prominent eyes and micrognathia (sometimes cleft palate); - typical radiographs: a "Swedish-key" or "monkey-wrench" proximal femur, with a short femoral neck and an exaggerated lesser trochanter, and advanced carpal and tarsal bone age.

Type 1 versus type 2. The two types were originally told apart by the hands: - Type 1: an accessory ossification centre distal to the second metacarpal, a bifid distal thumb phalanx, or a delta phalanx, with other changes. - Type 2: normal hands. - Kim variant: short metacarpals with elongated phalanges.

Molecular studies showed that this hand-based split does not map cleanly onto genes. CANT1 mutations are found in all type 1 cases, in the Kim variant, and in some type 2 cases (Nizon 2012, PMID:22539336; Furuichi 2011, PMID:21037275).

2. Etiology

  • Cause: biallelic (homozygous or compound heterozygous) loss-of-function variants in CANT1.
  • Huber 2009 (PMID:19853239): "Studying nine Desbuquois families, we identified seven distinct mutations in the" CANT1 gene. Four were nonsense or frameshift variants and three were missense ("p.R300C, p.R300H, and p.P299L").
  • Furuichi 2011 (PMID:21037275): "the missense mutations were all associated with loss-of-function."
  • Genetic risk factors:
  • Parental consanguinity is the main risk factor: "approximately 60% of patients had a history of parental consanguinity" (Piwar 2024, PMID:39273648).
  • There are founder alleles in East Asia (c.676G>A p.Val226Met) and in a German cluster (c.228_229insC).
  • Environmental risk factors, protective factors and gene–environment interactions: none known. The disease is fully genetic.
  • Modifier genes: none reported.

4. Genetic and molecular information

Gene: CANT1, hgnc:19721. Its product is a Golgi/ER-resident, type II transmembrane, calcium-dependent nucleotidase that preferentially hydrolyses UDP to UMP and phosphate (UniProt Q8WVQ1; verify).

6. Mechanism and pathophysiology

Ordered causal chain

  1. Biallelic loss-of-function CANT1 variants lead to absent or inactive CANT1 nucleotidase in the ER and Golgi. Loss of dimerization, secretion and activity is shown in vitro (Wang 2020; Furuichi 2011).
  2. Loss of Golgi UDP-to-UMP hydrolysis is thought to result in UDP building up in the Golgi lumen and less UMP being available for exchange through nucleotide-sugar antiporters. This would reduce the supply of UDP-sugars (UDP-xylose, UDP-GlcA, UDP-GalNAc) and possibly product-inhibit glycosyltransferases. This step is inferred from enzyme function and has not been measured directly in patient tissue.
  3. That results in impaired glycosaminoglycan (GAG) chain synthesis on proteoglycans.
  4. Patient fibroblasts: "significant reduced GAG synthesis in presence of β-D-xyloside" (Nizon 2012).
  5. Mouse chondrocytes: "the glycosaminoglycan synthesis was decreased in chondrocytes from Cant1 knock-out mice" (Paganini 2019, PMID:30439444).
  6. The resulting proteoglycans (aggrecan, decorin) carry shorter GAG chains with increased sulfation, and their secretion is delayed.
  7. Paganini 2019: "whilst the sulfation was increased and the overall proteoglycan secretion was delayed."
  8. Gramegna Tota 2024, PMID:39334831: "proteoglycans with shorter GAG chains compared with wild-type animals."
  9. Branch, cellular stress: chondrocytes show "dilated ER cisternae" but "no canonical ER stress response was detected" (Paganini 2019).
  10. The growth-plate extracellular matrix becomes depleted of GAG and matrix space shrinks. This leads to deregulated chondrocyte proliferation and impaired terminal hypertrophic differentiation (Kodama 2020, PMID:32277574: "terminal differentiation to hypertrophic chondrocytes, was impaired in Cant1 KO mice").
  11. Defective endochondral ossification results in prenatal-onset short stature, limb shortening, narrow thorax with pulmonary hypoplasia, and typical femoral and hand malformations.
  12. Branch, connective tissue: the same proteoglycan defect in ligament, tendon and skin (decorin with short dermatan/chondroitin sulfate chains, abnormal collagen fibrillogenesis; Yamashita 2025, PMID:40141107) is thought to lead to joint laxity and multiple dislocations. The extension to joint laxity is inferred from mouse tendon data.
  13. Branch, cardiovascular (inferred): a matrix proteoglycan defect in valves and aortic wall may underlie mitral valve prolapse and aortic root dilatation (Öztürk 2025). This link is proposed, not demonstrated.

8. Temporal development

  • Onset: prenatal. Short limbs and hydrops are detectable on second-trimester ultrasound, at 17–25 weeks in the Laccone 2011 series.
  • Course:
  • Neonatal and infant period: the highest risk is respiratory insufficiency.
  • In survivors: dislocations persist and kyphoscoliosis progresses through childhood.
  • Aortopathy can begin early and progress, over a median 7.7-year follow-up (Öztürk 2025).
  • Duration: lifelong. There is no remission.
  • Critical periods: the perinatal period for airway and respiration; childhood for orthopaedic management of dislocations and spinal deformity.

9. Inheritance and population

  • Inheritance: autosomal recessive (HP:0000007), with complete penetrance and variable expressivity, from lethal hydrops to an MED-like presentation. No anticipation is known.
  • Consanguinity: about 60% of published cases (Piwar 2024).
  • Founder effects:
  • c.676G>A in Japanese and Korean patients: "should be derived from a common founder" (Dai 2011);
  • c.228_229insC in Germany (Laccone 2011).
  • Prevalence: unknown. Orphanet lists DBQD as <1/1,000,000 (unverified). Piwar 2024 counted 111 published cases across all forms, of which 54 were DBQD1, 39 DBQD2 and 14 Kim variant.
  • Geography: reported worldwide, including Europe, the Middle East (Saudi Arabia, Egypt, Turkey), South Asia, East Asia and Thailand.
  • Sex ratio: about 1:1, as expected for an autosomal recessive disease.

10. Diagnostics

  • Imaging: prenatal ultrasound (short long bones, hydrops, narrow thorax) and a postnatal skeletal survey. The key signs are the monkey-wrench femur, hand accessory ossification centres, advanced carpal age and dislocations.
  • Genetic testing:
  • CANT1 sequencing, or a skeletal-dysplasia panel (CANT1, XYLT1, CHST3, B3GAT3, FLNB, SLC26A2 and others).
  • Exome sequencing for antenatal lethal presentations (Biji 2023).
  • SNP-array homozygosity mapping helps in consanguineous families (Forster 2019, PMID:31587486).
  • CMA, karyotype, FISH, mtDNA and repeat testing are not relevant.
  • Functional assays (research only): nucleotidase activity on expressed mutants; GAG synthesis in fibroblasts.
  • Biomarkers: none in clinical use.
  • Differential diagnosis:
  • DBQD2 (XYLT1): normal hands, CANT1 negative;
  • Larsen syndrome (FLNB);
  • CHST3-related spondyloepiphyseal dysplasia with dislocations;
  • B3GAT3-related multiple-joint-dislocation disorders;
  • pseudodiastrophic dysplasia;
  • Ehlers-Danlos syndromes;
  • multiple epiphyseal dysplasia, at the mild end.
  • Screening: no newborn screening. Carrier and cascade testing applies in families with a known variant, and prenatal or preimplantation diagnosis is available (Özalp 2025, PMID:40368527; Kuang 2020, PMID:32922611).

11. Outcome and prognosis

  • Mortality: high perinatal and infant mortality from respiratory insufficiency and pulmonary hypoplasia; hydropic fetuses do not survive (Laccone 2011; Thomas 2021 "respiratory distress"). No survival rates have been published.
  • Long-term problems in survivors: severe short stature, recurrent dislocations, progressive kyphoscoliosis, early osteoarthritis risk, motor delay, and aortopathy that needs surveillance.
  • Prognostic factors: truncating variants and hydrops point to severe disease. Some missense alleles, such as p.Trp125Cys, give a milder MED-like course.

13. Prevention

  • Primary prevention: not applicable.
  • Secondary prevention: carrier testing, prenatal diagnosis by ultrasound plus CANT1 testing, and PGT-M for at-risk couples (Forster 2019; Özalp 2025).
  • Tertiary prevention:
  • spinal and joint surveillance;
  • echocardiographic monitoring of the aortic root and mitral valve (Öztürk 2025 recommends early cardiovascular follow-up);
  • anaesthetic airway planning.
  • Genetic counselling: 25% recurrence risk per pregnancy.
