GJB2-GJB6 Digenic Nonsyndromic Hearing Loss

Mendelian MONDO:0009076 Pathograph 9 Show in embeddings browser Nonsyndromic Hearing Loss Inner Ear Disorder

DFNB1 is the most common cause of autosomal recessive nonsyndromic prelingual sensorineural hearing loss and maps to a complex locus on chromosome 13q12 that contains two genes encoding cochlear gap-junction beta connexins: GJB2 (connexin 26) and GJB6 (connexin 30). Most DFNB1 disease is monogenic, caused by biallelic GJB2 mutations. However, a substantial fraction of patients carry only a single GJB2 mutant allele, and in many of these the second lesion is a large deletion in GJB6, del(GJB6-D13S1830). Because connexin 26 and connexin 30 co-assemble into the same cochlear gap-junction plaques required for potassium recycling, the GJB2 mutation / GJB6 deletion genotype was first interpreted (2002) as digenic inheritance, and it is still widely cited as the textbook example of digenic inheritance in nonsyndromic deafness. The current GeneReviews account reads the same genotype differently: the GJB6-containing deletions (and smaller deletions involving only CRYL1) remove upstream regulatory regions of GJB2 on the same allele and abolish GJB2 expression in cis, so a GJB2 variant in trans with such a deletion amounts to biallelic loss of GJB2 expression. On that reading the disease is monogenic GJB2 deficiency, and the digenic interpretation is historical. Both readings converge on loss of cochlear connexin gap-junction function.

Digenic inheritance (GJB2/GJB6) HP:0010984
Historical interpretation. Patients carrying one GJB2 mutant allele in trans with the del(GJB6-D13S1830) deletion were reported in 2002 as double heterozygotes with a digenic pattern of inheritance, and the genotype is often cited as the canonical example of digenic inheritance in nonsyndromic deafness. GeneReviews now describes these deletions as removing GJB2 regulatory regions on the same allele, which makes the genotype a compound heterozygous loss of GJB2 expression, that is, monogenic autosomal recessive disease. The REFUTE item below records that reading. The binding is kept because the published digenic interpretation is what this entry is named after; whether the entry should remain separate from GJB2-related autosomal recessive hearing loss is an open scope question.

Pathophysiology

4
Deletion of GJB2 Upstream Regulatory Region
Large deletions upstream of GJB2, the most common being the 342-kb del(GJB6-D13S1830) that removes GJB6 and part of CRYL1, delete cis-regulatory sequences of GJB2 on the same chromosome and abolish expression of GJB2 from that allele. In trans with a GJB2 coding variant the result is loss of connexin 26 expression from both alleles.

Loss of Cochlear Connexin Gap-Junction Coupling
Connexin 26 (GJB2) and connexin 30 (GJB6) co-assemble into the gap-junction plaques of the non-sensory epithelial supporting cells and the spiral ligament fibrocytes of the cochlea. Biallelic GJB2 loss, whether from two GJB2 coding variants or from one coding variant in trans with an upstream deletion that silences GJB2 in cis, disrupts this connexin network and abolishes intercellular coupling. Deletions that remove GJB6 also remove the connexin 30 contribution from that allele.

Impaired Endocochlear Potassium Recycling
The cochlear connexin gap-junction networks form the route by which potassium ions entering sensory hair cells during mechanotransduction are recycled back to the endolymph. Loss of connexin 26/30 coupling interrupts this potassium recycling pathway, disturbing endolymph homeostasis.

  DFNB1 is the most common cause of autosomal recessive nonsyndromic prelingual sensorineural hearing loss and maps to a complex locus on chromosome 13q12 that contains two genes encoding cochlear gap-junction beta connexins: GJB2 (connexin 26) and GJB6 (connexin 30). Most DFNB1 disease is monogenic, caused by biallelic GJB2 mutations. However, a substantial fraction of patients carry only a single GJB2 mutant allele, and in many of these the second lesion is a large deletion in GJB6, del(GJB6-D13S1830). Because connexin 26 and connexin 30 co-assemble into the same cochlear gap-junction plaques required for potassium recycling, the GJB2 mutation / GJB6 deletion genotype was first interpreted (2002) as digenic inheritance, and it is still widely cited as the textbook example of digenic inheritance in nonsyndromic deafness. The current GeneReviews account reads the same genotype differently: the GJB6-containing deletions (and smaller deletions involving only CRYL1) remove upstream regulatory regions of GJB2 on the same allele and abolish GJB2 expression in cis, so a GJB2 variant in trans with such a deletion amounts to biallelic loss of GJB2 expression. On that reading the disease is monogenic GJB2 deficiency, and the digenic interpretation is historical. Both readings converge on loss of cochlear connexin gap-junction function.

- name: Loss of Cochlear Connexin Gap-Junction Coupling
  biological_scale: MOLECULAR
  description: |
    Connexin 26 (GJB2) and connexin 30 (GJB6) co-assemble into the gap-junction plaques of the non-sensory epithelial supporting cells and the spiral ligament fibrocytes of the cochlea. Biallelic GJB2 loss, whether from two GJB2 coding variants or from one coding variant in trans with an upstream deletion that silences GJB2 in cis, disrupts this connexin network and abolishes intercellular coupling. Deletions that remove GJB6 also remove the connexin 30 contribution from that allele.
