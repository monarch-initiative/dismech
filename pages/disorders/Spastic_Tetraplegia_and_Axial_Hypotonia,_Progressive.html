Spastic Tetraplegia and Axial Hypotonia, Progressive

Mendelian MONDO:0032828 Pathograph 31 Show in embeddings browser Motor Neuron Disease

Progressive spastic tetraplegia and axial hypotonia (STAHP), also called infantile SOD1 deficiency syndrome, is an autosomal recessive childhood-onset neurodegenerative disorder caused by biallelic SOD1 variants that leave no detectable superoxide dismutase 1 protein or enzyme activity. After apparently normal early development, truncal hypotonia and spasticity of all four limbs appear within the first year or two of life, followed by rapid loss of motor skills, mixed upper and lower motor neuron signs, bulbar involvement and, in some children, respiratory insufficiency requiring ventilation. Cerebellar vermis atrophy is a consistent imaging feature, and a mild hemolytic anemia with reduced erythrocyte glutathione is the main finding outside the nervous system. It is mechanistically the opposite of dominant SOD1 amyotrophic lateral sclerosis, which arises from a toxic gain of function of aggregation-prone mutant protein. Heterozygous carriers have about half the normal enzyme activity and no reported neurological disease.

Why does complete SOD1 absence cause infantile upper and lower motor neuron degeneration in humans but only a distal motor axonopathy, with motor neuron cell bodies spared, in Sod1 knockout mice?
HUMAN MODEL MISMATCH OPEN stahp-mouse-human-mismatch
Attached to
animal_models#SOD1-null mouse pathophysiology#Upper and Lower Motor Neuron Degeneration
The mouse knockout was the basis for the long-held view that loss of SOD1 activity does not damage motor neurons, a view the human syndrome contradicts. Knockout mice do develop a distal motor axonopathy from the first months of life, and SOD1-null dogs develop an early tetraparesis, so the mismatch lies in which parts of the motor system degenerate and how severely, rather than in an absence of motor involvement. Species differences in compensating antioxidants (mice synthesize ascorbate, humans do not), lifespan and motor system size are candidate explanations, but none has been tested.

Proteasomal Degradation of Unstable Mutant SOD1
The p.Cys112Trpfs*11 product is unstable and efficiently removed by the proteasome, so neither the truncated nor any wild-type SOD1 protein is detectable in patient erythrocytes. Unlike dominant ALS-causing SOD1 mutants, it shows little tendency to misfold into insoluble aggregates, which argues against a toxic gain of function.

Absent SOD1 Superoxide Dismutase Activity
SOD1, the principal cytosolic superoxide dismutase, converts superoxide to hydrogen peroxide. In affected children erythrocyte SOD1 activity is essentially zero across all reported alleles. Mouse knockout data suggest the mitochondrial and extracellular isoenzymes do not compensate, plausibly because charged superoxide crosses membranes poorly.

Upper and Lower Motor Neuron Degeneration
Children develop upper motor neuron signs (spasticity of all four limbs, Babinski signs) and, later, lower motor neuron signs (fasciculations, atrophy and chronic denervation on EMG) that are most prominent in the truncal and axial muscles. Elevated plasma phosphorylated neurofilament heavy chain indicates ongoing axonal injury, highest in younger patients. Whether this degeneration is best classed as a separate syndrome or as very early-onset ALS is disputed.

Spastic Tetraplegia and Axial Hypotonia, Progressive (STAHP): A Comprehensive Disease Characteristics Report

Disease: Spastic Tetraplegia and Axial Hypotonia, Progressive MONDO ID: MONDO:0032828 · OMIM: #618598 · Category: Mendelian (autosomal recessive) Causal gene: SOD1 (Cu/Zn superoxide dismutase 1; HGNC:11179; chromosome 21q22.11)

Summary

Spastic Tetraplegia and Axial Hypotonia, Progressive (STAHP) is an ultra-rare, autosomal-recessive, childhood-onset neurodegenerative disorder caused by complete biallelic loss of function of the antioxidant enzyme Cu/Zn superoxide dismutase (SOD1). It was first delineated as a distinct nosological entity in 2019 by Park and colleagues, who identified a homozygous truncating variant, c.335dupG (p.Cys112Trpfs*11), that abolishes all SOD1 enzyme activity in multiple consanguineous families (PMID: 31332433). The clinical picture comprises progressive loss of acquired motor milestones, spastic tetraplegia with lower-extremity predominance, axial (truncal) hypotonia, mild cerebellar atrophy, and a hyperekplexia-like exaggerated startle. Heterozygous carriers, despite having markedly reduced enzyme activity, are neurologically normal — establishing a recessive mechanism in which roughly 50% of SOD1 activity is sufficient for health.

STAHP is best understood as the mechanistic mirror-image of SOD1-associated amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS). Dominant SOD1 missense variants cause ~2% of ALS through a toxic gain-of-function (protein misfolding and aggregation), whereas STAHP arises from the total absence of the enzyme. This distinction has direct therapeutic-safety implications: the FDA-approved antisense oligonucleotide tofersen lowers SOD1 protein to treat SOD1-ALS, and the existence of a human SOD1-deficiency syndrome cautions that deep, chronic SOD1 suppression is not biologically inert. The pathophysiology converges on chronic oxidative stress — most critically in mitochondria and motor neurons — that produces a distal motor axonopathy and neuromuscular-junction (NMJ) failure. This causal chain is strongly supported by Sod1-null (knockout) mouse models, in which the phenotype is rescued by restoring SOD1 selectively to neurons or to the mitochondrial intermembrane space.

Because fewer than ~20 patients have been reported worldwide, almost all from consanguineous pedigrees carrying a recurrent founder frameshift, the evidence base is a hybrid of a small number of human clinical reports and an extensive body of Sod1-knockout mouse biology. There is no approved disease-modifying therapy; care is supportive. Mechanism-rational but clinically unproven strategies include antioxidants/SOD mimetics and SOD1 gene augmentation — the latter supported by the mouse rescue data. This report synthesizes eight confirmed findings across all 15 template sections, flagging clearly where human data are absent and where conclusions are extrapolated from model systems.

Section 1 — Disease Information

Overview. STAHP is a progressive, early-onset neurodegenerative disease of the central and peripheral motor system caused by inherited deficiency of SOD1. The cardinal triad is (i) progressive spastic tetraplegia (upper-motor-neuron signs in all four limbs, lower extremity > upper), (ii) axial hypotonia (truncal low tone), and (iii) progressive loss of previously acquired motor abilities. Additional features include mild cerebellar atrophy and hyperekplexia-like startle responses (PMID: 31332433).

Section 2 — Etiology

Primary cause (genetic). STAHP is a monogenic Mendelian disorder caused by homozygous (biallelic) loss-of-function variants in SOD1. The prototype is the frameshift c.335dupG (p.Cys112Trpfs*11), which truncates the protein and abolishes all enzyme activity. Park et al. state: "In this study we describe the homozygous truncating variant c.335dupG (p.C112Wfs*11) in SOD1 that leads to total absence of enzyme activity" (PMID: 31332433). A second independent homozygous loss-of-function SOD1 variant has since been reported, confirming the gene–disease relationship (PMID: 36935613).

Genetic risk factors. The only established genetic cause is biallelic SOD1 LoF. Consanguinity is the dominant risk factor — all reported families are consanguineous, and the recurrent c.335dupG allele behaves as a founder mutation. There are no established common-variant susceptibility loci (STAHP is Mendelian, not polygenic).

Protective / modifier factors. Being a heterozygous carrier is effectively "protective" against disease: carriers have reduced enzyme activity but no overt neurologic phenotype. Park et al.: "Heterozygous carriers have a markedly reduced enzyme activity when compared to wild-type controls but show no overt neurologic phenotype" (PMID: 31332433), indicating ~50% SOD1 activity suffices. In model systems, the mitochondrial-intermembrane-space pool of SOD1 and neuronal (vs. muscle) SOD1 expression are the critical protective compartments (see Sections 6 and 15). No validated human environmental protective factor is known.

Environmental risk factors / gene–environment interactions. No environmental trigger is required to manifest STAHP — the disorder is fully penetrant from the genetic lesion. However, because SOD1 is the frontline defense against superoxide, oxidative-stress–increasing exposures are biologically plausible modifiers. In Sod1-null mice, superimposed oxidative or metabolic stressors (e.g., diabetes, aging, a second cochlear mutation) accelerate organ-specific injury (renal: PMID: 17697870; hearing: PMID: 20470874). These gene–environment interactions are demonstrated in mice and only inferred for human STAHP.

Characteristics. De Souza et al. described a Brazilian series of 5 patients: childhood-onset spastic tetraplegia with axial hypotonia in all 5, with "gestational history showing polyhydramnios in 4/5 and intrauterine growth restriction in 3/5 patients" (PMID: 34380534), and progressive loss of acquired motor milestones. The prenatal features indicate that the disease process begins in utero, while overt neurodegeneration becomes clinically apparent in childhood. Park et al. summarize the core phenotype as "severe and marked by progressive loss of motor abilities, tetraspasticity with predominance in the lower extremities, mild cerebellar atrophy, and hyperekplexia-like symptoms" (PMID: 31332433).

Quality of life. No formal EQ-5D/SF-36/PROMIS data exist for this ultra-rare disease. Qualitatively, the combination of progressive tetraspasticity, motor regression, and axial hypotonia implies profound loss of independent ambulation and self-care, high caregiver dependence, and likely feeding/respiratory vulnerability — a severe QoL burden inferred from the phenotype rather than directly measured.

Section 4 — Genetic / Molecular Information

Causal gene: SOD1 (Cu/Zn superoxide dismutase 1), OMIM *147450, HGNC:11179, chromosome 21q22.11. SOD1 catalyzes dismutation of the superoxide radical (O₂·⁻) to O₂ and H₂O₂.

Classification: The truncating variants are pathogenic by ACMG/AMP criteria (null variant in a gene where LoF is an established mechanism; PVS1 + segregation + functional enzyme-activity evidence).

Functional consequence: Loss of function — complete abolition of superoxide dismutase activity in the homozygous state. This is the defining molecular feature distinguishing STAHP from the gain-of-function missense variants of SOD1-ALS.

Allele frequency: The c.335dupG founder allele is essentially private to the reported consanguineous pedigrees and is vanishingly rare/absent at homozygosity in population databases (gnomAD), consistent with a recessive ultra-rare disorder.

Somatic vs germline: Germline.

Modifier genes: None established in humans. Mouse data implicate redox-pathway genes (e.g., Gpx4 overexpression modulates the muscle phenotype, PMID: 37878529; the Nrf2/NFE2L2 pathway is broadly protective, PMID: 32061629).

Epigenetic information / chromosomal abnormalities: No disease-specific epigenetic signature or large-scale chromosomal rearrangement is described for STAHP.

Section 5 — Environmental Information

STAHP is a genetically determined, fully penetrant disorder; no environmental exposure, lifestyle factor, or infectious agent is required for or known to cause it. Because SOD1 is the primary cytosolic/mitochondrial-IMS superoxide scavenger, exposures that raise oxidative load (certain drugs, metabolic stress, aging) are plausible disease modifiers, but this is extrapolated from Sod1-knockout mouse organ-injury studies (diabetic nephropathy acceleration, PMID: 17697870; age-related hearing loss, PMID: 10466888) and is not established in patients. Infectious agents: not applicable.

Section 6 — Mechanism / Pathophysiology

Ordered causal chain

  1. Biallelic SOD1 loss-of-function mutation (e.g., c.335dupG) → leads to total absence of Cu/Zn superoxide dismutase enzyme activity. (Demonstrated — enzyme assay, PMID: 31332433.)
  2. Absence of dismutation → results in accumulation of superoxide radical (O₂·⁻) and chronic oxidative stress, most critically at the mitochondrial intermembrane space and in peripheral nerve. (Demonstrated in mouse: oxidized glutathione redox potential in tibial nerve, PMID: 21963651; mitochondrial-IMS SOD1 sufficiency, PMID: 21078595.)
  3. Chronic oxidative stress in motor axons → leads to a progressive distal motor axonopathy, with preferential denervation of fast-twitch muscle, while motor-neuron cell bodies and sensory fibers are relatively preserved. (Demonstrated in mouse, PMID: 21963651.)
  4. Distal axon injury → results in neuromuscular-junction (NMJ) destabilization and functional failure — reduced endplate occupancy, terminal sprouting, axon swelling, and impaired evoked neurotransmitter (synaptic-vesicle) release despite preserved acetylcholine-receptor density. (Demonstrated in mouse, PMID: 24971750.)
  5. NMJ failure + central motor-tract involvement → produces muscle weakness/atrophy and the clinical upper-motor-neuron picture (spastic tetraplegia) together with axial hypotonia, motor regression, mild cerebellar atrophy, and hyperekplexia-like startle. (Human phenotype, PMID: 31332433, PMID: 34380534; the step from NMJ/axon biology to the specific human CNS signs is partly inferred.)

Branch / protective node: Restoring SOD1 selectively to neurons (synapsin promoter) or to the mitochondrial intermembrane space prevents the axonopathy and muscle loss — defining the neuronal, mitochondrial-IMS redox pool as the critical protective compartment. Fischer et al.: "mitochondrial SOD1 is sufficient to prevent biochemical and morphological defects in the Sod1(-/-) model, and to rescue the motor phenotype of these mice when followed to 12 months of age" (PMID: 21078595); and neuron-specific CuZnSOD "is sufficient to prevent the loss of muscle mass and function" (PMID: 24378874).

  • Secondary involvement: Skeletal muscle (atrophy/weakness, secondary to denervation); respiratory and feeding musculature at risk with progression.
  • Tissue/cell level: Nervous tissue — motor neurons and their distal axons (CL:0000100 / CL:0011001); skeletal muscle fibers (CL:0000188), with fast-twitch (type II) fibers preferentially denervated.
  • Subcellular level (GO cellular component): Mitochondrion (GO:0005739) and specifically the mitochondrial intermembrane space (GO:0005758) — the critical protective SOD1 pool; cytosol (GO:0005829, principal SOD1 localization); neuromuscular junction (GO:0031594).

  • Lateralization: Bilateral and symmetric (tetraplegia; axial).

Section 8 — Temporal Development

  • Onset: Prenatal antecedents (polyhydramnios 4/5, IUGR 3/5) with childhood clinical onset of neurologic disease (PMID: 34380534). Onset pattern: insidious/chronic.
  • Progression: Progressive course — loss of previously acquired motor milestones (regression), worsening spasticity. No relapsing-remitting or episodic pattern described. Duration: chronic, lifelong.
  • Stages: Not formally staged; practically, early (axial hypotonia, developmental concerns) → intermediate (emerging spasticity, regression) → advanced (fixed tetraspasticity, severe disability).
  • Remission: None reported; no spontaneous remission.
  • Critical period: The in-utero-to-childhood window is when the degenerative process establishes; mouse rescue data suggest that restoring SOD1 before axonopathy is established is key (preventive rather than reparative), implying an early therapeutic window (PMID: 21078595).

Section 9 — Inheritance and Population

  • Inheritance: Autosomal recessive. Biallelic LoF required; heterozygous carriers are asymptomatic despite reduced enzyme activity (PMID: 31332433).
  • Penetrance: Complete in biallelic-null individuals (all reported homozygotes affected); zero in heterozygotes.
  • Expressivity: Core triad consistent; prenatal features (polyhydramnios, IUGR) variable (present in a majority but not all patients).
  • Genetic anticipation: Not applicable (not a repeat-expansion disorder).
  • Founder effect / consanguinity: Prominent — recurrent c.335dupG founder allele; all families consanguineous.
  • Carrier frequency: Not established; expected very low outside specific consanguineous populations.
  • Epidemiology: Ultra-rare — fewer than ~20 patients published worldwide (principally PMID: 31332433, PMID: 34380534, PMID: 36935613). No reliable prevalence/incidence figures (effectively <1/1,000,000).
  • Demographics / geography: Reported in consanguineous families; a cohort from Brazil (PMID: 34380534). No strong sex bias reported; sex ratio ~1:1 expected for an autosomal-recessive trait.

Section 10 — Diagnostics

  • Genetic testing (definitive): Diagnosis rests on identifying biallelic loss-of-function SOD1 variants. Recommended approach: whole-exome or whole-genome sequencing, or a hereditary-spastic-paraplegia / neurodegeneration gene panel that includes SOD1; targeted single-gene/founder-variant testing (c.335dupG) is appropriate in consanguineous families or known pedigrees.
  • Biochemical biomarker (disease-specific): Red-cell / tissue SOD1 enzyme-activity assay — absent activity in affected homozygotes, intermediate (~50%) in carriers (PMID: 31332433). This is a powerful, mechanism-specific confirmatory test that distinguishes STAHP from SOD1-ALS (where enzyme activity is retained).
  • Imaging: Brain MRI may show mild cerebellar atrophy (HP:0001272).
  • Electrophysiology: EMG / nerve-conduction studies may reflect the distal motor axonopathy / NMJ involvement inferred from mouse models (by extrapolation).
  • Clinical criteria: No formal consensus criteria; diagnosis is the characteristic clinical triad + biallelic SOD1 LoF + absent enzyme activity.
  • Differential diagnosis: Hereditary spastic paraplegias; cerebral palsy (non-progressive — the key distinguishing feature is STAHP's regression); other childhood neurodegenerative / leukodystrophy syndromes. Importantly, SOD1-ALS is NOT a childhood differential and is mechanistically opposite.
  • Screening: Cascade carrier testing within affected families; no newborn or population screening exists (ultra-rare).

Section 11 — Outcome / Prognosis

  • Course: Progressive, chronic, disabling; motor regression and fixed tetraspasticity lead to loss of ambulation and high dependency.
  • Survival/mortality: No formal survival statistics are published given the tiny cohort; prognosis is guarded, driven by progressive neurodegeneration and attendant complications (respiratory, feeding).
  • Morbidity/disability: Severe long-term functional impairment is expected (ICF: profound mobility and self-care limitation).
  • Complications: Inferred from severe spastic tetraplegia — contractures, respiratory insufficiency, aspiration / feeding difficulty, secondary musculoskeletal deformity.
  • Prognostic factors: Complete enzyme absence predicts the full phenotype; no validated molecular prognostic biomarkers exist. Any residual SOD1 activity would be expected to attenuate severity (hypothesis based on carrier data and mouse dosage effects).
  • Recovery: No spontaneous recovery; no disease-modifying therapy currently alters the trajectory.

Section 12 — Treatment

There is no approved disease-specific therapy. Management is supportive (PMID: 31332433, PMID: 34380534).

Supportive / rehabilitative (standard of care): - Physical, occupational, and speech therapy (NCIT: Physical Therapy; Occupational Therapy). - Spasticity management — oral antispastics (baclofen, tizanidine), botulinum toxin, intrathecal baclofen (NCIT: Baclofen; Botulinum Toxin). - Nutritional and respiratory support; orthopedic management of contractures.

Mechanism-rational but unproven (experimental) strategies: - Antioxidants / SOD mimetics (e.g., EUK-8, tempol) — protective in Sod1-null mouse organ-injury models (PMID: 14988070, PMID: 17697870); not tested in STAHP patients. - Nrf2 activators (e.g., sulforaphane) — upregulate endogenous antioxidant defenses and are neuroprotective in oxidative-stress models (PMID: 32061629); conceptual only for STAHP. - SOD1 gene augmentation / enzyme replacement — the most mechanistically direct approach for a loss-of-function disorder. Proof-of-concept from mouse rescue: restoring SOD1 to neurons (PMID: 24378874) or to the mitochondrial intermembrane space (PMID: 21078595) prevents the motor phenotype. Neurotrophic gene therapy (AAV1.NT-3) improved muscle/function in Sod1-KO mice (PMID: 37553101) — supportive, not curative.

Critical caveat — do NOT use SOD1-lowering therapy: Tofersen (an intrathecal antisense oligonucleotide that reduces SOD1 synthesis) is the approved therapy for SOD1-ALS. Miller et al. note tofersen "is the first and only approved therapy for the treatment of ALS in adults who have a variant in the SOD1 gene" (PMID: 41661214); robust human SOD1 reduction is confirmed in autopsy tissue (PMID: 42406382). STAHP, being a SOD1-deficiency disease, requires the opposite intervention (enzyme restoration). Park et al. explicitly caution that the deficiency phenotype "should be considered before application of previously proposed SOD1 silencing as a treatment option for amyotrophic lateral sclerosis" (PMID: 31332433).

Section 13 — Prevention

  • Primary prevention: Not applicable (not acquired). For at-risk couples: genetic counseling, carrier testing, and reproductive options (prenatal diagnosis, preimplantation genetic testing) — especially relevant in consanguineous families carrying the founder allele (NCIT: Genetic Counseling).
  • Secondary prevention: Cascade testing of relatives; early identification could, in principle, open an early therapeutic window should enzyme-restoration therapy become available (mouse data favor early/preventive intervention, PMID: 21078595).
  • Tertiary prevention: Proactive management to prevent complications of spasticity (contracture prevention, respiratory and nutritional surveillance).
  • Immunization / public health / environmental: Not applicable.

Section 14 — Other Species / Natural Disease

  • Taxonomy: Human disease (NCBI Taxon:9606). The closest natural SOD1-related disease in another species is canine degenerative myelopathy (DM) in dogs (NCBI Taxon:9615).
  • Orthologous gene: Canine SOD1 (NCBI Gene ID 403559).
  • Key comparative insight — DM is NOT a STAHP model: Canine DM is caused by homozygous SOD1 missense variants (E40K; also T18S) across ≥5 breeds (Pembroke Welsh corgi, Boxer, Rhodesian ridgeback, German Shepherd, Chesapeake Bay retriever) and features SOD1-immunoreactive cytoplasmic inclusions resembling human SOD1-ALS. Awano et al. report "The resequencing of SOD1 in normal and affected dogs revealed a G to A transition, resulting in an E40K missense mutation" (PMID: 19188595). Biochemically, the canine mutants "form enzymatically active dimers, arguing against a loss of function in affected homozygous animals" (PMID: 23707216), with unfolded-protein-response / ER-stress activation in spinal glia (PMID: 30273700). Thus DM is a gain-of-function (ALS-like) disease, mechanistically the opposite of STAHP.
  • Zoonotic potential: None (genetic disorder).

Mouse model — findings in words. The knockout develops "oxidation of the glutathione pool" in tibial nerve but not muscle, "suggesting that axonal degeneration is a consequence" of nerve oxidative stress (PMID: 21963651). At the synapse, "motor axons innervating the extensor digitorum longus (EDL) and gastrocnemius muscles release fewer synaptic vesicles upon nerve stimulation" (PMID: 24971750). Limitations of the mouse model: The Sod1-KO mouse was developed chiefly as an accelerated-sarcopenia / peripheral-axonopathy model and reproduces the oxidative-stress-driven distal motor axonopathy and NMJ failure. It does not fully capture the human CNS features — spastic tetraplegia (an upper-motor-neuron sign), axial hypotonia, cerebellar atrophy, and hyperekplexia-like startle. The human-to-mouse mapping of the central motor phenotype therefore remains partly inferred. No STAHP-specific iPSC, organoid, or non-murine genetic model has yet been reported.

Limitations and Knowledge Gaps

  1. Tiny human sample size (<~20 patients): No robust epidemiology, natural-history, survival, or quantitative penetrance/expressivity data; frequencies of individual phenotypes rest on small series.
  2. Model–human phenotype gap: The Sod1-KO mouse reproduces peripheral motor-axon/NMJ pathology but not the human upper-motor-neuron spasticity, cerebellar atrophy, or hyperekplexia-like startle; the central-nervous-system mechanism of STAHP is therefore inferred.
  3. No STAHP-specific omics: No patient transcriptomics, proteomics, metabolomics, single-cell, or spatial data are available; molecular-profiling claims are extrapolated.
  4. No disease-specific biomarker validation beyond enzyme activity: Prognostic biomarkers are unknown.
  5. No therapeutic trials: Antioxidant, Nrf2-activator, and SOD1-augmentation strategies are rationally motivated but entirely untested in STAHP patients.
  6. Ontology/identifier gaps: No dedicated ICD-11/MeSH code; Orphanet characterization is minimal.

Proposed Follow-up Experiments / Actions

  1. Establish an international STAHP patient registry to aggregate genotype, enzyme activity, natural history, imaging, and survival across the few known families (addresses Gap 1).
  2. Generate patient-derived iPSC motor neurons / NMJ organoids carrying c.335dupG to test whether the human central and peripheral phenotypes recapitulate the mouse oxidative-stress → axonopathy chain, and to screen antioxidants / SOD mimetics / Nrf2 activators (addresses Gaps 2–3).
  3. Pursue SOD1 gene-augmentation / enzyme-replacement proof-of-concept building on the mouse rescue data (neuronal and mitochondrial-IMS targeting), including biodistribution and an early-intervention window study (addresses Gaps 2, 5; leverages PMID: 24378874, PMID: 21078595).
  4. Deep-phenotype patients with CNS-focused MRI and electrophysiology to test whether a demonstrable distal motor axonopathy / NMJ signature exists in humans, directly bridging the model–human gap.
  5. Perform patient CSF/plasma redox and neurofilament profiling to search for a tractable pharmacodynamic/prognostic biomarker (mirroring neurofilament utility in SOD1-ALS).
  6. Formal nosology/ontology update: advocate for dedicated ICD-11 and Orphanet entries and ensure carrier/cascade-testing guidance for consanguineous populations harboring the founder allele.
