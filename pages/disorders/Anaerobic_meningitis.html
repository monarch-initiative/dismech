Anaerobic meningitis

Infectious Disease MONDO:0002000 Pathograph 18 Show in embeddings browser Bacterial meningitis Anaerobic Bacteria Infectious Disease

Anaerobic meningitis is bacterial meningitis caused by anaerobic bacteria, usually after oral, gastrointestinal, skin, or abscess-associated anaerobes exploit otogenic, odontogenic, traumatic, device-associated, or postoperative barrier disruption, reach the cerebrospinal-fluid compartment, replicate locally, and trigger neutrophilic meningeal inflammation with high rates of intracranial complications and neurologic sequelae.

Disease Information (Section 1)

Overview. Anaerobic meningitis is inflammation of the meninges caused by anaerobic bacteria. It is best regarded as a rare microbiological subset of bacterial meningitis rather than a distinct nosological entity. It is defined by the recovery (by culture or molecular methods) of obligate/aerotolerant anaerobes from cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) in a patient with clinical and CSF evidence of meningitis.

Etiology (Section 2)

Primary cause — infectious/mechanistic. Anaerobic meningitis is caused by anaerobic bacteria that are normal commensals of the oral cavity, gut, and skin gaining access to the subarachnoid space. In a 30-year single-center series of 540 culture-proven adult bacterial meningitis cases, 13 (2.4%) were anaerobic, from which 14 strains were isolated: Fusobacterium nucleatum (3), Prevotella spp. (3), Bacteroides fragilis (3), Cutibacterium/Propionibacterium acnes (3), and Parvimonas micra/Peptostreptococcus micros (2) (PMID: 29396061).

"including nine Gram-negative (G(-)) strains: Fusobacterium nucleatum (3), Prevotella species (3) and Bacteroides fragilis (3), and five Gram-positive (G(+)) strains: Propionibacterium acnes (3) and Peptostreptococcus micros (also known as Parvimonas micra) (2)" — PMID: 29396061

Risk factors (environmental / acquired). There are essentially no genetic causal risk factors. The dominant risk factors are anatomic and clinical:

  • Postneurosurgical state — 53.8% (7/13) in the adult series (PMID: 29396061).
  • Underlying medical conditions — 79.6% (10/13): diabetes mellitus, malignancy, liver cirrhosis, cerebral infarct, alcoholism (PMID: 29396061).
  • Head trauma, prior neurosurgical procedures, and implanted devices (VP shunts, ventricular drains) (PMID: 32717475).
  • ENT infections — near-universal precursor in pediatric F. necrophorum meningitis (acute otitis media in 21/27) (PMID: 41479136).
  • Odontogenic sources — recent molar extraction, periodontitis (PMID: 41330476, PMID: 35692070).

"53.8% (7/13) had a postneurosurgical state as the preceding event, and 79.6% (10/13) had underlying medical conditions including diabetes mellitus, malignancy, liver cirrhosis, cerebral infarct and alcoholism" — PMID: 29396061

Genetic modifiers (not causal). Host genetic variation modifies the inflammatory response and outcome, not susceptibility to infection per se: single-nucleotide polymorphisms in inflammasome genes CARD8 (rs2043211) and NLRP1 (rs11621270) are associated with poor outcome in bacterial meningitis (PMID: 23053059).

Protective factors. Prompt treatment/source control of predisposing ENT, dental, and abdominal infections; strict neurosurgical/device asepsis. No genetic protective alleles are established. Anticapsular antibody protects against B. fragilis bacteremia while T-cell-mediated immunity protects against abscess in animal models (PMID: 549184).

Gene-environment interactions. The only demonstrable GxE interplay is that inflammasome/complement genotype (CARD8, NLRP1; MASP-2) shapes how severely the host responds to the anaerobic infectious trigger (PMID: 23053059, PMID: 28086930).

Phenotypes (Section 3)

Clinical phenotypes are those of bacterial meningitis generally, though anaerobic cases are more likely to be subacute or atypical. In 696 adults with community-acquired bacterial meningitis, the classic triad of fever, neck stiffness, and altered mental status was present in only 44%, but 95% had ≥2 of the 4 cardinal features; 14% were comatose and 33% had focal neurologic deficits (PMID: 15509818).

"The classic triad of fever, neck stiffness, and a change in mental status was present in only 44 percent of episodes; however, 95 percent had at least two of the four symptoms of headache, fever, neck stiffness, and altered mental status." — PMID: 15509818

Quality of life impact. Substantial — the majority of survivors carry neurological deficits (see Prognosis).

Genetic / Molecular Information (Section 4)

Not applicable as a heritable disease. Anaerobic meningitis has no causal genes, no pathogenic germline variants, no inheritance pattern, no penetrance, and no carrier frequency (PMID: 29396061; acquired-infection framing). The only host-genetic contribution is as an outcome modifier:

  • CARD8 (rs2043211) and NLRP1 (rs11621270) inflammasome polymorphisms — associated with poor outcome (PMID: 23053059).
  • MASP-2 (complement lectin pathway) — higher CSF levels correlate with poor functional outcome (PMID: 28086930).

"single-nucleotide polymorphisms in the inflammasome genes CARD8 (rs2043211) and NLRP1 (rs11621270) are associated with poor disease outcome" — PMID: 23053059

The relevant "molecular" genetics are microbial: bacterial virulence loci encoding the B. fragilis capsular polysaccharide biosynthesis genes and the F. necrophorum leukotoxin operon (see Mechanism).

Environmental Information (Section 5)

Environmental / lifestyle contributors operate by predisposing to anaerobic invasion: chronic otitis media, sinusitis, periodontal disease, dental extraction, alcoholism, diabetes mellitus, malignancy, cirrhosis, and neurosurgery/indwelling devices (PMID: 29396061, PMID: 32717475, PMID: 41330476).

Mechanism / Pathophysiology (Section 6)

Ordered causal chain

  1. Endogenous anaerobes colonize a mucosal/skin site (oropharynx, gut, skin) → serve as the reservoir.
  2. A predisposing lesion (ENT/odontogenic infection, neurosurgery, trauma, device, immunosuppression) breaches the normal barrier → leads to anaerobic access to blood or contiguous CNS tissue.
  3. Anaerobes reach the subarachnoid space by contiguous spread, direct inoculation, or hematogenous/gut translocation → results in subarachnoid bacterial replication (favored by the immune-privileged, complement-poor CSF).
  4. Branch (F. necrophorum): oropharyngeal infection → septic internal jugular vein thrombophlebitis (Lemierre syndrome) → septic emboli → CNS seeding (PMID: 42568628, PMID: 41479136).
  5. Branch (B. fragilis): capsular polysaccharide → abscess formation independent of other organisms (PMID: 6954635).
  6. Bacterial components are recognized by Toll-like receptors on antigen-presenting/brain cells → activates NF-κB and MAPK pathways (PMID: 22618789).
  7. Transcriptional activation → up-regulates proinflammatory mediators TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-6, and chemokines (PMID: 22618789, PMID: 24105840).
  8. Cytokine/chemokine surge → recruits and activates polymorphonuclear neutrophils into the subarachnoid space.
  9. Activated neutrophils → release superoxide anion and nitric oxide → form peroxynitrite → generate oxidative stress (PMID: 22618789).
  10. Oxidative stress → causes lipid peroxidation, mitochondrial damage, and blood-brain-barrier breakdown (PMID: 22618789).
  11. BBB breakdown + inflammation → produces cerebral edema, raised intracranial pressure, impaired cerebral blood flow, and neuronal injury → clinical meningitis, deficits, death.
  12. Host genetic modifiers (CARD8/NLRP1 inflammasome, MASP-2 complement) tune the magnitude of steps 5–9 and thereby outcome (PMID: 23053059, PMID: 28086930).

Virulence factors. The B. fragilis capsular polysaccharide (CP) is the key virulence factor: encapsulated strains form abscesses directly attributable to CP, and CP protects against phagocytosis (PMID: 6954635, PMID: 3865350). By contrast, B. fragilis LPS is chemically incomplete and weakly endotoxic — the abscess-inducing LPS dose is ~5× higher than that of CP — explaining infrequent DIC/purpura in Bacteroides sepsis (PMID: 549184, PMID: 3711690).

"The abscesses caused by encapsulated strains were shown to be directly attributable to the capsular polysaccharide, which is an important virulence factor of this organism." — PMID: 6954635

"the lipopolysaccharides of Bacteroides lack the biologic potency characteristic of endotoxin" — PMID: 549184

Model-organism corroboration. In Wistar rats, experimental Gram-negative (Klebsiella) meningitis produced a TNF-α/IL-1β/IL-6/CINC-1 surge within 6–24 h and long-term memory impairment (PMID: 24105840).

  • Primary organ / body system: the leptomeninges (pia-arachnoid) and subarachnoid space of the central nervous system — UBERON:0002360 (meninx), UBERON:0002361 (pia mater), UBERON:0002362 (arachnoid mater), UBERON:0001359 (CSF), UBERON:0001017 (CNS).
  • Secondary involvement: brain parenchyma (cerebritis/abscess, UBERON:0000955), ventricles (ventriculitis), cranial nerves, cavernous sinus and internal jugular vein (thrombosis), mastoid air cells and paranasal sinuses (contiguous foci).
  • Tissue/cell level: vascular endothelium of the BBB, neutrophils (CL:0000775), microglia (CL:0000129), astrocytes (CL:0000127), neurons (CL:0000540).

  • Localization / lateralization: the meningitis itself is typically diffuse/bilateral; complications (mastoiditis, subperiosteal/epidural abscess, IJV thrombosis, cavernous sinus thrombosis) may be unilateral or bilateral (PMID: 41479136, PMID: 42568628).

Temporal Development (Section 8)

  • Onset: acute to subacute; anaerobic cases skew more subacute/insidious than pyogenic aerobic meningitis, sometimes without fever or neck stiffness (PMID: 37629718).
  • Age of onset: pediatric cluster (median 4 y for F. necrophorum), adults 22–77 y, and rare neonates (B. fragilis) (PMID: 41479136, PMID: 29396061, PMID: 31838318).
  • Progression: rapid if untreated; complicated meningitis (20/27 pediatric cases) with mastoiditis, subperiosteal/epidural abscess, or Lemierre syndrome, 12/27 requiring surgical drainage (PMID: 41479136).
  • Course/duration: typically requires prolonged therapy (often 4–8 weeks when abscess present); not relapsing-remitting, though superinfection/recurrence occurs with retained devices (PMID: 28089421).
  • Critical period for intervention: early antibiotic + corticosteroid administration (steroids before/with antibiotics) is the key therapeutic window (PMID: 41675892, PMID: 40113367).

Diagnostics (Section 10)

Core principle: routine aerobic culture misses fastidious anaerobes, and anaerobic culture is low-yield and contamination-prone (many C. acnes isolates are contaminants) (PMID: 32717475).

"In the majority of situations, recovery of C. acnes from CSF or ventricular fluid was regarded as contamination." — PMID: 32717475

Laboratory / CSF. Neutrophilic pleocytosis, elevated protein, low glucose; CSF lactate is the most useful discriminator — pooled sensitivity 0.93 (95% CI 0.89–0.96) and specificity 0.96 (95% CI 0.93–0.98) at ~35 mg/dL, generated by bacterial anaerobic metabolism (PMID: 21382412).

"The pooled test characteristics of CSF lactate were sensitivity 0.93 (95% CI: 0.89-0.96), specificity 0.96 (95% CI: 0.93-0.98)" — PMID: 21382412

Molecular diagnostics (increasingly first-line for anaerobes). 16S rRNA PCR and metagenomic next-generation sequencing (mNGS) recover pathogens when culture fails (PMID: 35692070, PMID: 37775739, PMID: 41479136).

Imaging. Contrast CT/MRI to detect meningeal enhancement, brain abscess, mastoiditis/sinusitis, and cavernous sinus / internal jugular vein thrombosis (PMID: 41330476, PMID: 42568628).

Differential diagnosis. Aerobic bacterial meningitis; aseptic/viral meningitis; tuberculous meningitis; drug-induced aseptic meningitis (e.g., trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole, PMID: 39540422); primary amebic meningoencephalitis (Naegleria fowleri), which mimics bacterial meningitis (PMID: 42426619). Genetic testing (WGS/WES/panels/karyotype/CMA) is not applicable.

Treatment (Section 12)

Pharmacotherapy — antimicrobial mainstay. Metronidazole (CHEBI:6909; NCIT: Metronidazole) is bactericidal against obligate anaerobes with excellent CSF penetration; it eradicated anaerobic pathogens in CNS infection with 5/6 patients recovering in an early series (PMID: 434970). Modern regimens combine metronidazole with a third-generation cephalosporin (ceftriaxone/cefotaxime) for Gram-negative/streptococcal coverage (PMID: 41330476). Alternatives: carbapenems (meropenem) and β-lactam/β-lactamase-inhibitor combinations (piperacillin-tazobactam). Gram-positive anaerobes (Cutibacterium, Parvimonas) are usually penicillin-susceptible (PMID: 29396061).

"In all cases the anaerobic pathogens were eradicated. Five of the six patients recovered." — PMID: 434970

Source control / surgery. Surgical excision or drainage of abscess and replacement of infected devices, with prolonged antibiotics (usually 4–8 weeks) when abscess is present (PMID: 28089421, PMID: 32717475).

"surgical excision or drainage combined with prolonged antibiotics (usually 4-8weeks) remains the treatment of choice." — PMID: 28089421

Adjunctive corticosteroids. Dexamethasone reduces mortality and neurological sequelae in acute bacterial (especially pneumococcal) meningitis when given before/with antibiotics (PMID: 41675892); pediatric data support dexamethasone 0.15 mg/kg q6h × 4 days within 12 h (adjusted 30-day death 6% vs 12%) (PMID: 40113367).

"Corticosteroids significantly reduce mortality and neurological sequelae in acute bacterial meningitis (particularly pneumococcal), especially when administered before or with antibiotics." — PMID: 41675892

Anticoagulation is used for associated cavernous sinus/IJV thrombosis (Lemierre) though evidence remains debated (PMID: 41330476, PMID: 42568628).

NCIT clinical-intervention terms: Metronidazole, Ceftriaxone, Cefotaxime, Meropenem, Piperacillin-Tazobactam, Dexamethasone, Surgical Drainage.

Prevention (Section 13)

Prevention is non-vaccine (no licensed vaccine targets these anaerobes):

  • Primary: prompt treatment/source control of predisposing ENT, dental, sinus, and abdominal infections; strict neurosurgical and device asepsis; judicious device management.
  • Secondary: early recognition of complicated ENT infection and Lemierre-associated thrombophlebitis to prevent CNS extension.
  • Tertiary: prolonged antibiotics, surgical drainage, and device replacement to prevent relapse/complications.
  • Public health: dental/oral hygiene, timely otitis media/sinusitis management.

No genetic counseling or carrier/newborn screening applies (non-heritable).

Other Species / Natural Disease (Section 14)

Anaerobes cause naturally occurring disease in animals — necrobacillosis. Fusobacterium necrophorum (NCBI:txid859) is an opportunistic pathogen causing calf diphtheria, foot rot, and liver abscesses in cattle; its leukotoxin (Lkt) is the major virulence factor and a protective antigen, with subsp. necrophorum more leukotoxic than subsp. funduliforme (PMID: 30771459).

"In animals, it is an opportunistic pathogen frequently associated with necrotic infections, generally called necrobacillosis, such as calf diphtheria, foot rot and liver abscesses in cattle." — PMID: 30771459

"Among several virulence factors, leukotoxin (Lkt) is considered to be a major factor and a protective antigen." — PMID: 30771459

Other implicated anaerobes span species: B. fragilis (txid817), Prevotella, Parvimonas micra, Clostridium perfringens (txid1502), Cutibacterium acnes (txid1747). Comparative pathology: abscess-forming, tissue-necrotizing anaerobic infection is broadly conserved across mammals, but frank anaerobic meningitis is documented predominantly in humans; veterinary anaerobic disease more often manifests as abscesses/soft-tissue necrosis. Zoonotic transmission of these commensal anaerobes is not a recognized route of human meningitis.

Model Organisms (Section 15)

There is no dedicated model of anaerobic meningitis, but rodent bacterial-meningitis models (Wistar rats; intracisternal S. pneumoniae or K. pneumoniae) recapitulate the shared downstream pathophysiology: a TNF-α/IL-1β/IL-6/CINC-1 cytokine surge within 6–24 h, BBB disruption, and long-term cognitive/memory impairment (PMID: 24105840, PMID: 23085269). Anaerobe-specific animal models exist for B. fragilis abscess pathogenesis (mouse/rat intra-abdominal abscess models establishing capsular polysaccharide as the virulence factor; PMID: 6954635, PMID: 3865350, PMID: 3711690) and for F. necrophorum necrobacillosis/leukotoxin (PMID: 30771459). Limitations: these models capture the inflammatory cascade and virulence-factor biology but not the specific meningeal invasion route of anaerobes in humans. Resources: MGI, RGD (rat), and standard laboratory rodent strains; no organoid/iPSC anaerobic-meningitis model is established.

Mechanistic Model / Interpretation

The unifying model is a two-hit process: (Hit 1) a barrier-breaching predisposing lesion allows endogenous anaerobic flora to reach the CSF; (Hit 2) subarachnoid replication triggers a stereotyped, TLR-driven innate inflammatory cascade whose intensity — modulated by pathogen virulence factors and host inflammasome/complement genotype — determines the degree of BBB breakdown and neuronal injury. Anaerobe-specific features graft onto this shared backbone: the B. fragilis capsule biases toward walled-off abscess (with weak systemic endotoxicity), while F. necrophorum biases toward septic thrombophlebitis (Lemierre) and leukotoxin-mediated tissue necrosis. Therapeutically, the model rationalizes the triad of (1) anaerobe-active antibiotics (metronidazole ± β-lactam) to remove the Hit-2 stimulus, (2) source control to remove the Hit-1 nidus, and (3) corticosteroids to blunt the injurious inflammatory amplification.

Limitations and Knowledge Gaps

  1. No anaerobe-specific mechanistic studies of meningitis — the inflammatory cascade is inferred from pneumococcal/Klebsiella models, not from anaerobe-induced meningitis directly.
  2. Small sample sizes — the largest dedicated adult series has only 13 cases; pediatric data 27 cases. No prospective trials of anaerobic meningitis specifically exist.
  3. Diagnostic under-ascertainment — routine practice omits anaerobic culture, so true incidence is likely underestimated; molecular detection is reshaping the epidemiology.
  4. Corticosteroid evidence is indirect — benefit is established for pneumococcal/general bacterial meningitis, not specifically anaerobic; TBM data show genotype-dependent heterogeneity (PMID: 41540106).
  5. No genetic architecture — beyond a few inflammasome/complement modifiers, host susceptibility genetics are unexplored.
  6. Optimal antibiotic duration, and the role/timing of anticoagulation in Lemierre-associated CNS disease remain undefined.

Proposed Follow-up Experiments / Actions

  1. Multicenter registry with routine mNGS/16S on all culture-negative meningitis CSF to establish true incidence and species distribution of anaerobic meningitis.
  2. Prospective validation of CSF lactate + molecular panels as a rapid rule-in pathway for anaerobic meningitis, with cost-effectiveness analysis.
  3. Develop an anaerobe-specific meningitis model (e.g., intracisternal F. necrophorum or B. fragilis) to directly test the TLR/inflammasome cascade and the effect of capsule/leukotoxin knockouts on CNS injury.
  4. Genotype-stratified adjunctive corticosteroid evaluation (CARD8/NLRP1/MASP-2) in bacterial meningitis cohorts including anaerobic cases, mirroring the LTA4H-stratified TBM approach.
  5. Structured outcome study of anticoagulation timing in anaerobic meningitis complicated by cavernous sinus/IJV thrombosis.
  6. Preventive audit of ENT/odontogenic source control and neurosurgical device asepsis protocols to quantify modifiable risk.
