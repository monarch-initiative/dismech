CNS Tumor with BCOR Internal Tandem Duplication

MONDO:0858966 Pathograph 15 Show in embeddings browser CNS embryonal tumour

CNS tumor with BCOR internal tandem duplication is a rare malignant tumour of the brain in young children. It was separated in 2016 from the tumours then called primitive neuroectodermal tumours of the central nervous system, and the 2021 World Health Organization classification lists it among the embryonal tumours. It is defined by the duplication of a short stretch of the last exon of the BCOR gene, the same lesion that defines clear cell sarcoma of kidney and a soft tissue sarcoma of infancy. Sixty-nine cases had been reported by 2025. The median age at diagnosis is about three years, and boys and girls are affected equally. The tumour is a single large, sharply bounded mass, in the cerebellum in about two fifths of cases and in the cerebral hemispheres in most of the rest, and children present with raised pressure in the head. Under the microscope the tumour is made of uniform oval or spindle cells arranged around vessels in the manner of an ependymoma, and the nuclei stain strongly for BCOR, a stain that is sensitive but not specific. The duplication lies in the part of the BCOR protein that binds PCGF1 to build the polycomb complex PRC1.1. In binding experiments it prevents that assembly, and in human neural stem cells it releases polycomb target genes from repression. Which cell the tumour starts in is not settled. There is no standard treatment. Resection, radiotherapy and multi-agent chemotherapy are used. The tumour often comes back, and a review of the reported cases states that most patients die of it, although survivors of more than ten years are recorded.

Which cell does the tumour start in, and is it an embryonal tumour or a mesenchymal one?

The classification places the tumour among the embryonal tumours, but the question was open from the first reports, because the same duplication defines sarcomas of the kidney and of soft tissue. Does the duplication abolish a function of BCOR, or change it?
KNOWLEDGE GAP OPEN cns_bcor_itd_mechanism
Attached to
pathophysiology#Disrupted PRC1.1 Assembly pathophysiology#Derepression of Polycomb Target Genes
Two observations pull in different directions. The tumours express the altered BCOR at high levels, unlike the tumours in which BCOR is deleted or truncated, and the paper that defined the entity read this as a sign of a different mechanism. Yet a BCOR fragment with the duplication cannot bind PCGF1, which would amount to a loss of PRC1.1. In neural stem cells the duplication releases polycomb targets and, in mouse cells only, speeds growth. No study has examined the complex in tumour cells, so it is not known whether the tumour lacks PRC1.1, has an altered one, or uses the abundant altered protein in another way.

BCOR Internal Tandem Duplication
A short stretch of the last exon of BCOR is duplicated in tandem and in frame, on one allele. The duplication adds 9 to 42 amino acids, most often 20 to 30, near the end of the BCOR protein. It was found in all ten tumours of the defining class that were sequenced in 2016, and it is the same duplication as in clear cell sarcoma of kidney. It is a somatic change. The tumours express BCOR at high levels, which is why a mechanism other than simple loss of BCOR has been proposed. The cell in which the duplication arises is not settled: one study argues from gene expression for a primitive neuro-glial progenitor, and another from stem cell markers for a neuroepithelial stem cell. The cell type bound here is the first of these and is an inference.

Disrupted PRC1.1 Assembly
BCOR is part of PRC1.1, a variant of polycomb repressive complex 1, and the complex is built through the binding of the PUFD domain of BCOR to PCGF1. The duplications of the kidney and brain tumours fall in the two ends of that domain, which form the binding surface. In binding experiments, a BCOR PUFD with a duplication failed to assemble with PCGF1, and the authors conclude that losing the ability to build PRC1.1 is the event that drives the tumours. Whether the complex is lost or altered in the tumour cells themselves has not been shown.

Derepression of Polycomb Target Genes
In human neural stem cells, BCOR with the duplication released genes that polycomb complexes keep silent, compared with normal BCOR. The duplication gave the human cells no growth advantage, and long-term cultures could not be established. In mouse neural stem cells it increased growth, invasion and migration, which normal BCOR did not. The neural stem cells are the laboratory model in which the effect was shown. No cell type is bound on this node, because the derepression has not been examined in the tumour cells.

Neuroepithelial Tumour Growth
The tumour is a solid, compact, sharply bounded mass of uniform oval to spindle cells with fine chromatin and a rich branching network of capillaries. The cells form pseudorosettes around vessels, like those of ependymoma, and there is palisading necrosis, but the vessels do not proliferate as they do in glioblastoma. The tumour is large when it is found: reported diameters run from 3.8 to 10.2 cm.
