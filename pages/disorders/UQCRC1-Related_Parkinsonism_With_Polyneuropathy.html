UQCRC1-Related Parkinsonism With Polyneuropathy

Mendelian MONDO:0036193 Pathograph 24 Show in embeddings browser hereditary neurological disease

UQCRC1-related parkinsonism with polyneuropathy (PKNPY; OMIM 619279) is a late-onset autosomal dominant movement disorder first described in 2020 in Taiwanese and Japanese families. UQCRC1 encodes ubiquinol-cytochrome c reductase core protein 1, one of the two non-catalytic core proteins of mitochondrial respiratory chain complex III; it carries no Q-cycle redox chemistry itself but stabilises the complex III homodimer. The reported heterozygous missense variants p.Tyr314Ser and p.Ile311Leu alter conserved residues required for homodimerisation, and structural modelling and functional assays indicate they destabilise the complex and selectively reduce complex III activity, raising reactive oxygen species and impairing ATP production. Affected carriers develop asymmetrical, tremor-predominant, levodopa-responsive parkinsonism with onset between the late forties and the mid-sixties, together with a slowly progressive, length-dependent axonal sensorimotor polyneuropathy that begins distally in the limbs, often years before the motor signs; anxiety is common. CRISPR knock-in human dopaminergic cells, Drosophila and mice recapitulate age-dependent locomotor decline, nigral dopaminergic loss, striatal dopamine deficiency and peripheral nerve degeneration, and the mouse motor phenotype improves with levodopa. The gene's status as a Parkinson disease gene is contested: multiple candidate-gene cohorts in European and Chinese populations did not replicate an association, while a UK Biobank analysis of ~500,000 people linked rare UQCRC1 protein-truncating variants to Parkinson disease risk and a further Austrian family and functional studies support pathogenicity.

The 2020 discovery rested on co-segregation in two Taiwanese families plus functional validation in flies and mice. Subsequent candidate-gene screens in a European cohort and in Chinese and Taiwanese cohorts did not replicate an association. A UK Biobank analysis of about 500,000 people then found rare protein-truncating variants associated with Parkinson disease (OR 6.59), proposing that the original missense screens were underpowered for such rare alleles, and a further Austrian family shares a different rare missense variant. The question matters for whether UQCRC1 should be offered in diagnostic panels and for the mechanism (missense dominant-negative vs loss-of-function haploinsufficiency).

UQCRC1 Homodimer-Interface Missense Variant
Heterozygous p.Tyr314Ser or p.Ile311Leu alters a conserved residue at the complex III homodimer interface. In silico structural modelling predicts both substitutions destabilise the homodimer, and because UQCRC1 carries no Q-cycle catalytic activity the effect is on complex stability rather than on a lost enzymatic function. The discovery report proposes a dominant-negative action on the homodimer; that is a hypothesis, and the later association of protein-truncating variants with Parkinson disease would instead fit haploinsufficiency, so the functional impact category is recorded as unknown.

Complex III Homodimer Destabilization
Ile311 and Tyr314 sit where the two UQCRC1 monomers of the complex III homodimer meet. In silico modelling on the published human complex III structure predicts that p.Ile311Leu alters the packing of neighbouring side chains and that p.Tyr314Ser removes an aromatic ring and disturbs van der Waals packing, so that both destabilise the homodimer. This step is a structural prediction: the discovery report did not measure complex III assembly or abundance in patient or model tissue.
