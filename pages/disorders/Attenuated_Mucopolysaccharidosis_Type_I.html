Attenuated Mucopolysaccharidosis Type I

Mendelian Pathograph 15 Show in embeddings browser Mucopolysaccharidosis Lysosomal Storage Disorder

Attenuated mucopolysaccharidosis type I is the non-neuronopathic end of the MPS I spectrum, comprising the entities historically named Hurler-Scheie syndrome (MPS I H/S, intermediate) and Scheie syndrome (MPS I S, mildest). Like Hurler syndrome it is caused by biallelic IDUA variants and lysosomal accumulation of dermatan and heparan sulfate, but residual alpha-L-iduronidase activity is sufficient to spare the developing brain. The defining contrast with Hurler syndrome is therefore cognitive: attenuated patients have normal or near-normal intelligence and normal early development, and the disease presents not in infancy but in later childhood or adulthood with progressive somatic and connective-tissue disease - joint stiffness and contracture, carpal tunnel syndrome, corneal clouding, mitral and aortic valve disease, restrictive and obstructive airway disease, short stature, dysostosis multiplex, and cervical cord compression. Because the presenting complaints are orthopaedic and rheumatological rather than developmental, attenuated MPS I is characteristically diagnosed late, often years after the first symptom and sometimes in adulthood. That preserved cognition is also what reverses the therapeutic logic: hematopoietic stem cell transplantation is the standard of care for severe MPS I because it delivers enzyme across the blood-brain barrier, whereas in attenuated MPS I there is no CNS disease to rescue and enzyme replacement therapy with laronidase, which does not cross the blood-brain barrier, is the appropriate first-line treatment. Attenuated MPS I is curated as an entry separate from Hurler syndrome because the two differ in age at presentation, in the organ systems that drive morbidity, and in the treatment decision - not merely in severity.

Autosomal recessive inheritance HP:0000007
Attenuated MPS I is autosomal recessive, caused by biallelic IDUA variants. Position on the severe-to-attenuated spectrum is largely genotype-driven: attenuated disease requires at least one allele retaining residual alpha-L-iduronidase activity, typically a missense allele, whereas two null alleles (nonsense or deletion) produce Hurler syndrome.

Subtypes

2
Hurler-Scheie syndrome (MPS I H/S) MONDO:0011759
The intermediate attenuated phenotype. Somatic disease is progressive and clinically significant - dysostosis multiplex, joint contracture, corneal clouding, valve disease, airway obstruction, short stature - with onset typically in later childhood, but without the primary cognitive decline of Hurler syndrome. Some patients have mild learning difficulty. Untreated, survival is usually into the second or third decade. The boundary with Scheie syndrome is a matter of degree and is not reliably drawable prospectively in an individual patient.

Scheie syndrome (MPS I S, formerly MPS V) MONDO:0011760

Pathophysiology

5
Partial IDUA deficiency with residual alpha-L-iduronidase activity
Biallelic IDUA variants reduce but do not abolish alpha-L-iduronidase activity. This node is the point at which the attenuated and severe arms of MPS I diverge, and the divergence is quantitative at the enzyme and qualitative in the clinic. Severe MPS I arises from absent or extremely low enzyme, typically from two null alleles - deletions and nonsense variants. Attenuated MPS I requires residual activity, which in practice means at least one missense allele producing a partially functional enzyme. The residual activity is not enough to prevent progressive somatic storage, but is enough to keep storage in the developing brain below the threshold that produces neurodegeneration, which is why cognition is preserved.

Lysosomal dermatan and heparan sulfate accumulation
Partially degraded dermatan and heparan sulfate accumulate in lysosomes and in the extracellular matrix. This is the GAG-accumulation hub of the mps_gag_storage module. In attenuated MPS I the dermatan-sulfate connective-tissue arm is fully engaged and drives the whole clinical picture, while the heparan-sulfate neuronopathic arm is engaged only weakly or not at all - the residual enzyme keeps cerebral heparan sulfate storage below the neurodegenerative threshold. That asymmetry is the mechanistic content of the word "attenuated": it does not mean uniformly milder disease, it means the CNS arm is spared while the connective-tissue arm continues to progress, sometimes to severe joint, valve, airway, and spinal morbidity. Extracellular accumulation matters as much as the lysosomal compartment here, because dermatan sulfate proteoglycans - decorin and biglycan - are the regulators of collagen fibril assembly in cartilage, tendon, cornea, and heart valve.

Extracellular matrix and collagen disorganization in connective tissue
The engine of attenuated MPS I. GAG-laden cells and excess extracellular dermatan sulfate distort collagen architecture in every tissue where dermatan sulfate proteoglycans regulate fibril assembly, and the clinical manifestations map onto that tissue list almost one for one: cornea (clouding, loss of transparency), cartilage and bone (dysostosis multiplex, short stature), tendon and joint capsule (stiffness, contracture, claw hand), flexor retinaculum (carpal tunnel syndrome), cardiac valve (thickened immobile leaflets with regurgitation and stenosis), airway and thoracic cage (obstructive and restrictive respiratory disease), and the craniocervical junction and spinal canal (cord compression). Because the process is additive rather than episodic, it progresses steadily over decades, which is why disease burden in attenuated MPS I is a function of how long the patient went undiagnosed.

Cardiac valve storage disease
Valvular disease is near-universal in attenuated MPS I and starts earlier than the diagnosis usually does. In the international MPS I Registry, mitral regurgitation was the commonest and earliest echocardiographic finding in attenuated patients, present in about three quarters of them at a median age of 8 years, and left-sided valve stenosis was also common (mitral 30 percent, aortic 25 percent). The pattern of progression differs from severe MPS I: ventricular wall and septal thickness z-scores rise with age in the severe phenotype but not in the attenuated one, so the attenuated arm is a valve disease rather than a progressive infiltrative cardiomyopathy. In practice this makes echocardiography a reasonable first-line investigation in a child with unexplained joint stiffness and corneal haze - the murmur is often there before anyone has thought of a storage disorder.

Preserved central nervous system function
A deliberately curated negative node. Preserved cognition is not the absence of a finding in attenuated MPS I - it is the defining feature, the thing that separates this entry from Hurler syndrome, and the reason the treatment algorithm differs. In a treated attenuated cohort followed for a median of ten years, 84 percent had normal cognitive development at last follow-up. Modeling this as a node rather than as prose keeps the contrast explicit in the pathograph: the heparan-sulfate arm of the storage hub terminates here rather than in neurodegeneration. It also has a direct clinical consequence, since it is why blood-brain-barrier penetration - the whole rationale for transplantation in severe MPS I - is not a requirement of therapy here.

Weekly intravenous recombinant human alpha-L-iduronidase, and the appropriate first-line disease-modifying therapy for attenuated MPS I. The reasoning is the mirror image of the severe case: laronidase does not cross the blood-brain barrier, which disqualifies it as sole therapy in neuronopathic MPS I, but in attenuated disease there is no CNS component to treat, so its inability to reach the brain costs nothing while its low procedural risk relative to transplantation counts for a great deal. In the pivotal randomized trial it improved percent-predicted forced vital capacity and six-minute walk distance and reduced urinary GAG; in long-term cohorts it stabilizes respiratory function, slows adverse cardiac progression, improves auditory transmission, and slows the decline in height z-scores when begun before about 9 years of age. What it does not do is reverse established skeletal, corneal, valvular, or spinal disease, which is why time to diagnosis dominates outcome. Infusion reactions are common but mild, and anti-laronidase antibodies develop in most treated patients without apparent clinical effect.

Diagnosis

4
Urinary glycosaminoglycan screening followed by enzyme assay and IDUA sequencing
The conventional symptomatic diagnostic pathway. Elevated urinary GAG with a dermatan-and-heparan-sulfate pattern prompts leukocyte or fibroblast alpha-L-iduronidase assay, and biallelic IDUA variants confirm the diagnosis and inform the severe-versus-attenuated prediction. The limitation of this pathway in attenuated MPS I is not sensitivity but initiation: someone has to think of it. The presenting complaints - stiff joints, a hernia, a heart murmur, carpal tunnel syndrome, corneal haze - are each individually unremarkable and are usually managed by separate specialties, so the diagnosis often follows years of specialist care for the parts.

Differential Diagnoses

4

Conditions with similar clinical presentations that must be differentiated from Attenuated Mucopolysaccharidosis Type I:

Hurler syndrome MONDO:0011758
Overlapping Features The same enzyme deficiency at the severe end of the spectrum. The distinction is not academic: it determines whether the patient is referred for transplantation or started on enzyme replacement therapy, and with newborn screening it must now often be made before any symptom has appeared. Genotype is the strongest predictor, supported by age at presentation and developmental trajectory.
Distinguishing Features
  • Normal early development with preserved cognition favors attenuated MPS I.
  • Onset after infancy, often in later childhood or adulthood, favors attenuated MPS I.
  • Two null IDUA alleles (nonsense or deletion) favor Hurler syndrome.
  • Developmental regression in the first years of life favors Hurler syndrome.
Show evidence (1 reference)
PMID:41353341 SUPPORT Human Clinical
"MPS I is classified as severe (Hurler syndrome, OMIM#607014) with extensive multisystemic and CNS involvement, and attenuated ((Hurler/Scheie, OMIM#607015 and Scheie, OMIM#607016) with slower progression and minimal neurological involvement"
States the classification and the CNS-involvement basis on which the two arms are distinguished.
Hunter syndrome MONDO:0010674
Overlapping Features Attenuated MPS II is the closest clinical mimic of attenuated MPS I: the same two stored GAGs, the same somatic picture of joint stiffness, valve disease, airway obstruction, and dysostosis multiplex, and preserved cognition in the attenuated form. The two most useful discriminators are the absence of corneal clouding in MPS II and its X-linked inheritance, so a boy with an affected maternal uncle and clear corneas points to Hunter syndrome. Enzyme assay settles it.
Distinguishing Features
  • Corneal clouding is characteristically absent in Hunter syndrome and present in MPS I.
  • X-linked inheritance and male-only affected relatives favor Hunter syndrome.
  • Deficient iduronate-2-sulfatase with normal alpha-L-iduronidase is definitive for Hunter syndrome.
Juvenile idiopathic arthritis
Overlapping Features The commonest wrong turn in the attenuated MPS I diagnostic pathway. A child with progressive stiffness of the small joints of the hand, restricted shoulders, and reduced function is a plausible juvenile arthritis referral, and the diagnosis is often held for years. The discriminating observation is that MPS joint disease is not inflammatory: there is stiffness and contracture without joint swelling, without warmth or effusion, and without raised inflammatory markers, and it does not respond to anti-inflammatory therapy.
Distinguishing Features
  • Stiffness and contracture without joint swelling, warmth, or effusion favors MPS I.
  • Normal inflammatory markers with progressive contracture favors MPS I.
  • Corneal clouding, a heart murmur, or dysostosis multiplex on radiographs favors MPS I.
  • Absence of response to anti-inflammatory therapy favors MPS I.
Maroteaux-Lamy syndrome MONDO:0009661
Overlapping Features MPS VI shares preserved intelligence with attenuated MPS I and produces a similar somatic picture with dysostosis multiplex, corneal clouding, valve disease, and carpal tunnel syndrome. It stores dermatan sulfate only, not heparan sulfate, so the urinary GAG pattern differs, and the deficient enzyme is arylsulfatase B.
Distinguishing Features
  • Isolated dermatan sulfate excretion without heparan sulfate favors MPS VI.
  • Deficient arylsulfatase B with normal alpha-L-iduronidase is definitive for MPS VI.

  Naming and entry-boundary rationale, because this is the part of MPS
  nosology most likely to confuse a reader or a curator.

  The eponyms are a historical three-way split of one enzyme deficiency:
  Hurler (severe), Hurler-Scheie (intermediate), Scheie (mildest). Scheie
  syndrome was for a time even numbered as a separate disorder, "MPS V", before
  it was recognised as the mild end of MPS I. Contemporary practice has largely
  abandoned the three-way eponymous split in favour of a two-way clinical one -
  severe (neuronopathic) versus attenuated (non-neuronopathic) - because that is
  the distinction that actually drives the treatment decision, and because the
  Hurler-Scheie/Scheie boundary is not reliably drawable in an individual
  patient. Registry studies and treatment cohorts are now reported as
  severe-versus-attenuated. This entry follows that practice: it is scoped to
  the attenuated arm, and carries Hurler-Scheie and Scheie as subtypes so the
  historical concepts and their MONDO identifiers remain addressable.

  Why this is a separate entry rather than a section of the Hurler syndrome
  entry. The two arms are not the same disease at two dial settings. They differ
  in age at presentation (infancy versus later childhood or adulthood), in which
  organ systems carry the morbidity (CNS and early cardiorespiratory failure
  versus joints, cornea, valves, and spinal cord), in the diagnostic pathway
  (developmental regression and coarse facies versus an orthopaedic or
  rheumatological referral years after symptom onset), and - most consequentially
  - in which of the two disease-modifying therapies is indicated. Newborn
  screening has made this boundary sharper rather than softer: a positive
  screen now forces an explicit severe-versus-attenuated prediction before
  symptoms appear, because that prediction determines whether the child is
  referred for transplantation or started on enzyme replacement therapy.
