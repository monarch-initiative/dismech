Laryngeal Tuberculosis

Infectious Disease MONDO:0005819 Pathograph 28 Show in embeddings browser Tuberculosis Extrapulmonary tuberculosis Laryngitis

Laryngeal tuberculosis is tuberculosis of the larynx and the most common granulomatous disease of that organ. Mycobacterium tuberculosis reaches the laryngeal mucosa either in bacilli-laden sputum from active, often cavitary, pulmonary tuberculosis (secondary laryngeal tuberculosis) or by direct seeding of inhaled bacilli without lung disease (primary laryngeal tuberculosis). In the pre-chemotherapy era it was a late complication of advanced pulmonary disease that mainly affected the posterior larynx. Modern series describe patients with mainly laryngeal symptoms, frequent lesions of the true vocal folds and the anterior larynx, multi-site involvement, and a substantial share with no active lung disease. Caseating granulomatous inflammation produces inflammatory-infiltrative, ulcerative, exophytic or leukoplakic lesions that can mimic laryngeal carcinoma. Hoarseness is the main symptom, with odynophagia, dysphagia, cough and, with extensive disease, dyspnea, stridor and airway obstruction. Diagnosis rests on biopsy histopathology with mycobacterial staining, culture or nucleic acid testing, supported by chest imaging and sputum examination. The disease is highly contagious. Standard antituberculous chemotherapy cures nearly all patients; scarring, vocal fold fixation, persistent dysphonia and laryngeal stenosis are uncommon sequelae.

description: >-
  Laryngeal tuberculosis is tuberculosis of the larynx and the most common
  granulomatous disease of that organ. Mycobacterium tuberculosis reaches the
  laryngeal mucosa either in bacilli-laden sputum from active, often cavitary,
  pulmonary tuberculosis (secondary laryngeal tuberculosis) or by direct
  seeding of inhaled bacilli without lung disease (primary laryngeal
  tuberculosis). In the pre-chemotherapy era it was a late complication of
  advanced pulmonary disease that mainly affected the posterior larynx. Modern
  series describe patients with mainly laryngeal symptoms, frequent lesions of
  the true vocal folds and the anterior larynx, multi-site involvement, and a
  substantial share with no active lung disease. Caseating granulomatous
  inflammation produces inflammatory-infiltrative, ulcerative, exophytic or
  leukoplakic lesions that can mimic laryngeal carcinoma. Hoarseness is the
  main symptom, with odynophagia, dysphagia, cough and, with extensive disease,
  dyspnea, stridor and airway obstruction. Diagnosis rests on biopsy
  histopathology with mycobacterial staining, culture or nucleic acid testing,
  supported by chest imaging and sputum examination. The disease is highly
  contagious. Standard antituberculous chemotherapy cures nearly all patients;
  scarring, vocal fold fixation, persistent dysphonia and laryngeal stenosis
  are uncommon sequelae.

No causal human gene exists (the template lists "not recorded"). The pathogen is the cause. 1. Disease Information

Overview. LTB is extrapulmonary tuberculosis of the larynx. It is the most common granulomatous disease of the larynx. It is rare, cited as about 1% of TB cases or under 1% of extrapulmonary TB, and it often mimics laryngeal cancer. - Migliorelli et al. 2022 (PMID 36541378) state: "Laryngeal tuberculosis (LTB) is a rare manifestation of tuberculosis (TB), accounting for 1% of all TB cases." and "Most cases involved the true vocal folds." These are fragments of the abstract from a search snippet. - The MeSH scope note defines LTB as "Tuberculosis involving the larynx, producing ulceration of the VOCAL CORDS and the LARYNGEAL MUCOSA." (MeSH D014387) - A 2023 case report describes LTB as the most common granulomatous laryngeal disease, usually linked to pulmonary TB but able to present as primary disease (Achache 2023, PMID 38133296; PMC10745871).

2. Etiology

Cause: infection of the laryngeal mucosa by M. tuberculosis. There are two recognised forms: - Secondary LTB. This is the classic form. It comes from bronchogenic spread of bacilli-laden sputum from active pulmonary TB. - Primary LTB. This is isolated laryngeal disease with no pulmonary focus.

Primary cases are increasingly reported. Guan 2022 (PMID 35634235) describes three primary cases in which "The routine workup for pulmonary tuberculosis was unremarkable." Nerurkar 2024 (PMID 38440428) reported 2 primary and 1 secondary case among 890 voice-clinic patients (paraphrased).

Risk factors. The main pooled source is the Rubin 2024 SWiM review of 119 articles (PMID 38238187, paraphrased). - Smoking was reported in 41% of cases. - 20% had prior lung TB. - 18% had immunodepression. - 3% had HIV.

Other risk factors: - Active pulmonary TB. Lung involvement was present in 54% (Rubin 2024) and 56.0% (Gao 2024, n=134, PMID 37979215, paraphrased). In the Teixeira Marques 2024 series (PMID 39111930), all but 1 of 10 patients had active pulmonary TB. - HIV and immunosuppression. These are general TB risk factors. The Pang 2018 head and neck TB literature analysis reports "465 patients were positive according to a HIV test" (PMID 29323108). - Male sex. The Teixeira Marques series had 8 males and 2 females. - High-burden settings. In a prospective South African study, 5 of 80 biopsied adults (6.25%) had LTB (Matimba 2020, PMID 32328767; paraphrased and unverified).

Genetic risk and protective factors: No laryngeal-specific host genetic data were retrieved. General TB-susceptibility genetics were not searched in this session and are not reported here.

Protective factors: - Treatment of pulmonary TB, and the resulting fall in prevalence, is protective at the population level. Secondary LTB made up 15–40% of pulmonary TB cases before antibiotics and fell below 1% with routine antitubercular therapy (review cited at PMC5446342). - No data on BCG protection against laryngeal disease specifically were retrieved.

Gene–environment interactions: No data were retrieved.

6. Mechanism / Pathophysiology

Causal chain. Steps marked [inferred] are extrapolated from general TB biology and were not shown specifically in the larynx.

  1. Initiating infection. Bacilli reach the laryngeal mucosa. There are two branches:
  2. 1a (secondary LTB). Expectorated sputum from active pulmonary (often cavitary) TB is deposited on the posterior glottis and vocal folds. This leads to direct mucosal inoculation. The mechanism is the classic one and is supported by the 54–56% rate of co-existing lung disease (PMIDs 38238187, 37979215).
  3. 1b (primary LTB). Inhaled droplets seed the larynx directly, or bacilli spread by blood or lymph from an occult focus. This leads to isolated laryngeal disease with a negative pulmonary workup (PMID 35634235). [The route is inferred.]
  4. Uptake by macrophages. M. tuberculosis is taken up by and resides in macrophages within the laryngeal lesions. This was demonstrated by immunofluorescence and in situ hybridization in a human biopsy (Achache 2023, PMID 38133296, paraphrased).
  5. Granuloma formation. Infected macrophages trigger cell-mediated immunity, with T-cell recruitment, epithelioid transformation and Langhans giant cells. This produces granulomatous inflammation. [The T-cell steps are inferred from general TB biology; the granuloma itself is shown on histology.]
  6. Caseous necrosis. Central necrosis follows, leading to mucosal ulceration of the vocal folds and laryngeal mucosa (demonstrated histologically, PMID 38133296; MeSH scope note).
  7. Clinical expression. Glottic lesions, oedema and ulceration cause stiffness of the vocal fold cover. This leads to an absent mucosal wave and hoarseness. Supraglottic or epiglottic ulceration leads to odynophagia (PMIDs 35634235, 37979215).
  8. Complications. Two downstream outcomes branch from here:
  9. 6a. Deep or advanced disease leads to impaired fold mobility (6%) and to airway compromise or a need for tracheotomy (1%).
  10. 6b. Healing with fibrosis leads to scar-like mucosa (34.4% at follow-up in Gao) and persistent dysphonia or other laryngeal sequelae (5%, Rubin).
  11. Transmission. Laryngeal disease produces highly infectious aerosols, leading to onward transmission (Braden 1995, PMID 8527545: "The source case was highly infectious; transmission following only a few hours of exposure was documented.").

Checklist coverage: - Cellular processes and immune involvement: granuloma formation, macrophage-resident intracellular bacteria, Th1-type cell-mediated immunity [inferred], and caseous necrosis. - Tissue damage: necrosis, ulceration and fibrosis. - Molecular pathways, proteomics, metabolomics, single-cell data, and other omics: no laryngeal-specific data were retrieved. - Suggested GO labels (IDs unverified): granuloma formation; macrophage activation; response to bacterium; inflammatory response; necrotic cell death; T cell mediated immunity. - Suggested CL labels: macrophage; epithelioid cell; multinucleated giant cell (Langhans); T cell (CD4+); squamous epithelial cell (vocal fold).

7. Anatomical Structures Affected

  • Primary organ: larynx (UBERON label "larynx").
  • Sites:
  • The true vocal folds are the most frequent site. The glottis was involved in 87.3% in Gao; Migliorelli notes "Most cases involved the true vocal folds."
  • Other sites are the false folds, ventricle, epiglottis (Raj 2022, PMID 35131802) and subglottis (PMID 39559153, title only).
  • Suggested UBERON labels: larynx; vocal cord; vestibular fold; epiglottis; laryngeal mucosa.
  • Secondary involvement: lungs, in about 54–56% of cases. Disseminated TB can occur; one of Guan's 3 patients "developed disseminated tuberculosis despite optimal therapy."
  • Body systems: respiratory system (upper and lower airway).
  • Tissue and cell level: laryngeal mucosa (stratified squamous and respiratory epithelium) and lamina propria. The cells involved are macrophages and granuloma cells.
  • Subcellular level: bacteria reside inside macrophages, in the phagosome [inferred]. Suggested GO cellular component label: phagocytic vesicle.
  • Laterality: lesions can be unilateral or bilateral. Raj 2022 describes bilateral true-cord involvement; Guan 2022 describes irregular lesions on one fold.

8. Temporal Development

  • Onset: adults. Case ages range from 24 to 73 years in the retrieved reports (PMIDs 35131802, 37927677, 35634235). The onset is insidious, subacute or chronic.
  • Diagnostic delay: median 3 months (PMID 38238187).
  • Course: without treatment the disease progresses. With standard therapy, symptoms resolve 4–16 weeks after starting treatment (PMID 39111930). No formal staging system exists.
  • Remission: remission is treatment-induced; pooled cure was 99% (PMID 38238187).
  • Critical period: early diagnosis, before biopsy under general anaesthesia, matters for infection control. Teixeira Marques advises a chest X-ray before rigid laryngoscopy for any suspicious laryngeal lesion (PMID 39111930).

10. Diagnostics

  • Laryngoscopy and stroboscopy: irregular, ulcerative, exophytic or oedematous lesions with an absent mucosal wave. The appearance is often taken for cancer; in Migliorelli's review the initial suspicion was laryngeal cancer in 64 cases (snippet; denominator unknown).
  • Biopsy and histopathology (reference standard):
  • Findings are granulomatous inflammation with caseous necrosis, plus Ziehl–Neelsen acid-fast bacilli (PMIDs 38133296, 35131802).
  • Bacteriological confirmation was achieved in only 28% of pooled cases (PMID 38238187).
  • Nerurkar considers excisional biopsy with histology essential in primary LTB (PMID 38440428).
  • Molecular testing:
  • Xpert MTB/RIF on biopsy tissue gave a result within 24 h in Guan 2022.
  • Xpert was positive in only 1 of 5 LTB cases in the South African study (PMID 32328767, unverified summary).
  • Mycobacterial identification on homogenised biopsy speeds diagnosis (Yu 2020, PMID 33336005).
  • Sputum AFB smear and culture: positive mainly in secondary LTB. Sputum tests perform poorly in head and neck TB overall (PMID 31080090).
  • Imaging:
  • Chest CT detects lung involvement better than chest X-ray: 71.2% vs 25.6% positive (PMID 37979215).
  • Laryngeal CT may mimic carcinoma (El Kettani 2010, PMID 20351985).
  • ¹⁸F-FDG PET/CT can show avid uptake that mimics carcinoma (PMID 29889030, title only).
  • Immunological tests: tuberculin skin test or IGRA. The Mantoux test was negative in Guan's primary cases.
  • Genetic, omics and liquid-biopsy testing: not applicable.
  • Differential diagnosis:
  • Laryngeal squamous cell carcinoma, the main mimic.
  • Other granulomatous diseases: sarcoidosis, granulomatosis with polyangiitis, fungal infection (histoplasmosis), syphilis, leprosy.
  • Non-tuberculous laryngeal granulomas (PMID 41001724, title only).
  • Contact granuloma, and laryngitis or leukoplakia.
  • Screening: none for asymptomatic individuals. Contact investigation uses TST or IGRA (see §13).

12. Treatment

  • Pharmacotherapy:
  • Standard first-line regimen is 2 months of isoniazid, rifampicin, pyrazinamide and ethambutol (HRZE), then 4 months of isoniazid and rifampicin (HR). Median treatment duration was 6 months, and a 4-drug regimen was used in 80% of cohorts (PMID 38238187).
  • The case reports used RIPE or HRZE for 6 months (PMIDs 35131802, 37927677).
  • Drug-resistant TB requires WHO regimens for MDR or rifampicin-resistant TB (not retrieved in detail).
  • Suggested NCIT and CHEBI labels: isoniazid; rifampicin; pyrazinamide; ethambutol. NCIT labels: Antitubercular Agent; Directly Observed Therapy.
  • Pharmacogenomics: NAT2 acetylator status affects isoniazid toxicity in general TB treatment. This was not retrieved in this session, so it needs verification.
  • Surgical and interventional:
  • Diagnostic biopsy or microlaryngoscopic excision (PMID 38440428).
  • Tracheotomy for airway obstruction (PMID 28215824).
  • Late phonosurgery or airway surgery for sequelae.
  • Supportive and rehabilitative: voice therapy and voice assessment after treatment (MDVP analysis in PMID 35131802; voice focus in PMID 36541378). No outcome data on speech therapy were retrieved.
  • Advanced or experimental therapies (gene, cell, RNA, immunotherapy): not applicable. No LTB-specific trials or NCT IDs were found.
  • Response: symptoms resolve within 4–16 weeks (PMID 39111930). Lesions showed "almost complete resolution" in Raj 2022 (PMID 35131802). In Gautam 2023 (PMID 37927677), ulcers regressed by 2 months.

13. Prevention

  • Primary prevention:
  • Early detection and treatment of pulmonary TB, which prevents secondary LTB.
  • BCG vaccination, mainly protective against severe childhood TB. No LTB-specific data were retrieved.
  • Smoking cessation (inferred from the 41% smoking rate).
  • HIV control.
  • Infection control:
  • LTB is highly infectious. In Braden 1995, "TST conversions were documented in 23 students; eight had, at most, 5 hours of classroom contact" (PMID 8527545).
  • Airborne precautions are needed for laryngoscopy and biopsy. A decision tree for protecting healthcare workers exists (Roberts 2008, PMID 18596478).
  • Chest imaging before rigid endoscopy is advised (PMID 39111930).
  • Secondary prevention: contact tracing with TST or IGRA, and preventive treatment of contacts with latent infection.
  • Tertiary prevention: completing therapy, and monitoring the airway and voice for stenosis.
  • Genetic counseling: not applicable.

14. Other Species / Natural Disease

  • Animal laryngeal TB: no reports of naturally occurring laryngeal TB in animals were retrieved.
  • Mycobacterium bovis in cattle: causes granulomatous disease, and atypical granulomas have been described in calves (PMC6629060). Laryngeal lesions were not specifically described in that source.
  • Zoonotic potential: M. bovis is a recognised zoonotic member of the M. tuberculosis complex. Its role in laryngeal disease was not documented in the retrieved sources.
  • OMIA: not applicable.

15. Model Organisms

  • Laryngeal-specific models: none were found.
  • General TB models:
  • Guinea pig. Granulomas progress from macrophage aggregates to caseous necrosis with abundant acid-fast bacilli. Unlike in humans, "Multinucleated giant cells are very rare and fibrotic capsules are not formed" (PMC10556493). These data come from lung infection.
  • Mouse, non-human primate and zebrafish (M. marinum) models are standard in TB research. They were not retrieved in this session.
  • Limitations: no model reproduces laryngeal involvement, vocal fold lesions or voice outcomes. Human biopsy studies remain the main evidence for laryngeal pathology (PMID 38133296).
