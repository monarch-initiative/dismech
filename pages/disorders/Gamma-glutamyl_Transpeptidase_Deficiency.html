Gamma-glutamyl Transpeptidase Deficiency

Mendelian MONDO:0009285 Pathograph 20 Show in embeddings browser Inherited Glutathione Metabolism Disease Inborn Error of Metabolism

Gamma-glutamyl transpeptidase (GGT) deficiency, or glutathionuria, is an ultra-rare autosomal recessive inborn error of the gamma-glutamyl cycle in which the extracellular, membrane-anchored enzyme that begins glutathione breakdown is missing. GGT1 hydrolyses or transfers the gamma-glutamyl moiety of glutathione and its S-conjugates at the outer face of the plasma membrane, most abundantly on the brush border of the renal proximal tubule. Without it, glutathione filtered at the glomerulus and exported by tissues cannot be broken down, so it accumulates in plasma and is excreted in urine in gram quantities, together with gamma-glutamylcysteine and cysteine. The same block prevents conversion of leukotriene C4 to leukotriene D4. Diagnosis is biochemical (glutathionuria with very low serum or leukocyte GGT activity); a causal GGT1 variant has been identified in only one family, a homozygous 16.9 kb intragenic deletion. The clinical picture is uncertain. Most of the handful of reported patients were identified because of intellectual disability, developmental delay or behavioural problems, so the reported neurological involvement is subject to strong ascertainment bias, and expert review states that whether it belongs to the disease remains to be established. Glutathione-deficient mouse models are far more severe than any reported patient, and human cellular glutathione is reported to be normal.

GGT1 is an extracellular, plasma-membrane-anchored enzyme that hydrolyses or transfers the gamma-glutamyl moiety of glutathione and other gamma-glutamyl compounds; it catalyses the first step of glutathione degradation. Impaired extracellular glutathione catabolism
Glutathione is exported from cells and filtered at the glomerulus, and is normally degraded at the luminal brush border of the renal proximal tubule, where GGT is concentrated. Without GGT, filtered and circulating glutathione cannot be broken down for recovery of its constituent amino acids. Older work proposed that the gamma-glutamyl cycle mediates amino-acid transport, but GGT-deficient patients have normal plasma amino acids and renal amino-acid reabsorption, and the extracellular hydrolysis of glutathione is now regarded as the enzyme's physiological role.

Diagnosis

1
Biochemical diagnosis of GGT deficiency
Diagnosis rests on two findings: low GGT activity in nucleated cells (leukocytes or cultured skin fibroblasts; erythrocytes cannot be used because they lack the enzyme normally), and high plasma and urinary glutathione. Glutathione is not detected by routine amino-acid screening and must be measured specifically. GGT1 testing can confirm the diagnosis, but only one causal variant has been published.

description: >
  Gamma-glutamyl transpeptidase (GGT) deficiency, or glutathionuria, is an
  ultra-rare autosomal recessive inborn error of the gamma-glutamyl cycle in
  which the extracellular, membrane-anchored enzyme that begins glutathione
  breakdown is missing. GGT1 hydrolyses or transfers the gamma-glutamyl moiety
  of glutathione and its S-conjugates at the outer face of the plasma membrane,
  most abundantly on the brush border of the renal proximal tubule. Without it,
  glutathione filtered at the glomerulus and exported by tissues cannot be
  broken down, so it accumulates in plasma and is excreted in urine in gram
  quantities, together with gamma-glutamylcysteine and cysteine. The same block
  prevents conversion of leukotriene C4 to leukotriene D4. Diagnosis is
  biochemical (glutathionuria with very low serum or leukocyte GGT activity);
  a causal GGT1 variant has been identified in only one family, a homozygous
  16.9 kb intragenic deletion. The clinical picture is uncertain. Most of the
  handful of reported patients were identified because of intellectual
  disability, developmental delay or behavioural problems, so the reported
  neurological involvement is subject to strong ascertainment bias, and expert
  review states that whether it belongs to the disease remains to be
  established. Glutathione-deficient mouse models are far more severe than any
  reported patient, and human cellular glutathione is reported to be normal.

  The patient count is small and partly overlapping across sources: a 2007

  first molecularly confirmed family counts six previously reported patients
  before adding its two siblings. Most published patients were diagnosed

  related GGT genes. No specific treatment has been recommended.

- name: Impaired extracellular glutathione catabolism
  biological_scale: CELLULAR
  description: >
    Glutathione is exported from cells and filtered at the glomerulus, and is
    normally degraded at the luminal brush border of the renal proximal tubule,
    where GGT is concentrated. Without GGT, filtered and circulating glutathione

    work proposed that the gamma-glutamyl cycle mediates amino-acid transport,
    but GGT-deficient patients have normal plasma amino acids and renal
    amino-acid reabsorption, and the extracellular hydrolysis of glutathione is
    now regarded as the enzyme's physiological role.

Gamma-glutamyl Transpeptidase Deficiency (Glutathionuria): Comprehensive Disease Characteristics Report

Disease: Gamma-glutamyl Transpeptidase Deficiency (Glutathionuria) MONDO ID: MONDO:0009285 · OMIM: 231950 · Category: Mendelian (autosomal recessive inborn error of metabolism) Report date: 2026-10-15

Summary

Gamma-glutamyl transpeptidase (GGT) deficiency, historically termed glutathionuria, is an ultra-rare autosomal recessive inborn error of the γ-glutamyl (glutathione) cycle caused by biallelic loss-of-function variants in GGT1 on chromosome 22q11.23. The disease was described biochemically in the 1970s–1980s but its molecular cause was only proven in 2018, when whole-genome sequencing of two affected siblings revealed a 16.9 kb homozygous intragenic deletion in GGT1, with the parents and a healthy sibling confirmed as carriers/wild-type — providing the first direct genetic evidence of autosomal recessive inheritance PMID: 29483667.

The core biochemical defect is a failure of extracellular glutathione (GSH) catabolism. GGT is a membrane-bound ectoenzyme on the apical (brush-border) surface of transport/secretory epithelia — most importantly the renal proximal tubule — where it initiates breakdown of filtered and plasma glutathione and salvages cysteine. When the enzyme is absent, glutathione cannot be reclaimed and is lost in the urine. This produces the diagnostic hallmark of glutathionuria plus glutathionemia, accompanied by elevated plasma γ-glutamylcysteine, cysteine and cysteinylglycine, with serum/tissue GGT activity that is very low or undetectable. Notably, plasma amino acid levels and renal amino acid reabsorption remain normal, demonstrating that the γ-glutamyl cycle is not essential for amino acid transport.

The human clinical phenotype is remarkably mild relative to the dramatic biochemical abnormality: fewer than ~8 patients have ever been reported, and the dominant features are neurological/cognitive (intellectual disability, developmental delay, behavioral problems), with variable, generally mild, expressivity. By contrast, Ggt1-knockout and ENU-mutant mice display a severe multisystem phenotype — growth failure, cataracts, lethargy, infertility, osteopenia and shortened lifespan — all traced to systemic cysteine/GSH depletion and all reversible with cysteine repletion (N-acetylcysteine or the cysteine prodrug L-2-oxothiazolidine-4-carboxylate, OTZ). The species discrepancy likely reflects paralog redundancy (the human GGT family has 13 homologous genes) and differences in GSH handling. No approved disease-specific therapy exists for humans; management is supportive, and prevention relies on genetic counseling and cascade testing. Mechanistic causality (cysteine depletion as the proximate cause) is proven only in the mouse model and is inferred, but not formally trialed, in humans.

The clinical and biochemical entity is defined by massive urinary and plasma glutathione accumulation ("glutathionuria" / "glutathionemia") in the setting of absent GGT enzyme activity.

2. Etiology

Primary cause — genetic. The disease is caused by biallelic loss-of-function variants in GGT1, inherited in an autosomal recessive pattern. The first molecular proof was a 16.9 kb homozygous intragenic deletion in GGT1 with a 13 bp insertion at the breakpoint identified by whole-genome sequencing in two affected siblings: "Whole-genome sequencing revealed the presence of a 16.9 kb homozygous deletion in GGT1, one of the genes encoding enzymes with γ-glutamyl transpeptidase activity in the human genome" PMID: 29483667. Earlier case series inferred autosomal recessive inheritance from reduced fibroblast GGT activity in clinically normal, presumably heterozygous, parents PMID: 6118466; PMID: 238530.

Genetic risk factors. The sole established genetic risk factor is inheritance of two pathogenic GGT1 alleles. No susceptibility loci or modifier genes have been formally established in humans (the ultra-rare case count precludes association studies). gnomAD constraint metrics indicate GGT1 is moderately intolerant to heterozygous LOF (see Section 4), yet heterozygous carriers are clinically healthy, consistent with recessive disease.

Environmental risk factors. None established. There is no evidence that toxins, lifestyle, occupational exposure, age or sex trigger or modify the primary Mendelian disease. (Separately, acquired/pharmacologic GGT inhibition can phenocopy the biochemistry: administration of γ-glutamyl amino acids or transpeptidase inhibitors induces glutathionuria in mice — relevant mechanistically but not an etiology of the inherited disease PMID: 2873573; PMID: 34150.)

Protective factors. None characterized in humans. In mice, provision of an alternative cysteine source (N-acetylcysteine, OTZ) is "protective" against the downstream consequences — not a protection against the genetic lesion itself but a bypass of its metabolic consequence.

Gene–environment interactions. Not characterized for this Mendelian disorder. Mechanistically, dietary cysteine/protein intake would be expected to modulate the severity of cysteine deficiency (as shown pharmacologically in mice), but this interaction has not been formally studied in human patients.

4. Genetic / Molecular Information

Causal gene. GGT1 (HGNC:4250; NCBI Gene 2678; Ensembl ENSG00000100031), chromosome 22q11.23, GRCh38 chr22:24,594,811–24,629,005; canonical transcript ENST00000400382.

Pathogenic variants. - The index molecularly proven lesion is a 16.9 kb homozygous intragenic deletion (structural/copy-number variant) with a 13 bp insertion at the breakpoint — a loss-of-function allele requiring WGS/CNV analysis to detect (standard exome would likely miss it) PMID: 29483667. - Variant classification: pathogenic (biallelic LOF causing a loss-of-function disease). - Variant types expected across the disorder: structural deletions, and (by inference) nonsense/frameshift/splice/missense LOF alleles. - Somatic vs germline: germline. - Functional consequence: loss of function (absent enzyme activity).

Protein and family. GGT belongs to the N-terminal nucleophile (Ntn) hydrolase superfamily. The human GGT family comprises 13 homologous genes (GGT1–7 and GGTLC1–3) on chromosomes 20 and 22: "The human GGT family comprises 13 homologous genes (e.g., GGT1-7 and GGTLC1-3) localized on chromosomes 20 and 22" PMID: 41316810 — a likely explanation for residual activity and the comparatively mild human phenotype.

6. Mechanism / Pathophysiology

Ordered causal chain

  1. Biallelic loss-of-function variants in GGT1 (e.g., 16.9 kb homozygous deletion) → loss of functional GGT protein on epithelial brush-border membranes (demonstrated in humans; PMID: 29483667).
  2. Absent membrane-bound GGT → failure to hydrolyze extracellular/filtered glutathione at the renal proximal tubule brush border and other epithelia (demonstrated; PMID: 42531).
  3. Failure of GSH breakdown → glutathione is not reclaimed and is lost in urine → glutathionuria + glutathionemia, with accumulation of γ-glutamylcysteine, cysteine and cysteinylglycine in plasma (demonstrated in humans; PMID: 15702405).
  4. Loss of extracellular GSH → impaired cysteine salvage (GGT normally liberates cysteine for de novo intracellular GSH/protein synthesis) → intracellular cysteine deficiency (proven in mouse; inferred in human; PMID: 11089562).

Branch A (mouse, severe): Tissue cysteine/GSH depletion → growth failure, cataracts, lethargy, shortened lifespan; reproductive failure via suppressed IGF-1 and FSH; skeletal osteopenia/dwarfism via chondrocyte proliferation defect and osteoblast/osteoclast imbalance → all reversible with N-acetylcysteine / OTZ (proven in mouse; PMID: 12757858; PMID: 11089562; PMID: 12810527).

9. Inheritance and Population

Epidemiology. Ultra-rare. Fewer than ~8 patients have ever been reported worldwide; precise prevalence/incidence are unknown and almost certainly underestimated. "this condition has probably been underdiagnosed due the difficulty to routinely analyze glutathione in clinical samples" PMID: 29483667.

Genetic parameters. - Inheritance pattern: Autosomal recessive, now directly proven by segregation in a confirmed family: "genetic characterization of the patients' parents and a healthy sibling has provided direct genetic evidence regarding the autosomal recessive nature of this disease" PMID: 29483667. - Penetrance/expressivity: The biochemical phenotype appears fully penetrant; the neurological phenotype shows variable expressivity and uncertain penetrance given tiny numbers. More broadly for γ-glutamyl cycle disorders: "All disorders are very rare and inherited in an autosomal recessive manner. Most of the mutations are leaky so that many patients have residual enzyme activity" PMID: 17397529. - Genetic anticipation: Not applicable (not a repeat-expansion disorder). - Germline mosaicism / founder effects: None documented. - Consanguinity: Homozygosity for a structural deletion in the index family is consistent with a role for shared ancestry/consanguinity, as is typical of rare AR disease, though not explicitly characterized. - Carrier frequency: Unknown; cannot be reliably estimated from gnomAD for a disease defined largely by structural variants.

Population demographics. No ethnic predilection established (reported cases include European and Japanese patients PMID: 15702405). No defined geographic clustering, sex ratio, or age distribution given the few cases.

10. Diagnostics

Diagnostic approach. Diagnosis rests on the biochemical demonstration of glutathionuria + glutathionemia together with very low/absent serum and tissue GGT activity (<1 IU/L), confirmed by GGT1 sequencing PMID: 15702405; PMID: 6118466.

Clinical/laboratory tests. - Quantitative urine and plasma glutathione (markedly elevated), plus γ-glutamylcysteine, cysteine, cysteinylglycine. "Glutathionaemia and glutathionuria were evident in both brothers. The analyses of sulphydryl compounds in the plasma ... and urine indicated high concentrations of glutathione, gamma-glutamylcysteine, cysteine and cysteinylglycine" PMID: 15702405. - Serum GGT enzyme activity (very low/undetectable). LOINC: serum GGT activity (e.g., LOINC 2324-2). - Diagnostic pitfall: on routine amino-acid chromatography, thiol compounds produce "a large peak in the citrulline position," which can mislead — "Urine amino acid analysis on an automatic analyser showed a slightly increased excretion of cystine and a large peak in the citrulline position due, at least in part, to thio-compounds" PMID: 15702405.

Genetic testing. GGT1 sequencing is confirmatory. Because the index lesion was a 16.9 kb structural deletion, WGS or CNV/deletion-duplication analysis may be required — standard whole-exome sequencing can miss such events PMID: 29483667. Single-gene testing or a metabolic gene panel including GGT1 is appropriate; chromosomal microarray may detect larger deletions.

Omics-based diagnostics. Targeted metabolomics (thiol/glutathione quantification by HPLC/LC-MS) is effectively the functional diagnostic test; no transcriptomic/proteomic diagnostic is established.

Clinical criteria / differential diagnosis. No formal consensus diagnostic criteria exist. Differential diagnosis includes other γ-glutamyl cycle / glutathione-metabolism disorders (e.g., glutathione synthetase deficiency / 5-oxoprolinuria, γ-glutamylcysteine synthetase deficiency) — distinguished by their specific metabolite patterns and enzyme assays PMID: 17397529. The citrulline-position peak also raises a spurious concern for a urea-cycle abnormality that must be excluded.

Screening. Not included in standard newborn screening because glutathione is not measured in routine metabolic panels PMID: 29483667. Cascade/carrier testing is feasible once a family's GGT1 variant is known.

11. Outcome / Prognosis

  • Survival/mortality: No evidence of reduced survival in reported human patients; the biochemical abnormality appears compatible with normal life span. (Mouse knockouts, by contrast, have shortened life spans.)
  • Morbidity/function: Driven by the neurocognitive phenotype — intellectual disability and behavioral difficulties account for functional impact. No validated QoL data.
  • Disease course/complications: Chronic and apparently non-progressive; no characteristic organ-failure complications documented in humans.
  • Recovery potential: The genetic/biochemical defect is permanent. Downstream metabolic consequences are, in principle, modifiable (mouse data), but human cognitive outcomes are not known to be reversible.
  • Prognostic factors/biomarkers: None validated. Degree of intellectual disability is the main determinant of outcome in reported cases; true genotype–phenotype correlations cannot be drawn from <8 patients.

12. Treatment

There is no approved disease-specific pharmacotherapy; human management is supportive. No targeted, gene, cell, RNA, immunologic or surgical therapy exists.

Rational (mechanism-based, mouse-validated) strategy — cysteine repletion. In Ggt-deficient mice, supplying an alternative cysteine source corrects the downstream phenotype: - N-acetylcysteine (NAC) restores gonad size, hormone levels and fertility, and ameliorates skeletal abnormalities: "N-acetylcysteine treatment of GGT-/- mice ameliorates their skeletal abnormalities by normalizing chondrocytes proliferation and osteoblastic function" PMID: 12810527; reproductive rescue PMID: 11089562. Suggested NCIT: Acetylcysteine (NCIT:C28159). - L-2-oxothiazolidine-4-carboxylate (OTZ / procysteine), a cysteine prodrug, partially restores liver GSH and improves growth/behavior in mice PMID: 12757858.

These approaches are not formally trialed in human patients — they represent a biologically rational but unproven therapeutic avenue for humans.

Supportive/rehabilitative care. Developmental, educational and behavioral support for intellectual disability and behavioral problems, as for other causes of neurodevelopmental impairment.

Pharmacogenomics / personalized medicine: Not applicable beyond genotype-confirmed diagnosis guiding counseling.

13. Prevention

  • Primary prevention: Not possible for a Mendelian disorder; relies on genetic counseling and reproductive options for at-risk couples.
  • Secondary prevention / early detection: Not currently part of newborn screening (glutathione is not routinely measured) PMID: 29483667. Targeted glutathione measurement should be considered in unexplained intellectual disability with abnormal amino-acid chromatography (the citrulline-position pitfall).
  • Tertiary prevention: Mechanistically, cysteine repletion (NAC/OTZ) could in theory prevent downstream metabolic complications, extrapolating from mouse data; unproven in humans.
  • Genetic screening/counseling: Once a family's GGT1 variant(s) are known, carrier testing, cascade screening, prenatal and preimplantation genetic testing are feasible. Genetic counseling (25% recurrence risk per pregnancy for carrier couples) is the mainstay of prevention.
  • Immunization / public-health / environmental interventions: Not applicable.

Principal models — mouse. Two mouse models are the main disease models: 1. ENU-induced GGTenu1 mutant, discovered via aminoaciduria screening: "A mouse mutant with glutathionuria was discovered by screening for amino acidurias in the progeny of ethylnitrosourea-mutagenized mice" PMID: 9139708. 2. Model characteristics / phenotype recapitulation. Both reproduce the biochemistry faithfully: autosomal-recessive glutathionuria/glutathionemia, reduced liver GSH. Additional (more severe than human) features: growth failure, cataracts, coat-color defects, lethargy, infertility, osteopenia/dwarfism, and shortened life span PMID: 9139708; PMID: 12757858. Reproductive failure is attributed to intracellular cysteine deficiency: mice are "growth retarded as a result of cysteine deficiency secondary to excessive glutathione excretion in urine" PMID: 11089562.

Model limitations. The mouse is more severe than human disease and does not model the predominantly mild neurocognitive human phenotype — a discrepancy attributed to paralog redundancy and species GSH-handling differences.

Applications. The models established the proximate mechanism (tissue GSH/cysteine deficiency) and therapeutic reversibility (NAC/OTZ), underpinning the rational treatment hypothesis for humans.

Mechanistic Model / Interpretation

The disease is best understood as a failure of the salvage arm of the glutathione (γ-glutamyl) cycle. Glutathione synthesized intracellularly (predominantly in liver) is exported to plasma and filtered at the glomerulus; the renal proximal-tubule brush-border GGT normally hydrolyzes this extracellular GSH, releasing amino acids (notably cysteine) for reuptake and reuse. When GGT1 is lost, this salvage step fails: glutathione cannot be reclaimed and spills into urine (glutathionuria) while accumulating in plasma (glutathionemia), and the cell is deprived of recovered cysteine.

Whether that cysteine deprivation becomes clinically significant depends on how much redundant/alternative cysteine supply remains. In mice — with the sharpest metabolic dependence and (apparently) less effective buffering — systemic cysteine/GSH depletion produces a severe, multisystem, life-shortening phenotype that is fully bypassable by feeding cysteine (NAC) or a cysteine prodrug (OTZ). In humans, the presence of 13 GGT-family paralogs plus dietary cysteine appears to buffer systemic cysteine supply, so the phenotype is limited to a mild, chiefly neurocognitive presentation. This two-branch model (identical upstream lesion; divergent downstream severity set by cysteine-supply redundancy) reconciles the dramatic human biochemistry with the surprisingly benign human clinical course, and it directly motivates cysteine-repletion therapy as the rational — though still unproven in humans — intervention.

Consistency. The findings are internally consistent: physiologic localization studies (brush border; PMID: 42531) explain the urinary-loss phenotype; pharmacologic inhibition experiments (PMID: 34150; PMID: 2873573) phenocopy the inherited defect; mouse genetics (PMID: 11089562; PMID: 12757858; PMID: 12810527) establish causality and reversibility; and human genetics (PMID: 29483667) confirm the gene and inheritance. Limitations and Knowledge Gaps

  1. Tiny human sample (<8 patients): prevalence, penetrance, expressivity, sex ratio, and genotype–phenotype correlations cannot be reliably estimated.
  2. Mechanism in humans is inferred: cysteine depletion as the proximate cause and NAC/OTZ reversibility are proven only in mice; never formally demonstrated or trialed in human patients.
  3. No validated biomarkers of prognosis and no natural-history studies.
  4. Causal spectrum incomplete: only a structural deletion is molecularly proven; the full allelic spectrum (point mutations) and carrier frequency remain undefined; structural variants may be missed by standard exome sequencing.
  5. Species discrepancy unresolved at the molecular level: the paralog-redundancy explanation is plausible but not directly tested.
  6. No disease-specific therapy is approved or trialed in humans.
  7. Under-ascertainment: glutathione is not in standard screens, so the true disease burden is unknown.
