Hypermobile Ehlers-Danlos Syndrome <-> Myalgic Encephalomyelitis Chronic Fatigue Syndrome

Comorbidity UNKNOWN CANDIDATE
A clinical overlap between hypermobile Ehlers-Danlos syndrome (hEDS) and myalgic encephalomyelitis/chronic fatigue syndrome (ME/CFS) is widely described, and chronic fatigue is a major feature of hEDS. Most of the quantitative evidence, however, measures generalized joint hypermobility by Beighton score, not clinically diagnosed hEDS. Beighton-defined hypermobility is common in the general population and is a much broader phenotype than hEDS, and in a chronic-fatigue clinic the Beighton score agreed poorly with clinical assessment of joint laxity (kappa about 0.4; PMID:34957987). With that caveat, two case-control studies found joint hypermobility more often in CFS than in controls (20.6% against 4.3% in adults; 60% against 24% in children, OR 3.5), and a meta-analysis of functional somatic syndromes including CFS found an overall OR of 3.27 while rating most studies fair to poor. A Dutch adolescent study found no difference in Beighton score or collagen biochemistry between CFS and controls, though CFS patients had more extensible skin. In a registry of 815 people with ME/CFS, the 15.5% who were hypermobile were more likely to report EDS and POTS and had worse quality of life. A US pediatric cohort, by contrast, found no difference in ME/CFS presentation by hypermobility status. The only direct hEDS-ME/CFS data are a case series of 12 adolescents with CFS and EDS (6 hypermobile type), all with orthostatic intolerance. Directionality is UNKNOWN. No ICEES KG signal is possible, because hEDS (MONDO:0007523) is not a node in the ICEES KG 8-20-2024 snapshot.

Hypotheses

Hypothesis: laxity of connective tissue in the walls of dependent veins lets them distend excessively under ordinary hydrostatic pressure, so blood pools on standing. The resulting orthostatic intolerance (postural tachycardia or neurally mediated hypotension) contributes to the fatigue and exercise intolerance that lead to an ME/CFS diagnosis. This was proposed from a small case series. It has not been tested experimentally, and it would explain only the subset of ME/CFS with a connective-tissue phenotype and orthostatic intolerance.
PMID:10518084 (SUPPORT)
Source: HUMAN_CLINICAL
"Twelve patients (11 female), median age 15.5 years, met diagnostic criteria for CFS and EDS, and all had either postural tachycardia or neurally mediated hypotension in response to orthostatic stress. Six had classical-type EDS and 6 had hypermobile-type EDS."
A case series in which every adolescent with both CFS and EDS (half of them hypermobile type) had orthostatic intolerance, the observation the venous-pooling hypothesis was proposed to explain.
PMID:10518084 (SUPPORT, indirect, primary result)
Source: HUMAN_CLINICAL
"We propose that the occurrence of these syndromes together can be attributed to the abnormal connective tissue in dependent blood vessels of those with EDS, which permits veins to distend excessively in response to ordinary hydrostatic pressures."
The authors' proposed mechanism, stated as a proposal rather than demonstrated, linking EDS connective tissue to venous pooling.
Pathophysiology:
Venous wall laxity and orthostatic venous pooling: Proposed (not experimentally confirmed) mechanism: abnormal connective tissue in dependent veins allows excessive distension and pooling on standing, reducing venous return and producing orthostatic intolerance.
No biological process is bound. The cited proposal (PMID:10518084) is passive distension of a connective-tissue-abnormal vein wall, not reduced smooth-muscle constriction, so GO:0042310 vasoconstriction (DECREASED) would assert a mechanism the source does not make. OLS searches of GO for "vein distension" and "blood vessel wall compliance" returned no terms; "vein" returned only vein smooth muscle contraction and its regulation (GO:0014826, GO:0062086-GO:0062088), which are active smooth-muscle processes, plus morphogenesis and insect wing-vein terms.
