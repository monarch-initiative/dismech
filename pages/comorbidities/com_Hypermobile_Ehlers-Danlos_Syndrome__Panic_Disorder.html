Hypermobile Ehlers-Danlos Syndrome <-> Panic Disorder

Comorbidity A_BEFORE_B CANDIDATE
Panic disorder/agoraphobia is the single anxiety disorder most consistently and specifically associated with joint hypermobility across a body of case-control and cohort literature spanning three decades, largely from one research group (Bulbena, Martín-Santos and colleagues, Barcelona). A 1993 case-control study found panic disorder, agoraphobia, and simple phobia -- but not generalized anxiety disorder, dysthymic disorder, or major depression -- were specifically elevated in joint hypermobility syndrome (age- and sex-adjusted OR 10.7). A 1998 case-control study replicated this in patients newly diagnosed with panic disorder/agoraphobia (67.7% had joint hypermobility syndrome versus 10.1%-12.5% of controls). A 2010 case-control study replicated the association against three separate control groups (psychiatric, fibromyalgia, and healthy). A 2011 prospective 15-year cohort study established temporal precedence: joint hypermobility syndrome assessed at baseline (ages 16-20) predicted a markedly higher cumulative incidence of panic/agoraphobia at follow-up (relative risk 22.3). A 2012 systematic review of 17 studies concluded panic disorder/agoraphobia was the anxiety disorder most consistently linked to joint hypermobility across the literature. These studies predate the 2017 hEDS diagnostic criteria and use Beighton-criteria "joint hypermobility syndrome" (JHS), the diagnostic predecessor that was folded into the hypermobility spectrum disorder/hEDS nosology; they are treated here as the closest available literature for the current hEDS diagnosis. Directionality is recorded as A_BEFORE_B on the strength of the 2011 prospective cohort, in contrast to most other hEDS comorbidity entries in this KB where only cross-sectional evidence exists. No ICEES KG signal exists for this pair: hEDS (MONDO:0007523) is not a node in the ICEES KG snapshot at all (confirmed while curating the hEDS-POTS comorbidity entry).

Hypotheses

Hypothesis (the "neurovisceral phenotype" model, developed substantially around this specific pairing): connective-tissue laxity in hEDS is accompanied by altered autonomic/interoceptive signaling and heightened amygdala and insular cortex reactivity to threat-related stimuli. Because panic disorder is itself centrally defined by recurrent, acute misinterpretation of interoceptive (bodily) signals as threatening, this constitutional interoceptive/autonomic difference is proposed to lower the threshold for panic attacks specifically, more than for anxiety disorders without a prominent interoceptive-misappraisal component. The precise causal chain from connective-tissue collagen abnormality to altered central threat processing is not established.
PMID:20963520 (SUPPORT, review synthesis)
Source: HUMAN_CLINICAL
"the most significant and important association between joint hypermobility syndrome (JHS) and any other disorder from a clinical point of view is with panic disorder"
A dedicated review of the joint-hypermobility/anxiety literature singles out panic disorder as the specific, clinically significant association, distinguishing it from anxiety disorders in general.
PMID:25713509 (SUPPORT, indirect, review synthesis)
Source: HUMAN_CLINICAL
"In a recent functional neuroimaging study of healthy volunteers, hypermobile, compared to non-hypermobile, participants displayed heightened neural reactivity to sad and angry scenes within brain regions implicated in anxious feeling states, notably insular cortex (Mallorqui-Bague et al., 2014)."
The structural/functional neuroimaging correlate proposed to underlie the hypermobility-anxiety association generally, and cited here as the basis for the panic-specific interoceptive-threshold hypothesis.
PMID:8464956 (SUPPORT, indirect, primary result)
Source: HUMAN_CLINICAL
"DSM-III-R diagnoses of panic disorder, agoraphobia, and simple phobia, but not generalized anxiety disorder, dysthymic disorder, or major depression were found to be highly associated with JHS (age- and sex-adjusted odds ratio = 10.7)."
The founding case-control study establishes that the association is specific to panic-spectrum and phobic disorders and explicitly does not extend to generalized anxiety disorder or depression, supporting a mechanism tied to acute/interoceptive threat response rather than anxiety or mood disturbance in general.
Pathophysiology:
Heightened Amygdala and Insular Threat Reactivity: Functional neuroimaging in hypermobile individuals shows heightened amygdala and insular cortex reactivity to threat-related emotional stimuli, proposed as the neural substrate underlying the association between joint hypermobility and anxiety/panic. A separate, more speculative pathway proposed in the same source involves a perturbation of autonomic afferent feedback, distinct from amygdala/insular reactivity itself and not independently evidenced here.

Source: HUMAN_CLINICAL
"These neuroimaging data implicate the amygdala and insular cortices as the most likely neural substrate underlying the association between of hypermobility, clinical anxiety and psychosomatic disorders."
The review's own synthesis naming the amygdala and insula as the proposed neural substrate for the hypermobility-anxiety association.
PMID:25713509 (SUPPORT, indirect, review synthesis)
