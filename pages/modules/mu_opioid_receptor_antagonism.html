Mu-Opioid Receptor Antagonism Module

Toxicology Pharmacology Neuroscience Module mu_opioid_receptor_antagonism 3 disorders Pathograph 16
A conserved drug-mechanism module for competitive antagonism at the mu-opioid receptor (MOR, OPRM1), the shared mechanism of the overdose-reversal agents (naloxone, nalmefene), the antagonist maintenance treatment for opioid use disorder (extended-release naltrexone), and the peripherally acting mu-opioid receptor antagonists used for opioid-induced constipation (methylnaltrexone, naloxegol, naldemedine). An antagonist occupies the orthosteric site without activating the receptor and, because the competition is surmountable in both directions, displaces or prevents agonist binding in proportion to the relative concentrations and receptor affinities of the two drugs. The rate-limiting step and key conformance target is the resulting loss of Gi/o signalling: adenylyl cyclase inhibition, GIRK current and presynaptic calcium channel inhibition all fall away, so the hyperpolarization and reduced transmitter release that is the central effector of mu_opioid_receptor_agonism is withdrawn and the receptor-expressing neuron returns to, or exceeds, its prior excitability. Four branches follow, and they are not a simple inversion of the agonism module's four: restoration of brainstem inspiratory rhythm (reversal of opioid-induced respiratory depression, limited by naloxone's short duration of action relative to long-acting opioids); noradrenergic rebound in the locus coeruleus on a background of adenylyl cyclase superactivation (precipitated withdrawal, a toxicity of the same drug action); blockade of opioid and endogenous-opioid reward signalling (relapse prevention in opioid use disorder, and reduced alcohol-induced reward in alcohol use disorder); and blockade of enteric mu-opioid receptors by an antagonist excluded from the brain (relief of opioid-induced constipation without loss of central analgesia). Drug-target pattern: a treatment points at the antagonist-occupancy trigger node with treatment_effect ACTIVATES, and what distinguishes the treatments on that one node is pharmacokinetic and distributional rather than pharmacodynamic - duration of receptor occupancy for the reversal agents against the maintenance antagonist, and blood-brain-barrier exclusion for the peripherally restricted agents. Complementary to, and deliberately not overlapping with, mu_opioid_receptor_agonism (the same receptor driven in the opposite direction; partial agonists such as buprenorphine belong there, not here) and addiction_cycle (which models the neurocircuitry of addiction downstream of mesolimbic dopamine release and carries its own opioid-antagonist treatment entry for alcohol use disorder and gambling disorder).

Notes

This is a drug-mechanism module, not a disease. Do not create a generic "Opioid Antagonist Effect" or "Opioid Withdrawal" Disease entry from it. The disease entries are the ones that conform: Acute_Opioid_Poisoning on the respiratory-restoration branch, Opioid_Use_Disorder on the reward-blockade and precipitated-withdrawal branches, Alcohol_Use_Disorder on the endogenous-opioid arm of the reward branch, and any entry whose treatments include a peripherally acting antagonist on the enteric branch. SCOPE. The module covers competitive antagonism at the mu-opioid receptor only. Agonism at the same receptor, including the partial agonist buprenorphine with its respiratory ceiling, is mu_opioid_receptor_agonism; the decision to model the two directions as separate modules was taken because the consequence chains differ rather than simply inverting - precipitated withdrawal and renarcotization have no counterpart in the agonism module, and the peripherally restricted agents separate a branch that no agonist separates. Kappa- and delta-receptor pharmacology, the mixed agonist-antagonists, naloxone's non-opioid actions, and the GABAergic and glutamatergic physiology of spontaneous opioid withdrawal outside the noradrenergic arm are out of scope and stay on the disorder entries. INVERSE NODES. Five nodes here are the explicit inverse of a node in mu_opioid_receptor_agonism and say so in their own descriptions, one for one along the trunk and on two of the branches: - "Loss of Gi/o Coupling and Relief of Adenylyl Cyclase Inhibition" inverts "Gi/o Coupling and Adenylyl Cyclase Inhibition". - "GIRK Closure and Recovery of Presynaptic Calcium Current" inverts "G Beta-Gamma Gating of GIRK Current and Calcium Channel Inhibition", and binds the same two molecular-function terms with the modifiers reversed. - "Restored Neuronal Excitability" inverts "Neuronal Hyperpolarization and Reduced Neurotransmitter Release"; both are the central effector and key conformance target of their module. - "Brainstem Respiratory Rhythm Restoration" inverts "Brainstem Respiratory Rhythm Depression". - "Enteric Mu-Opioid Receptor Blockade and Restored Propulsive Motility" inverts "Enteric Neuron Inhibition and Slowed Propulsive Motility". The agonism module's trunk is three nodes and so is this one, so a conforming entry that models both directions should point at the matching pair at each step rather than reusing one node for both. The two branches with no inverse here are the agonism module's analgesia branch, which antagonism does not separately model because loss of analgesia is simply the agonism branch withdrawn, and its desensitization branch, which is an adaptation to continued agonist exposure rather than anything an antagonist produces. BOUNDARY WITH addiction_cycle. The reward branch here stops at blockade of the dopamine-reinforcement signal, which is the receptor-level pharmacology upstream of the addiction_cycle trigger node "Acute Mesolimbic Dopamine Reinforcement". That module already carries an "Opioid Receptor Antagonists" treatment entry pointing at its own trigger node for alcohol use disorder and gambling disorder; this module records why the antagonist reaches that node, and does not re-model incentive salience, the ventral-to-dorsal striatal shift, or the extended-amygdala stress systems. Node term-binding follows module convention (GO, CL and UBERON terms only; the drug identities live on the treatments' therapeutic_agent as CHEBI ids). The receptor gene OPRM1 is bound on the trigger node, reusing the identifier mu_opioid_receptor_agonism binds. Two consequence nodes carry no biological_processes term: GO has no process for an opioid withdrawal syndrome and none for drug reinforcement or relapse. Searches run against the configured GO source immediately before committing this file were `runoak -i ols:go search "withdrawal"` (no results), `runoak -i ols:go search "reward"` (no results) and `runoak -i ols:go search "drug-seeking"` (no results); the nearest hits for the behavioural arm, `GO:0043278` response to morphine and `GO:1990708` conditioned place preference, are deliberately not bound anywhere in this module: neither names the loss of a drug effect that the reward branch asserts, and the branch carries `GO:0014046` dopamine secretion plus prose instead. The locus coeruleus is bound with UBERON's own spelling, `UBERON:0002148` locus ceruleus. WHAT THIS MODULE DOES NOT CLAIM. The respiratory branch is reversible but the reversal is not durable: naloxone's duration of effect is shorter than that of many opioid agonists, so a single dose can be followed by renarcotization, and illicitly manufactured fentanyl analogs may need higher or repeated dosing. Nalmefene is included as a higher-affinity, longer-acting reversal agent, but whether that is an advantage in overdose is contested: its intranasal approval rests on a bridge to the injectable formulation and a healthy-volunteer pharmacodynamic study rather than a randomized efficacy trial in overdose, and two toxicology societies recommend against replacing naloxone with it because the longer occupancy may lengthen precipitated withdrawal. Both sides are recorded on its treatment entry, including a REFUTE item; the module itself takes no position. Precipitated withdrawal is modelled as a toxicity of the same receptor action, not as a separate drug effect, and the module makes no claim that it can be dissociated from reversal efficacy - that is the open knowledge gap below.

Competitive Mu-Opioid Receptor Antagonist Occupancy
trigger
The trigger and the drug-target node, and the inverse of the agonist-binding trigger of mu_opioid_receptor_agonism. An antagonist - naloxone, nalmefene, naltrexone, or a peripherally restricted agent - occupies the orthosteric site of the mu-opioid receptor encoded by OPRM1 without stabilizing an active receptor conformation, so it displaces an agonist already bound and prevents further agonist binding. The competition is surmountable in both directions, which is the whole clinical character of the node: an agonist of high receptor affinity needs a higher antagonist concentration or a continuous infusion before reversal is achieved, and a further agonist dose can reclaim the receptor. Antagonists differ on this node in pharmacokinetics and distribution rather than in receptor mechanism - how long occupancy lasts, and whether the drug enters the brain at all.

Loss of Gi/o Coupling and Relief of Adenylyl Cyclase Inhibition
amplifier
The first amplifier, and the inverse of "Gi/o Coupling and Adenylyl Cyclase Inhibition" in mu_opioid_receptor_agonism. An occupied but unactivated receptor no longer engages inhibitory Gi/o heterotrimers, so the G alpha-i subunit is not dissociated, adenylyl cyclase is released from inhibition and cyclic AMP recovers. The step is quantitatively competitive rather than all-or-none: an antagonist's potency against agonist-driven cAMP inhibition is measured as a concentration producing half-maximal relief, and that concentration rises steeply with the affinity and efficacy of the agonist it has to displace - the cell-level form of the surmountable competition on the trigger node. In a neuron adapted to chronic agonist exposure the cyclase this node releases has itself been compensatorily upregulated, which is why the withdrawal branch downstream is a rebound rather than a return.

GIRK Closure and Recovery of Presynaptic Calcium Current
amplifier
The second amplifier, and the inverse of "G Beta-Gamma Gating of GIRK Current and Calcium Channel Inhibition" in mu_opioid_receptor_agonism. With no G beta-gamma released from an activated heterotrimer, the G-protein-gated inwardly rectifying potassium channels close and the potassium conductance falls, while the direct G beta-gamma block of P/Q-, N- and L-type voltage-gated calcium channels is lifted and presynaptic calcium current recovers. The agonist-evoked outward potassium current is the measurable handle on this node - it reverses at the potassium equilibrium potential and is barium-sensitive, and an antagonist abolishes the physiological effect it produces within minutes. As in the agonism module the two halves are complementary rather than redundant: the potassium arm governs somatic and postsynaptic firing, the calcium arm governs transmitter release.
Downstream

Restored Neuronal Excitability
central effector
The central effector and the key conformance target: the single rate-limiting cellular event of mu-opioid receptor antagonism, and the exact inverse of the mu_opioid_receptor_agonism central effector "Neuronal Hyperpolarization and Reduced Neurotransmitter Release". The hyperpolarization lifts, tonic firing resumes and evoked transmitter release returns. In an opioid-naive neuron this restores baseline excitability; in a neuron adapted to chronic agonist exposure, with adenylyl cyclase and cAMP signalling compensatorily upregulated, removal of the inhibition leaves the cell firing above its prior rate, which is what makes the withdrawal branch a rebound rather than a return. Everything below this node is the same event read out in a different neuronal population, and conforming entries substitute the population their disease concerns (pre-Botzinger complex rhythm-generating neurons, locus coeruleus noradrenergic neurons, mesolimbic GABAergic interneurons, enteric neurons) while keeping this node unchanged.

Brainstem Respiratory Rhythm Restoration
effector
The therapeutic branch in overdose, and the inverse of "Brainstem Respiratory Rhythm Depression" in mu_opioid_receptor_agonism. Antagonist occupancy of mu-opioid receptors on rhythm-generating neurons of the pre-Botzinger complex and on the modulatory Kolliker-Fuse and parabrachial nuclei lifts the inhibition of the inspiratory rhythm generator, and respiratory rate recovers. The branch is pharmacologically unlike its agonism counterpart in one respect that matters clinically: recovery lasts only as long as antagonist occupancy exceeds what the remaining agonist can reclaim, so the branch has to be sustained rather than merely triggered.

Reversal of Opioid-Induced Respiratory Depression
consequence
The consequence an acute opioid poisoning entry conforms to: recovery of respiratory drive and consciousness, and survival of an otherwise fatal overdose. The consequence is real at the population level - naloxone distribution has been followed by reduced overdose mortality - and is time-limited at the individual level, because antagonist occupancy decays faster than the agonist burden in a patient who has taken a large dose or a long-acting formulation. Clinical practice therefore titrates to effect and continues treatment by infusion, and the illicitly manufactured fentanyl analogs have pushed the required dose and the number of repeat administrations upward.

Locus Coeruleus Noradrenergic Rebound
effector
The toxicity branch, and the one with no counterpart in the agonism module. Locus coeruleus noradrenergic neurons are inhibited by mu-opioid receptor agonism and adapt to chronic exposure by upregulating adenylyl cyclase and cAMP signalling, so their basal drive is restored against a continuing agonist load. Antagonist occupancy removes the inhibition from an already compensated cell, and firing and norepinephrine release overshoot: an antagonist given to a dependent patient markedly increases norepinephrine release in forebrain targets, and the same agents that reduce that efflux reduce the behavioural signs. This branch is not reached in an opioid-naive patient, which is why it is a toxicity of the drug in one population rather than a property of the drug.

Precipitated Opioid Withdrawal
consequence
The toxic consequence, and the dose-limiting one for every centrally acting antagonist. In an opioid-dependent patient, antagonist occupancy produces within minutes the autonomic, gastrointestinal and affective syndrome that spontaneous cessation would produce over hours - yawning, rhinorrhea, lacrimation, abdominal cramps, restlessness and aversion - together with cortisol release and increased dorsal striatal dopamine release that tracks the severity of the subjective aversion. Clinically this is why overdose reversal is titrated to respiratory effect rather than to full receptor blockade, and why antagonist maintenance for opioid use disorder requires a completed medical withdrawal before induction. It is the reason the peripherally restricted agents exist: an antagonist that does not enter the brain does not reach this branch.

Blockade of Opioid Reward Signalling
effector
The reward branch. With mu-opioid receptors on ventral tegmental area GABAergic interneurons occupied by an antagonist, an exogenous opioid can no longer hyperpolarize them, the dopamine neurons are not disinhibited, and the reinforcing dopamine signal that follows opioid use is not produced - which is read out clinically as loss of drug liking after a heroin challenge. The same blockade applies to the endogenous opioid arm: ethanol consumption releases beta-endorphin and other endogenous opioid peptides into the ventral striatum, and an antagonist competitively impedes their action at the same receptor, which is the mechanism by which naltrexone and nalmefene reduce alcohol-induced reward. Peripherally restricted antagonists do not reach this branch.

consequence
The therapeutic consequence in substance use disorder: use that produces no reward is not reinforced, so antagonist maintenance reduces relapse to regular opioid use and reduces heavy drinking in alcohol use disorder. Enteric Mu-Opioid Receptor Blockade and Restored Propulsive Motility
effector
The gastrointestinal branch, and the inverse of "Enteric Neuron Inhibition and Slowed Propulsive Motility" in mu_opioid_receptor_agonism. Mu-opioid receptors on enteric neurons of the myenteric and submucosal plexuses are peripheral, so an antagonist designed not to cross the blood-brain barrier - a quaternary amine such as methylnaltrexone, or a pegylated or efflux-substrate molecule such as naloxegol or naldemedine - occupies them while leaving central receptors free. The inhibition of coordinated propulsive contraction is relieved and motility recovers, with central analgesia preserved and the withdrawal branch not reached. This is the one branch of the module that is separable from the others by drug distribution rather than by dose, and it is the clearest demonstration that the module's branches are one receptor action in different populations.

An opioid receptor antagonist approved as a nasal spray for emergency treatment of known or suspected opioid overdose, and used for the same purpose by injection. Mechanistically it engages the same trigger node as naloxone; what distinguishes it there is higher receptor affinity and a longer duration of action, and in a healthy-volunteer model of remifentanil-induced respiratory depression a faster onset of reversal. Whether those properties are an advantage in overdose is contested rather than settled, and the dispute belongs on this entry rather than on the module's branches: the intranasal formulation was approved without new clinical data and has not been studied in patients currently overdosing, comparative effectiveness data against naloxone are sparse, and longer occupancy at higher affinity may mean a more prolonged and severe precipitated withdrawal. Two toxicology societies therefore recommend against replacing naloxone with it as the primary antidote.
