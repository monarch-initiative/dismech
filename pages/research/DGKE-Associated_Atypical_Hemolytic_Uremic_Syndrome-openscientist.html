DGKE-Associated Atypical Hemolytic Uremic Syndrome — Comprehensive Disease Report

MONDO: MONDO:0100590 · OMIM: #615008 (nephrotic syndrome type 7 / aHUS 7) · Causal gene: DGKE (diacylglycerol kinase epsilon; HGNC:2852) · Category: Mendelian (autosomal recessive)

Summary

DGKE-associated atypical hemolytic uremic syndrome (aHUS) is an autosomal-recessive, complement-independent thrombotic microangiopathy caused by biallelic loss-of-function variants in DGKE, the gene encoding diacylglycerol kinase epsilon (DGKε). This distinguishes it mechanistically from the majority of aHUS cases, which arise from dysregulation of the alternative complement pathway. The causal gene — to state it explicitly in line with the research brief — is DGKE, a lipid kinase with no role in the complement cascade. The seminal exome-sequencing study of Lemaire and colleagues identified recessive DGKE mutations co-segregating with aHUS in nine unrelated kindreds, defining a distinctive Mendelian disease (PMID: 23542698).

DGKε is a small, membrane-bound, constitutively active lipid kinase that is unique among the ten mammalian DGK isoforms in its specificity for 1-stearoyl-2-arachidonoyl-sn-glycerol — the diacylglycerol (DAG) backbone of the phosphatidylinositol (PI) cycle. Loss of enzyme function leaves arachidonoyl-DAG elevated, which activates protein kinase C (PKC) and p38-MAPK to generate a prothrombotic, repair-impaired endothelial state, and simultaneously starves VEGFR2 signaling of phosphatidylinositol-4,5-bisphosphate (PIP2), collapsing the downstream Akt→COX-2→prostaglandin E2 (PGE2) axis that normally protects the glomerular microvasculature. The net result is microvascular thrombosis and podocyte/endothelial injury in the kidney.

Clinically, the disease presents in infancy (typically before 1–2 years of age) with the classic TMA triad (microangiopathic hemolytic anemia, thrombocytopenia, acute kidney injury) plus a distinctive nephropathy — nephrotic-range proteinuria, hematuria, and hypertension — frequently with normal complement C3. The course is relapsing and progresses to chronic kidney disease (CKD)/end-stage kidney disease (ESKD) in a substantial fraction of patients. Because the disease is not complement-driven, anti-C5 therapy (eculizumab) is frequently ineffective at preventing relapses, and genetic diagnosis supports de-escalation of complement blockade in favor of supportive, nephroprotective care. Restoration of the PGE2 axis reversed disease in endothelial-specific Dgke-knockout mice and represents the most promising mechanism-based therapeutic lead.

Section 1 — Disease Information

Overview. DGKE-associated aHUS is an ultra-rare, infantile-onset thrombotic microangiopathy affecting principally the kidney. It belongs to the broader aHUS family but is pathogenically distinct: it is caused by loss of an intracellular lipid kinase rather than by complement dysregulation. The phenotypic spectrum overlaps with membranoproliferative glomerulonephritis (MPGN)-like injury and with isolated steroid-resistant nephrotic syndrome (SRNS), such that the same DGKE genotype can present as aHUS, MPGN, or SRNS (PMID: 32413569; PMID: 32386968).

Section 2 — Etiology

Primary cause (genetic). Biallelic (homozygous or compound-heterozygous) loss-of-function variants in DGKE. Lemaire et al. identified recessive DGKE mutations in nine unrelated kindreds (PMID: 23542698). This is a monogenic, fully genetic etiology; there is no established infectious or primary environmental cause.

Genetic risk factors / modifiers. The disease itself is recessive and Mendelian, but co-inherited complement variants can modify onset and severity. In the Spanish aHUS registry, among patients with DGKE mutations, several also carried heterozygous variants in thrombomodulin (THBD) or C3, and complement dysregulation appeared to "influence the onset and disease severity in carriers of diacylglycerol kinase-ε mutations" (PMID: 25135762). Thus complement genes act as potential modifiers rather than primary causes.

Environmental / triggering factors. As in other TMAs, intercurrent infections and, in reported cases, pregnancy can act as triggers or relapse precipitants; one DGKE mutation was identified in a pregnancy-related TMA cohort (PMID: 40306205). Age (infancy) is the dominant demographic risk window.

Protective factors. No validated genetic or environmental protective factors have been defined for this ultra-rare monogenic disease. Information not available.

Gene–environment interactions. The principal documented interaction is between the causal DGKE genotype and modifier complement genotype/triggers, which together shape clinical expressivity (PMID: 25135762).

Section 4 — Genetic / Molecular Information

Causal gene. DGKE (chromosome 17q22), encoding diacylglycerol kinase epsilon, a 64-kDa lipid kinase. Inheritance is autosomal recessive; disease requires two loss-of-function alleles.

Pathogenic variant spectrum. Variants span the gene and include missense, frameshift, nonsense, and splice-site classes. In a 12-patient cohort, 13 homozygous/compound-heterozygous variants were reported (7 pathogenic, 3 likely pathogenic by ACMG/AMP criteria), 11 of which were novel (PMID: 36988693). Recurrent/illustrative alleles include:

  • c.966G>A, p.Trp322Ter (nonsense) — recurrent; reported homozygous in SRNS and compound-heterozygous in aHUS (PMID: 32413569).
  • c.171delG, p.Ser58Alafs*111 (frameshift) (PMID: 32413569).
  • p.His536Qfs*16 (homozygous frameshift); p.Trp322*/p.Pro498Arg and p.Gln248His/p.Gly484Glyfs*10 (compound heterozygous) (PMID: 25135762).

Variant classification & frequency. Pathogenic alleles are individually ultra-rare in gnomAD, consistent with a recessive, severe pediatric phenotype. Origin is germline; no somatic contribution is described.

Functional consequence. Variants are loss-of-function (enzyme-dead). Lemaire et al. inferred that "loss of DGKE function results in a prothrombotic state" (PMID: 23542698). Mechanistically this is a true loss-of-function (not dominant-negative), consistent with recessive inheritance.

Modifier genes. Heterozygous complement variants (THBD, C3, CFI) can co-occur and modify severity/treatment response (PMID: 25135762; PMID: 38374836).

Epigenetic information / chromosomal abnormalities. No disease-specific methylation signature or large-scale cytogenetic abnormality has been described. Not applicable / not available.

Section 5 — Environmental Information

  • Environmental toxins/pollution/radiation/occupational exposure: none established. Not applicable.
  • Lifestyle factors: none established (disease onsets in infancy). Not applicable.
  • Infectious agents: The disease is not caused by Shiga-toxin–producing E. coli (which causes typical HUS). Infections may act as non-specific relapse triggers, as in other TMAs, but no specific pathogen is causal. The defining distinction from typical HUS is the absence of a Shiga-toxin etiology.

Section 6 — Mechanism / Pathophysiology

Ordered causal chain (initiating lesion → clinical manifestation)

  1. Biallelic loss-of-function mutation in DGKE leads to absent/non-functional diacylglycerol kinase epsilon protein in cells that normally express it (endothelium, podocytes, platelets). (Demonstrated: PMID: 23542698.)
  2. Loss of the only arachidonoyl-DAG–specific kinase results in accumulation of 1-stearoyl-2-arachidonoyl-DAG and failure to regenerate phosphatidylinositol/PIP2 via the PI cycle. (Demonstrated biochemically: PMID: 27803897; PMID: 38008259.)

Branch A — prothrombotic/proinflammatory endothelium: 3A. Elevated arachidonoyl-DAG activates protein kinase C (PKC) and p38-MAPK. (Inferred from Lemaire; demonstrated in endothelial cells: PMID: 23542698; PMID: 25498910.) 4A. PKC/p38-MAPK signaling upregulates tissue factor and ICAM-1, promotes endothelial activation, apoptosis, and impaired angiogenesis, resulting in a prothrombotic, proinflammatory endothelial surface — without complement deposition. (Demonstrated in vitro: PMID: 25498910.)

Branch B — loss of endothelial protective signaling: 3B. PIP2 shortage impairs VEGFR2 signaling (VEGFA pathway) downstream of the receptor. (Demonstrated in vivo: PMID: 33986189.) 4B. Failed VEGFR2→Akt activation reduces COX-2 expression and PGE2 production. (Demonstrated: PMID: 33986189; PMID: 26887830.) 5. Branches A and B converge: a prothrombotic, repair-deficient glomerular microvasculature leads to microvascular/capillary thrombosis, endothelial swelling, and podocyte injury (thrombotic microangiopathy + nephrotic-range proteinuria). 6. Glomerular TMA results in the clinical triad (hemolytic anemia from mechanical RBC shearing, platelet consumption/thrombocytopenia, acute kidney injury) plus proteinuria, hematuria, and hypertension. 7. Recurrent injury leads to chronic kidney disease / ESKD over time.

Supporting detail

  • Molecular pathways: PI cycle (lipid resynthesis); DAG–PKC signaling; p38-MAPK; VEGFA–VEGFR2→PI3K–Akt; COX-2/prostaglandin (PGE2) axis.
  • Cellular processes: endothelial activation, apoptosis, impaired angiogenesis/migration, platelet activation, podocyte dysfunction, microthrombosis.
  • Protein dysfunction: complete loss of a constitutively active lipid kinase (catalytic/expression loss-of-function, not misfolding-aggregation).
  • Immune involvement: complement-independent — a defining feature. Bruneau et al. showed endothelial activation and death "independently of complement activation": "loss of DGKε expression/activity in EC induces an increase in ICAM-1 and tissue factor expression through the upregulation of p38-MAPK-mediated signals" (PMID: 25498910).

Section 7 — Anatomical Structures Affected

  • Primary organ: kidney — glomerulus (UBERON:0000074), renal glomerular capillary/endothelium.
  • Secondary / systemic: microvasculature broadly; hematologic effects (hemolysis, thrombocytopenia). Rare extrarenal TMA features may occur but renal involvement dominates.
  • Body systems: urinary/renal; hematologic; cardiovascular (microvascular, hypertension).
  • Tissue / cell level: glomerular endothelial cells (CL:1000746), podocytes (CL:0000653), platelets (CL:0000233). Histology shows TMA and, in a subset, MPGN-like changes (PMID: 32413569).
  • Subcellular compartments: endoplasmic reticulum and Golgi (DGKε is S-palmitoylated at Cys38/40 and localizes to the ER; PMID: 38008259); plasma membrane (PI-cycle lipid signaling). GO cellular-component terms: GO:0005789 (ER membrane), GO:0005886 (plasma membrane).
  • UBERON suggestions: UBERON:0002113 (kidney), UBERON:0000074 (renal glomerulus), UBERON:0004638 (renal glomerular capillary).
  • Lateralization: bilateral (systemic genetic disease; both kidneys).

Section 8 — Temporal Development

  • Onset: congenital/infantile — median ~11 months, essentially all before age 2 years (PMID: 36988693; PMID: 23542698). Onset pattern is acute (TMA episode) on a chronic genetic background.
  • Progression / course: relapsing-remitting superimposed on a progressive decline. Relapses occurred in ~66.7% and death/CKD progression in ~60% at median 3.3-year follow-up (PMID: 36988693). A notable natural-history feature is that acute TMA episodes may diminish after early childhood while proteinuria/CKD persists.
  • Disease duration: chronic, lifelong.
  • Remission patterns: both spontaneous and treatment-associated remissions are reported, but relapses are common; notably, relapses can occur as isolated nephrotic-range proteinuria during or despite C5 blockade (PMID: 42077707; PMID: 33879077).
  • Critical period: infancy/early childhood is the window of greatest vulnerability and the key period for diagnosis and nephroprotective intervention.

Section 9 — Inheritance and Population

  • Epidemiology / incidence: ultra-rare. UK registry data defined the incidence of DGKE aHUS at 0.009/million/year and DGKE "MPGN" at 0.006/million/year, a combined 0.015/million/year (PMID: 32386968). In early-onset (<2 years) aHUS cohorts, DGKE mutations account for a meaningful minority — e.g., 4/83 in the Spanish registry (PMID: 25135762).
  • Inheritance pattern: autosomal recessive.
  • Penetrance: high/complete for the renal phenotype in biallelic carriers (with variable expressivity across aHUS/MPGN/SRNS presentations).
  • Expressivity: variable — same genotype can yield aHUS, MPGN-like disease, or isolated SRNS (PMID: 32413569).
  • Consanguinity: homozygous cases in consanguineous or founder settings are reported; consanguinity increases recessive risk.
  • Anticipation / mosaicism / founder effects: no repeat-expansion anticipation; no specific founder mutation established, though recurrent alleles (e.g., p.Trp322Ter) exist. Carrier frequency is low (ultra-rare alleles).
  • Demographics: reported across multiple ethnicities worldwide; no strong sex bias documented for this monogenic disease. Age distribution is strongly skewed to infancy.

Section 10 — Diagnostics

Clinical/laboratory signature. The diagnostic trigger is infantile aHUS (TMA triad) accompanied by nephrotic-range proteinuria/hematuria and typically normal complement C3. "For infants and young children with aHUS in conjunct with nephrotic level proteinuria, variants of the DGKE gene should be screened" (PMID: 37994143).

Core labs: CBC with schistocytes, low haptoglobin, elevated LDH, indirect hyperbilirubinemia (hemolysis); thrombocytopenia; elevated creatinine; urinalysis (proteinuria, hematuria); serum albumin (low), cholesterol (elevated); complement C3/C4 (usually normal); ADAMTS13 activity (to exclude TTP); stool studies/Shiga toxin (to exclude STEC-HUS).

Biopsy/pathology: renal biopsy shows thrombotic microangiopathy; a subset shows MPGN-like glomerular changes (PMID: 32413569).

Genetic testing (definitive). Molecular confirmation requires DGKE sequencing, best delivered via a targeted aHUS/TMA gene panel that includes DGKE alongside complement genes (CFH, CFHR1–5, CFI, CD46/MCP, C3, CFB, THBD) and, where panels are negative, whole-exome sequencing (as in the original discovery). MLPA of the CFH-CFHR region is useful to characterize co-modifiers. Chromosomal microarray, karyotyping, FISH, mtDNA, and repeat-expansion testing are not applicable.

Clinical criteria / differential diagnosis. Must distinguish from (1) complement-mediated aHUS (low C3 more typical, complement variants, often eculizumab-responsive), (2) STEC/typical HUS (preceding bloody diarrhea, Shiga toxin), (3) TTP (ADAMTS13 <10%), and (4) secondary TMA. The combination of infantile onset + heavy proteinuria + normal C3 points to DGKE (PMID: 37994143).

Screening. Cascade genetic testing of at-risk siblings and carrier testing of parents (recessive); no population newborn screen exists.

Section 11 — Outcome / Prognosis

  • Renal outcomes: guarded. ~60% death or progression to CKD and ~67% relapse at median 3.3 years (PMID: 36988693); persistent hypertension/proteinuria in ~40%.
  • Natural history nuance: acute TMA episodes may attenuate after early childhood, but proteinuria and progressive CKD persist, so long-term prognosis is dominated by nephropathy rather than recurrent hemolytic crises (PMID: 32386968).
  • Mortality: disease-specific mortality is low relative to morbidity; the dominant adverse outcome is ESKD requiring dialysis/transplant.
  • Transplant: because the defect is intrinsic to kidney cells (endothelium/podocytes) and is not circulating-complement-mediated, post-transplant TMA recurrence risk is expected to be lower than in complement-mediated aHUS — an important prognostic/therapeutic distinction (mechanistic inference supported by the cell-intrinsic, complement-independent biology; PMID: 25498910).
  • Prognostic factors: degree/persistence of proteinuria, hypertension control, baseline GFR, relapse frequency, and co-inherited complement variants (worse with complement modifiers; PMID: 25135762).

Section 12 — Treatment

Overarching principle: DGKE aHUS is complement-independent, so complement blockade is often ineffective at preventing relapses; genotype-guided care emphasizes nephroprotection and de-escalation of unnecessary anti-C5 therapy.

  • Complement inhibition (eculizumab / ravulizumab; NCIT: Eculizumab C88248). Widely used empirically at presentation before genotype is known. Evidence is mixed: some patients with low C3 appear to respond (PMID: 33879077; PMID: 25135762), but relapses occur despite C5 blockade in the absence of complement activation — "eculizumab did not prevent disease relapses, which occurred in the absence of complement activation" (PMID: 42077707). Genetic confirmation supports discontinuation/de-escalation to avoid overtreatment: "The lack of genetic testing options has led to overtreatment in this patient" (PMID: 42077707).
  • Nephroprotection (first-line for the chronic phase). RAAS blockade (ACE inhibitors/ARBs) for proteinuria and hypertension; blood-pressure control; supportive care for CKD.
  • Plasma therapy. Plasma infusion/exchange has controlled recurrences in some patients, particularly those with co-existing complement/THBD variants (PMID: 25135762).
  • Antithrombotic strategies. Given the prothrombotic mechanism (tissue factor/PKC), antiplatelet/anticoagulant approaches are a rational consideration, though not formally validated.
  • Mechanism-based / experimental. Restoration of the PGE2 axis is the leading targeted concept: a stable PGE2 analog reversed TMA and proteinuria in endothelial-specific Dgke-knockout mice (PMID: 33986189). This is preclinical; no human trial yet.
  • Renal replacement / transplant. Dialysis and kidney transplantation for ESKD; recurrence risk appears lower than complement-mediated aHUS (see Prognosis).
  • Pharmacogenomics: none established.

NCIT term suggestions: Eculizumab (C88248), ACE Inhibitor (C705)/Angiotensin Receptor Antagonist, Plasmapheresis/Plasma Exchange (C50785), Kidney Transplantation (C15366), Dialysis (C15248).

Section 13 — Prevention

  • Primary prevention: not possible for a monogenic recessive disease beyond reproductive counseling. Genetic counseling for consanguineous/carrier families; options include carrier testing, prenatal testing, and preimplantation genetic diagnosis (recessive, 25% recurrence risk per pregnancy).
  • Secondary prevention: early recognition of infantile aHUS with heavy proteinuria and prompt DGKE sequencing to enable early nephroprotection and to avoid overtreatment with complement inhibitors (PMID: 37994143; PMID: 42077707); cascade testing of siblings.
  • Tertiary prevention: aggressive BP/proteinuria control to slow CKD; relapse surveillance; meningococcal vaccination is required for any patient placed on eculizumab (anti-C5 therapy increases meningococcal risk; PMID: 27811836).
  • Public-health / environmental interventions: not applicable.

Section 14 — Other Species / Natural Disease

  • Orthologous gene: mouse Dgke (NCBI Gene 56077); orthologs conserved across vertebrates. DGKε's arachidonoyl specificity and PI-cycle role are evolutionarily conserved (PMID: 27803897).
  • Naturally occurring disease in other species: no naturally occurring DGKE nephropathy has been documented in companion animals or wildlife (OMIA search not productive). Not available.
  • Comparative biology: engineered mouse models (below) faithfully reproduce the endothelial TMA mechanism, supporting conservation of the pathway.
  • Zoonotic potential / transmission: none (genetic disease).

Key Findings (with evidence)

Finding 1 — Recessive, complement-independent Mendelian disease caused by biallelic DGKE loss-of-function

Exome sequencing identified recessive DGKE mutations co-segregating with aHUS in nine unrelated kindreds, and the authors stated they "identified recessive mutations in DGKE (encoding diacylglycerol kinase ɛ) that co-segregated with aHUS in nine unrelated kindreds, defining a distinctive Mendelian disease" (PMID: 23542698). DGKε has no role in complement; variants span missense, frameshift, nonsense, and splice classes; a 12-patient cohort reported 13 biallelic variants (7 pathogenic, 3 likely pathogenic, 11 novel) (PMID: 36988693). Lemaire explicitly inferred that "loss of DGKE function results in a prothrombotic state."

Finding 2 — Mechanism: prothrombotic endothelium via DAG/PKC + collapse of VEGFR2→COX-2→PGE2 signaling

DGKε is arachidonoyl-DAG specific and recycles phosphatidylinositol; its loss elevates arachidonoyl-DAG, activating PKC/p38-MAPK and upregulating tissue factor and ICAM-1 (PMID: 23542698; PMID: 25498910). In endothelial-specific Dgke-KO mice, "loss of Dgke in the endothelium results in impaired signaling downstream of VEGFR2 due to cellular shortage of phosphatidylinositol 4,5-biphosphate," and "treating the endothelial specific Dgke-knockout mice with a stable PGE2 analog was sufficient to reverse the clinical manifestations of thrombotic microangiopathy and proteinuria" (PMID: 33986189).

Finding 3 — Infantile-onset nephropathy with proteinuria and progression to CKD

"Patients presented at a median age of 11 (7.5, 12.3) months; all were younger than 2 years," and at median 3.3-year follow-up "significant hypertension and/or proteinuria (40%), relapses (66.7%), and death or progression to CKD (60%) were common" (PMID: 36988693). Lemaire: affected individuals "present with aHUS before age 1 year, have persistent hypertension, hematuria and proteinuria (sometimes in the nephrotic range), and develop chronic kidney disease with age" (PMID: 23542698).

Finding 4 — Ultra-rare; eculizumab efficacy controversial

"We defined the incidence of DGKE aHUS as 0.009/million/year and so-called DGKE MPGN as 0.006/million/year, giving a combined incidence of 0.015/million/year" (PMID: 32386968). "Eculizumab did not prevent disease relapses, which occurred in the absence of complement activation" (PMID: 42077707).

Finding 5 — Models recapitulate endothelial TMA and are pharmacologically rescuable

Endothelial-specific KO mice develop TMA/proteinuria reversible by a PGE2 analog (PMID: 33986189); global KO mice show impaired glomerular Cox-2/PGE2 (PMID: 26887830); DGKε-knockdown endothelial cells activate without complement deposition (PMID: 25498910).

Finding 6 — DGKε is a uniquely arachidonoyl-specific, constitutively active PI-cycle lipid kinase

"It is the only DGK isoform with specificity for a particular species of DAG, i.e., 1-stearoyl-2-arachidonoyl glycerol" (PMID: 27803897); "DGKε is involved in the PI cycle maintaining the cellular level of phosphorylated PI derivatives of signaling activity" (PMID: 38008259). Its substrate selectivity (LOX-like motif) explains the disease's lipid-signaling selectivity (PMID: 22266092; PMID: 28199087).

Finding 7 — Diagnostic signature + genotype-guided management

Infantile onset + TMA + nephrotic-range proteinuria/hematuria + normal C3 + MPGN-like histology → sequence DGKE: "For infants and young children with aHUS in conjunct with nephrotic level proteinuria, variants of the DGKE gene should be screened" (PMID: 37994143). Genetic diagnosis prevents overtreatment with complement blockade — "the lack of genetic testing options has led to overtreatment in this patient" (PMID: 42077707).

Mechanistic Model / Interpretation

The disease is best understood as a cell-intrinsic lipid-signaling failure of the glomerular microvasculature. DGKε sits at a single, non-redundant node in the phosphatidylinositol cycle, uniquely handling the arachidonoyl-DAG that is the PI backbone. When that node fails, two consequences propagate in parallel: (i) a gain of a prothrombotic/proinflammatory program (DAG→PKC/p38-MAPK→tissue factor/ICAM-1, with endothelial apoptosis), and (ii) a loss of a protective program (PIP2 depletion→VEGFR2→Akt→COX-2→PGE2). The convergence of "more thrombosis, less repair" on the glomerular endothelium and podocyte explains the simultaneous TMA and heavy proteinuria that make this entity clinically distinctive. The absence of complement involvement is the organizing clinical fact: it explains the typically normal C3, the frequent failure of eculizumab to prevent relapses, and the rationale for genotype-guided de-escalation. The model is strongly supported because the critical protective node (PGE2) is both necessary (lost in KO mice) and sufficient (its pharmacologic restoration reverses disease) — an unusually complete causal demonstration for a rare disease.

Limitations and Knowledge Gaps

  • Small sample sizes. All clinical data derive from small case series/registries (largest cohorts ~12 patients); frequencies and outcome percentages carry wide uncertainty.
  • Phenotype–genotype correlation is incomplete. Why the same DGKE genotype produces aHUS vs MPGN vs isolated SRNS is unresolved; modifier contributions (complement variants, triggers) are only partly characterized.
  • Eculizumab evidence is anecdotal and conflicting. No randomized data exist; apparent responses in low-C3 patients may reflect co-modifiers.
  • PGE2 therapy is preclinical. The compelling mouse rescue has not been translated to humans; dosing, delivery, and safety in infants are unknown.
  • Transplant recurrence risk is inferred from mechanism rather than established by large transplant cohorts.
  • QoL, natural history beyond childhood, and adult outcomes are poorly documented.

Proposed Follow-up Experiments / Actions

  1. International DGKE nephropathy registry with harmonized genotype, histology, complement status, treatment, and long-term renal outcomes to firm up frequencies and genotype–phenotype correlations.
  2. Prospective / structured evaluation of complement-inhibitor withdrawal in genetically confirmed DGKE patients, to formalize de-escalation guidance.
  3. Translational development of PGE2-axis therapeutics (stable PGE2 analogs or EP-receptor agonists) toward first-in-human studies, building on the KO-mouse rescue.
  4. Mechanistic dissection of the aHUS/MPGN/SRNS branch point, including podocyte-specific Dgke models and single-cell profiling of glomerular cell types.
  5. Evaluate antithrombotic/PKC-directed strategies in model systems given the tissue-factor/PKC arm of the mechanism.
  6. Define post-transplant recurrence risk through multicenter transplant-cohort analysis.
