Lipoprotein Glomerulopathy (LPG): A Comprehensive Disease Characteristics Report

Target Disease: Lipoprotein Glomerulopathy MONDO ID: MONDO:0012725 · OMIM: 611771 · Gene: APOE (HGNC:613; chr19q13.32) · Category: Mendelian (autosomal dominant, incomplete penetrance)

Summary

Lipoprotein glomerulopathy (LPG) is an ultra-rare, autosomal-dominant renal lipidosis caused by heterozygous (rarely homozygous) missense and small in-frame variants in the APOE gene, clustered in or near the LDL-receptor-binding region of apolipoprotein E (apoE). It is histologically defined by markedly dilated glomerular capillary loops occluded by lamellated, vacuolated, apoE-rich lipoprotein thrombi that lack foam cells and are negative for immunoglobulins and complement. Clinically, patients present with proteinuria/nephrotic syndrome, microscopic hematuria, and a type III hyperlipoproteinemia-like dyslipidemia with markedly elevated serum apoE. More than 200 cases have been reported worldwide since the entity was first described ~30 years ago, with cases concentrated in East Asia (Japan, China) through documented founder variants (PMID: 40306438; PMID: 35602473).

The core mechanism is conformational: LPG-associated APOE variants (predominantly proline substitutions and small deletions/duplications around residues 136–158) thermodynamically destabilize the N-terminal domain of apoE, driving it toward a molten-globule-like state with increased exposed hydrophobic surface and aggregation propensity. The misfolded, aggregation-prone apoE forms aberrant lipoprotein particles that deposit as intraglomerular thrombi, occluding dilated capillaries and producing proteinuria and progressive chronic kidney disease (PMID: 23110818; PMID: 31874799). The etiologic role of mutant apoE has been confirmed in vivo: adenoviral transduction of apoE-Sendai (and apoE-Kyoto) into apoE-deficient mice reproduces LPG-like glomerular lesions (PMID: 16431249; PMID: 19459027; PMID: 33663537).

LPG carries a guarded prognosis — approximately half of patients progress to end-stage kidney disease (ESKD) within 1–27 years — but it is not steroid-responsive. The cornerstone of treatment is lipid-lowering therapy, especially fibrates (fenofibrate, bezafibrate, and the selective PPARα modulator pemafibrate), often combined with RAAS blockade (ACEi/ARB); these can markedly reduce proteinuria and, in some reports, induce complete remission. Because the pathogenic apoE is a circulating protein, LPG recurs after kidney transplantation. Diagnosis rests on two pillars: a characteristic kidney biopsy plus molecular confirmation by APOE sequencing (PMID: 40306438; PMID: 38769490).

1. Disease Information

Overview. LPG is an inherited glomerular disease in which apoE-rich lipoproteins accumulate as "thrombi" within dilated glomerular capillary lumina, producing proteinuria, nephrotic syndrome, and progressive renal dysfunction. It is a renal lipidosis distinct from systemic lipid storage diseases and from immune-complex glomerulonephritis. Definitive diagnosis requires molecular genetic analysis of APOE in addition to characteristic biopsy findings (PMID: 40306438).

"Lipoprotein glomerulopathy (LPG) is an ultrarare kidney disorder caused by pathogenic variants in the APOE gene. Although kidney biopsy presents typical findings, such as dilated capillary loops containing lipoprotein thrombi, definitive diagnosis requires molecular genetic analysis of APOE." — PMID: 40306438

2. Etiology

Disease causal factors. LPG is a purely monogenic genetic disorder caused by variants in APOE. No infectious or toxic cause is implicated, and no environmental trigger is required. ApoE is the principal ligand for LDL-receptor–mediated clearance of triglyceride-rich/remnant lipoproteins; LPG-causing variants lie in or near the receptor-binding region (residues ~136–158), producing a dysbetalipoproteinemia-like (type III) plasma profile with remnant accumulation and elevated serum apoE (PMID: 27277824; PMID: 23407349).

"Apolipoprotein (apo) E was initially described as a lipid transport protein and major ligand for low density lipoprotein (LDL) receptors with a role in cholesterol metabolism and cardiovascular disease." — PMID: 27277824

Genetic risk factors. The causal variants are heterozygous APOE missense substitutions (frequently arginine→proline) and small in-frame deletions/duplications near the LDL-receptor-binding site. Recurrent variants include apoE-Sendai (Arg145Pro), apoE-Kyoto (Arg25Cys; signal-inclusive Arg43Cys), apoE-Osaka/Kurashiki (Arg158Pro), apoE-Chicago (Arg147Pro), apoE-Tokyo/Maebashi (Lys143–Arg145 deletion), apoE-Guangzhou (Arg150Pro), and apoE-Kanto (Asp151dup) (PMID: 23407349; PMID: 39141311). Founder effects are documented for apoE-Sendai (eastern Japan) and apoE-Kyoto (China).

Environmental / modifier risk factors. Because penetrance is incomplete (many asymptomatic carriers exist in affected families), additional genetic, epigenetic, and/or environmental modifiers are inferred to contribute. Co-inheritance of additional apoE alleles (e.g., apoE2 Arg158Cys, apoE5 Glu3Lys) or other conditions has been proposed to modify expression in individual kindreds (PMID: 23407349; PMID: 29356944; PMID: 26955632).

"In addition to APOE mutation, other genetic and/or epigenetic factors are considered to be involved in the pathogenesis of LPG because of its low penetrance." — PMID: 23407349

Protective factors. No specific genetic or environmental protective factors are established. Early lipid-lowering therapy is associated with better renal outcomes (see Treatment), functioning as a modifiable disease-course factor rather than a prevention of onset.

Gene–environment interactions. Incomplete penetrance strongly implies gene-environment/gene-gene interaction, but specific interacting exposures are not well characterized.

Age of onset. Predominantly adult-onset with an average age of presentation of 32 years (range 4–69) (PMID: 38438730). Pediatric and early-onset cases occur, including 6- and 7.5-year-old boys, a 12-year-old girl, and a 23-month-old boy with homozygous APOE Kyoto and parental consanguinity presenting as steroid-resistant nephrotic syndrome (PMID: 39993083; PMID: 22874113; PMID: 42417997).

"It is a monogenic disorder with autosomal dominant inheritance and the average age of presentation is 32 years (4-69 years)." — PMID: 38438730

Severity / progression / variability. Severity is variable and progression is generally chronic and progressive, often culminating in ESKD. A notable phenotypic nuance: a subset of patients are normolipidemic (e.g., certain APOE Kyoto and APOE Kanto cases with normal serum lipids and apoE), so normal lipids do not exclude LPG (PMID: 40186270; PMID: 42309892; PMID: 39141311).

"In contrast to previous cases, this patient lacked hyperlipidemia and ApoE was a Kyoto mutation." — PMID: 40186270

Quality of life impact. No formal EQ-5D/SF-36 data exist for LPG. Impact is inferred from nephrotic-range proteinuria, edema, and progression to dialysis-dependent ESKD, all of which substantially impair daily functioning. LPG can co-occur with IgA nephropathy, membranous nephropathy, or lupus nephritis, which can add to morbidity.

4. Genetic / Molecular Information

Causal gene. APOE (apolipoprotein E), HGNC:613, OMIM gene 107741; disease OMIM 611771; located at 19q13.32. LPG is caused by rare, protein-altering APOE variants — distinct from the common E2/E3/E4 isoform polymorphisms (though some patients carry combinations of a rare LPG variant with a common isoform allele).

Pathogenic variants. Causal variants cluster in/near the LDL-receptor-binding region (residues ~136–158). The table below lists the principal named variants (mature-protein numbering unless noted):

Variant classification & type. Variants are pathogenic (ACMG/AMP), predominantly missense (frequently arginine→proline substitutions) plus small in-frame deletions/duplications. Origin is germline. Functional consequence is a conformational/structural defect (destabilization/aggregation) — effectively a toxic gain-of-function / dominant-negative-like behavior at the lipoprotein-particle level rather than simple loss of ligand function, consistent with autosomal-dominant inheritance.

Allele frequency. Causal variants are rare/absent in population databases; the apoE-Sendai founder variant was not detected in 2,023 controls, confirming rarity in the general population (PMID: 23407349).

Modifier genes / epigenetics. Incomplete penetrance implicates additional genetic/epigenetic modifiers; co-inheritance of apoE2 (Arg158Cys) or apoE5 (Glu3Lys) has been proposed to modify phenotype in specific families (PMID: 26955632; PMID: 29356944). No specific DNA-methylation/histone signatures have been defined for LPG.

Chromosomal abnormalities. None reported; LPG is a single-gene point-variant/small-indel disorder.

5. Environmental Information

LPG has no established environmental cause. There are no implicated toxins, radiation, pollutants, or occupational exposures; no lifestyle factor is established as causal; and no infectious agent triggers the disease. Incomplete penetrance implies that unidentified genetic/epigenetic/environmental modifiers influence whether carriers develop overt disease, but these remain uncharacterized (PMID: 23407349). Not applicable / not established for most environmental subcategories.

6. Mechanism / Pathophysiology

Causal chain (initiating lesion → clinical manifestation)

  1. A heterozygous APOE missense/in-frame variant near the LDL-receptor-binding region (e.g., Arg145Pro) results in an amino-acid change incompatible with normal helical packing of the N-terminal domain.
  2. This leads to thermodynamic destabilization of the apoE N-terminal four-helix bundle, loss of unfolding intermediates, and a shift toward a molten-globule-like state (demonstrated in vitro).
  3. The destabilized protein exposes hydrophobic surface to solvent, which results in increased aggregation propensity and protease sensitivity (demonstrated in vitro).
  4. Aggregation-prone mutant apoE leads to formation of aberrant, destabilized apoE-rich lipoprotein particles and impaired remnant-lipoprotein clearance, producing a type III–like dyslipidemia with elevated serum apoE (inferred from plasma profiles + in vitro particle data).
  5. These abnormal lipoproteins deposit within glomerular capillaries as lamellated lipoprotein thrombi (branch: a complementary mechanism posits impaired macrophage clearance of lipoproteins, explaining thrombi forming without foam cells).
  6. Thrombi occlude markedly dilated glomerular capillary loops, which results in proteinuria, hematuria, and reduced filtration.
  7. Chronic capillary occlusion and glomerular injury lead to progressive chronic kidney disease and, in ~50% of patients, ESKD (demonstrated by natural-history data).

"All three variants were found to have significantly reduced helical content, to expose a larger portion of hydrophobic surface to the solvent, and to be significantly thermodynamically destabilized, often lacking functionally relevant unfolding intermediates." — PMID: 23110818

"Overall, our data suggest that these mutations induce a generalized unfolding of the N-terminal domain of apoE3 toward a molten-globule-like structure." — PMID: 23110818

"LPG can be induced by thermodynamic destabilization, hydrophobic surface exposure, and the aggregation of apoE resulting from the incompatibility of apoE mutated residues within helical regions." — PMID: 31874799

Detail by category

  • Molecular pathways / metabolic changes. LDL-receptor-mediated clearance of triglyceride-rich/remnant lipoproteins (apoE is the key ligand); remnant/VLDL metabolism; lipid transport. Disruption yields dysbetalipoproteinemia-like remnant accumulation (PMID: 27277824; PMID: 10359567). Suggested GO: GO:0006869 (lipid transport), GO:0034447 (very-low-density lipoprotein particle clearance), GO:0042157 (lipoprotein metabolic process). CHEBI: CHEBI:16113 (cholesterol), CHEBI:17855 (triglyceride).
  • Protein dysfunction. Misfolding, structural destabilization, hydrophobic exposure, and aggregation of apoE — a conformational disease mechanism (PMID: 23110818; PMID: 30309894; PMID: 24149834). Suggested GO: GO:0006457 (protein folding), GO:0043234/GO:0032983 (protein-containing complex / aggregation-related).
  • Cellular processes / immune involvement. Immunofluorescence for immunoglobulins and complement is negative — LPG is not immune-complex mediated. Macrophage handling of lipoproteins is implicated as a complementary mechanism (thrombi without foam cells); a variant/atypical case showed foamy-macrophage infiltration when combined with apoE2, bridging LPG and apoE2-homozygote glomerulopathy (PMID: 26955632; PMID: 24149835). Suggested CL: CL:0000650 (mesangial cell), CL:0000653 (podocyte), CL:0000235 (macrophage), CL:0002188 (glomerular endothelial cell).
  • Tissue damage mechanisms. Capillary occlusion by lipoprotein thrombi → glomerular ischemia/mechanical injury → proteinuria and progressive glomerulosclerosis/fibrosis.
  • Lipidomics / molecular profiling. Plasma shows a type III (dysbetalipoproteinemia) pattern with elevated triglycerides, elevated apoB, and markedly elevated apoE (variant-dependent). Glomerular proteomics by LC-MS/MS of laser-microdissected tissue shows apoE enrichment (PMID: 38769490).

7. Anatomical Structures Affected

  • Organ level (primary): Kidney — specifically the renal glomerulus (UBERON:0000074). Secondary involvement: systemic lipid metabolism (dyslipidemia); cardiovascular risk from dysbetalipoproteinemia; arterial stiffness reported in some patients (PMID: 38769490).
  • Body system: Urinary/renal system (primary); cardiovascular/metabolic (secondary).
  • Tissue/cell level: Glomerular capillary tuft — dilated glomerular capillaries (UBERON:0005750, glomerular capillary). Cell types implicated: glomerular endothelial cells (CL:0002188), mesangial cells (CL:0000650), podocytes (CL:0000653), and infiltrating macrophages (CL:0000235) in atypical cases.
  • Subcellular level: Extracellular deposition within the capillary lumen (lipoprotein thrombi); at the protein level, N-terminal domain misfolding. Suggested GO cellular components: GO:0034361 (very-low-density lipoprotein particle), GO:0042627 (chylomicron), GO:0005615 (extracellular space).
  • Localization / lateralization: Bilateral, diffuse glomerular involvement (systemic circulating apoE drives deposition in both kidneys).

"Immunofluorescence staining showed that the thrombi were strongly positive for apo A, B, and E. Granules and various sizes of vacuoles were observed in the thrombi under electron microscope." — PMID: 15498375

8. Temporal Development

  • Onset: Typically adult-onset (mean ~32 years, range 4–69), insidious/chronic. Pediatric and even infantile cases occur (23-month-old homozygote) (PMID: 38438730; PMID: 42417997).
  • Progression: Chronic and generally progressive. Approximately half of patients reach ESKD over a wide window of 1–27 years from onset (PMID: 38769490).
  • Disease course: Progressive proteinuria → declining eGFR → ESKD. Proteinuria reduction >50% is associated with slower renal decline (see Treatment) (PMID: 32441489).
  • Remission patterns: Spontaneous remission is not characteristic; treatment-induced remission (proteinuria reduction, even complete remission) is reported with fibrates (PMID: 42371225).
  • Critical period / window of opportunity: Early initiation of lipid-lowering therapy is emphasized as the best opportunity to preserve renal function (PMID: 24149835).

"approximately half of the patients develop end-stage kidney disease within 1-27 years of disease onset" — PMID: 38769490

9. Inheritance and Population

  • Inheritance: Autosomal dominant with incomplete penetrance; rare homozygous (recessive-presenting) cases occur with consanguinity (PMID: 38438730; PMID: 42417997).
  • Penetrance/expressivity: Incomplete penetrance (many asymptomatic carriers); variable expressivity (including normolipidemic forms) (PMID: 41612256; PMID: 40186270).
  • Founder effects: apoE-Sendai (eastern Japan, including Yamagata) and apoE-Kyoto (China) descend through founder haplotypes (PMID: 23407349; PMID: 24149835).
  • Consanguinity: Relevant in homozygous pediatric cases (PMID: 42417997).
  • Epidemiology: Ultra-rare — >200 cases reported worldwide over ~30 years; an earlier review counted 65 cases. Most patients are in Japan and East Asia. Formal prevalence/incidence rates are not established (no Orphanet prevalence class). Clinical series show a male predominance and adult onset (PMID: 35602473; PMID: 16431249).

"At present, 65 cases have been reported worldwide, although most patients are found in Japan and east Asian countries." — PMID: 16431249

"the haplotype of all APOE-Sendai mutations was identical, suggesting that APOE-Sendai mutation is common in Japanese patients probably through a founder effect." — PMID: 23407349

10. Diagnostics

Two-pillar diagnostic standard: (1) characteristic kidney biopsy and (2) molecular confirmation by APOE sequencing (PMID: 40306438).

Biopsy / histopathology. Light microscopy shows markedly dilated glomerular capillary loops filled with pale, weakly-staining lamellated lipoprotein thrombi. Staining profile: PAS weak-positive, Masson pale blue, silver/MT pale, Congo-red negative (excludes amyloid), Oil Red O / Sudan positive on frozen sections (confirms lipid), and apoE-positive by immunohistochemistry (also apoA, apoB positive). Immunofluorescence for immunoglobulins and complement is negative (distinguishes from immune-complex GN). Electron microscopy shows granular/vacuolated thrombi of variable electron density (PMID: 41405262; PMID: 15498375).

"These thrombi appeared PAS weak positive, MT pale blue, silver negative, and Congo-Red negative. These capillary luminal contents stained reddish with Oil-red O confirming lipid contents." — PMID: 41405262

Genetic testing. Single-gene APOE Sanger sequencing is the primary confirmatory test; whole-exome sequencing is increasingly used and can reveal co-inherited variants (e.g., COL4A5 in an Alport/LPG dual-diagnosis case) (PMID: 40306438; PMID: 41612256).

Supportive labs / biomarkers. Elevated serum apoE and apoB, hypertriglyceridemia, type III dysbetalipoproteinemia pattern (mid-band on electrophoresis). Caveat: serum apoE can be normal in some variants (Osaka, Kanto, normolipidemic Kyoto) — normal apoE does not exclude LPG (PMID: 21325775; PMID: 39141311).

Advanced / omics diagnostics. LC-MS/MS of laser-microdissected glomeruli demonstrates glomerular apoE enrichment, providing molecular confirmation of deposit composition (PMID: 38769490).

"Liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry of laser-microdissected glomeruli from paraffin sections revealed an increase in ApoE." — PMID: 38769490

Differential diagnosis. Amyloidosis (Congo-red negative in LPG), thrombotic microangiopathy, cryoglobulinemic GN, fibronectin glomerulopathy, histiocytic glomerular lipidosis (e.g., macrophage activation syndrome), and apoE2-homozygote glomerulopathy (foam-cell rich) (PMID: 31874799; PMID: 37936128).

Screening. Cascade family screening (APOE sequencing ± urinalysis) is appropriate given autosomal-dominant inheritance; many carriers are asymptomatic (PMID: 41612256).

11. Outcome / Prognosis

  • Renal survival: Guarded — ~50% progress to ESKD within 1–27 years of onset (PMID: 38769490).
  • Prognostic factors: Degree of proteinuria control is a key modifiable prognostic factor. In an 8-patient cohort, patients with >50% proteinuria reduction had slower renal decline: eGFR change −5.0 ± 0.8 vs +1.5 ± 0.7 mL/min/1.73 m² reduction rate (i.e., treated-responders declined more slowly) (PMID: 32441489). Variants in the LDL-receptor-binding region were associated with higher blood pressure and higher serum apoE.
  • Transplant outcome: LPG recurs after kidney transplantation (early and late relapses documented), because the pathogenic apoE is circulating (PMID: 40306438).
  • Quality of life / mortality: No formal QoL or disease-specific mortality statistics; morbidity is driven by nephrotic syndrome and ESKD.

"it may recur after kidney transplantation" — PMID: 40306438

"LPG patients with urine protein reduced more than 50% had a slower decrease in renal function than those with less urine protein reduction." — PMID: 32441489

12. Treatment

LPG is not steroid-responsive; immunosuppression is ineffective (PMID: 42417997). The mainstay is lipid-lowering therapy plus antiproteinuric RAAS blockade.

Representative responses: a boy with apoE-Tokyo/Maebashi had urine protein turn negative within 1 week on fenofibrate + telmisartan (PMID: 39993083); complete resolution with fenofibrate was reported from Pakistan (PMID: 38327135); and sustained benefit with statin monotherapy over 3 years in a young Chinese man with apoE-Kyoto (PMID: 30685233). In normolipidemic LPG, lipid-lowering may be withheld and ARB used alone (PMID: 42309892).

Advanced/experimental therapeutics. No approved gene, cell, or RNA-based therapy exists. Because the disease is driven by a circulating misfolded protein, therapies targeting apoE production or conformation are conceptually attractive but remain experimental. Early initiation of fibrate therapy is emphasized to prevent renal dysfunction (PMID: 24149835).

"the administration of fibrate with the intensive control of triglyceride and apolipoprotein E particularly from the early phase will ameliorate LPG and prevent renal dysfunction." — PMID: 24149835

13. Prevention

  • Primary prevention: Not possible for mutation carriers (genetic); focus is on genetic counseling given autosomal-dominant inheritance.
  • Secondary prevention: Cascade family screening (APOE sequencing plus urinalysis/lipid profile) to identify carriers early; early lipid-lowering therapy in affected carriers to preserve renal function (PMID: 41612256; PMID: 24149835).
  • Tertiary prevention: Proteinuria control (fibrates + RAAS blockade), blood-pressure control, and standard CKD management to delay ESKD.
  • Genetic counseling: Important for at-risk relatives, prenatal/reproductive planning, and interpretation of incomplete penetrance (asymptomatic carriers are common).
  • Immunization / public-health / environmental interventions: Not applicable (non-infectious, non-environmental disease).

14. Other Species / Natural Disease

  • Taxonomy / orthologs: Human APOE (NCBI Gene 348; NCBI Taxon 9606). The mouse ortholog is Apoe (NCBI Gene 11816; NCBI Taxon 10090). No naturally occurring LPG analog is established in companion animals or wildlife.
  • Comparative biology: Mouse apoE biology closely parallels human lipoprotein clearance, enabling the transgenic/adenoviral models below. Human apoE isoform structure determines VLDL clearance and atherosclerosis risk in humanized mice, underscoring evolutionary conservation of apoE function (PMID: 10359567).
  • Zoonotic potential: None (genetic disease).

Phenotype recapitulation. Models reproduce the hallmark glomerular lipoprotein thrombi and confirm the pathogenic role of mutant apoE. Important control: aged Apoe⁻/⁻ mice develop superficially similar dilated-lumen lesions, but these contain proteins/fibrils rather than lipid, distinguishing spontaneous knockout lesions from true mutant-apoE-driven LPG (PMID: 19459027).

Model limitations. Adenoviral expression is transient and non-germline; models do not fully recapitulate chronic progression to ESKD. No germline knock-in mouse for human LPG variants is established in the reviewed literature.

In vitro models. Recombinant apoE3-based mutants (R145P/Sendai, R147P/Chicago, R158P/Osaka, and non-proline R25C/Kyoto) characterized biophysically establish the destabilization/aggregation mechanism (PMID: 23110818; PMID: 30309894).

Upstream vs downstream. The APOE variant and apoE misfolding/aggregation are upstream; lipoprotein-thrombus deposition and glomerular capillary occlusion are intermediate; proteinuria, CKD, and ESKD are downstream. The disease unifies a spectrum of apoE-related glomerular disorders: LPG (heterozygous LDL-R–region variants, lamellated thrombi, no foam cells) is distinct from apoE2-homozygote glomerulopathy (glomerulosclerosis with foam-cell infiltration) and apoE-Toyonaka (Ser197Cys)+apoE2/2 (membranous-like), yet all converge on apoE conformational abnormality and renal lipid accumulation (PMID: 31874799).

Limitations and Knowledge Gaps

  1. No formal epidemiology. Prevalence and incidence are not quantified; estimates rest on cumulative case counts (>200 worldwide). No Orphanet prevalence class exists.
  2. Ascertainment bias. The heavy East-Asian representation partly reflects founder variants and may be amplified by awareness/reporting bias; true global distribution is uncertain.
  3. Mechanistic branch not fully resolved. The relative contributions of apoE misfolding/aggregation versus impaired macrophage lipoprotein clearance to thrombus formation remain to be dissected in vivo.
  4. Model limitations. Available mouse models use transient adenoviral (non-germline) expression and do not fully recapitulate chronic ESKD progression; no validated germline knock-in model is reported.
  5. Penetrance modifiers unknown. The genetic/epigenetic/environmental factors explaining incomplete penetrance and normolipidemic phenotypes are not identified.
  6. Treatment evidence is low-level. Efficacy data come from case reports and small series, not randomized trials; optimal agent (fibrate vs statin vs pemafibrate), timing, and genotype-specific response are not established.
  7. No QoL/mortality data. Disease-specific quality-of-life and mortality metrics are absent.

Proposed Follow-up Experiments / Actions

  1. Germline knock-in mouse models of recurrent human variants (apoE-Sendai Arg145Pro, apoE-Kyoto) to study chronic progression to ESKD and test therapeutics longitudinally.
  2. Prospective international LPG registry with standardized genotype, histology, serum apoE, and renal-outcome capture to establish prevalence, natural history, and genotype–phenotype correlations (including normolipidemic variants).
  3. Randomized or well-controlled comparative studies of fibrates (incl. pemafibrate) vs statins vs combination with RAAS blockade, with proteinuria and eGFR-slope endpoints, stratified by variant and baseline lipids.
  4. Mechanistic dissection of the macrophage-clearance branch (e.g., macrophage-specific manipulation in reconstituted Apoe⁻/⁻ mice) to clarify why thrombi form without foam cells.
  5. apoE-targeted therapeutic exploration — small molecules/chaperones that stabilize the apoE N-terminal domain, or RNA-based approaches lowering mutant apoE — given that circulating pathogenic apoE drives disease and post-transplant recurrence.
  6. Routine cascade APOE screening protocols for relatives of probands, paired with early lipid-lowering intervention trials in asymptomatic carriers to test secondary prevention.
  7. Structural/biophysical profiling of newer variants (e.g., apoE-Kanto Asp151dup) to confirm the generality of the destabilization-aggregation model across variant classes (missense, deletion, duplication).
