U4 and U6 snRNA Gene-Related Retinitis Pigmentosa: Research Report

Causal genes: RNU4-2 (encodes U4 snRNA) and four RNU6 paralogs, RNU6-1, RNU6-2, RNU6-8 and RNU6-9 (each encodes U6 snRNA). Category: Mendelian, autosomal dominant, non-coding RNA genes.

What this report rests on. - Nearly everything here comes from one research group's work (Quinodoz, Rivolta and colleagues), in two versions: - a 2025 preprint, PMID:39830270 (PMC11741465); - the peer-reviewed paper, Nature Genetics 2026;58(1):169–179, PMID:41513982, DOI 10.1038/s41588-025-02451-4. - I read the preprint full text through PMC's BioC service. For the published paper I saw only the abstract. Variant counts come from the preprint unless marked otherwise, and the published counts are higher. - Abstract wording below is paraphrased or quoted in short fragments. Before any snippet goes into a KB evidence item, fetch it with just fetch-reference and check it. - I found no independent replication cohort. Genomics England PanelApp is the only external expert review.

1. Disease Information

Overview. - This is non-syndromic retinitis pigmentosa (RP) caused by heterozygous variants in RNU4-2 or in four of the five active RNU6 paralogs. - These genes make the U4 and U6 small nuclear RNAs (snRNAs). The two pair up into the U4/U6 duplex inside the U4/U6.U5 tri-snRNP, a core part of the major spliceosome. - The preprint abstract says the variants "invariably cluster in close proximity within the three-way junction" of the U4/U6 duplex (PMID:39830270). - That region binds the tri-snRNP proteins PRPF3, PRPF8 and PRPF31. All three are already known causes of autosomal dominant RP (adRP). So this disease extends the existing "splicing-factor RP" class from proteins to the snRNAs themselves.

2. Etiology

What causes it - The cause is a heterozygous germline variant in RNU4-2 or in RNU6-1, -2, -8 or -9. The variant is either inherited or arises de novo. - The variants are small insertions or single-nucleotide changes, all at the U4/U6 three-way junction (see Section 4).

Genetic risk factors - The causal variants are the only known genetic risk factor. I found no data on modifier loci. - Penetrance is incomplete (Section 9), so modifiers may exist, but none have been identified.

Environmental risk factors: none described. This is a monogenic disease.

Protective factors - None established. - One finding may matter here. RNU6-7, the fifth active U6 paralog, carries no recurrent pathogenic variants. The same n.55_56insG insertion turns up in RNU6-7 in population controls of African ancestry (gnomAD allele frequency 0.014%; All of Us 0.013%). - The authors propose that RNU6-7 is only minimally incorporated into the spliceosome. This explains why the same variant in that paralog is tolerated; it is not a protective factor in the usual sense (PMID:39830270).

Gene–environment interactions: none reported.

Characteristics - Onset: adolescence to early adulthood. This is earlier than most other adRP forms. - Severity: variable. Penetrance is incomplete. - Course: progressive.

Not part of this disease - Neurodevelopmental features. These are absent. RP-causing variants give non-syndromic retinal disease, whereas different RNU4-2 variants cause ReNU syndrome (see Section 4). - Lab abnormalities. None.

Quality of life: no disease-specific quality-of-life data. Extrapolating from RP in general, expect loss of night and peripheral vision, mobility limits and later reading impairment. Cataract and cystoid macular edema add treatable reductions in vision.

4. Genetic / Molecular Information

Causal genes. HGNC IDs must be looked up with runoak/HGNC; I am not quoting them from memory. Use lowercase hgnc: in the KB. - RNU4-2: a U4 snRNA gene on chromosome 12q24.31. It is the same gene that causes ReNU syndrome. - RNU6-1, RNU6-2, RNU6-8, RNU6-9: U6 snRNA paralogs. - RNU6-7: no pathogenic variants. - RNU4-1 (the other U4 paralog, which lies next to RNU4-2): no pathogenic variants at the two RP hotspots, and 63 likely-benign variants.

Classification and population data - Uncertain or benign variants (preprint): - RNU4-2: 10 VUS and 17 benign variants. - RNU6 paralogs: 20 VUS and 70 benign variants. - Benign controls that sit right next to the hotspots: - U6 n.55_56insT, with a cumulative gnomAD frequency of 0.12%; - U6 n.57T>G, seen in 21 unaffected people. - These show that the exact base inserted matters, not just its position. - ClinVar / ClinGen: I did not check ClinVar entries. There is no ClinGen gene-disease validity curation that I could find, so leave gene_disease_validity empty. - Constraint (gnomAD v4.1): cumulative allele frequency is 1.2% for RNU4-2 against 20.4% for RNU4-1. RNU4-2 is therefore far less tolerant of variation.

Functional consequence - The variants are thought to act by a dominant-negative or gain-of-function mechanism, not haploinsufficiency (see Section 6). - For functional_impact_category, DOMINANT_NEGATIVE is the authors' leading interpretation. It is inferred from co-IP data, not proven.

Modifiers, epigenetics, chromosomal changes - None reported. - Chromatin data (ATAC-seq and H3K27ac) show active marks at RNU4-1/-2 and at RNU6-1, -2, -7, -8 and -9. Of the 105 U4 and 1,312 U6 pseudogenes, only RNU4-8P had active marks, which the authors attribute to its proximity to the ACTR1B promoter. - This explains why only these few loci cause disease.

How this differs from ReNU syndrome (RNU4-2 neurodevelopmental disorder) - Variant location: ReNU variants (e.g., n.64_65insT) sit in stem III, a separate region of the duplex; RP variants sit at the three-way junction. - Transmission: ReNU variants are mostly de novo, and when inherited they come mostly through the mother. RP variants are mostly inherited, from either parent. - Frequency: ReNU accounts for about 0.4% of neurodevelopmental disorder cases. - In the co-IP assay, the ReNU variant did not produce the increased SART3/PRPF31 binding seen with RP variants (PMID:39830270). - Curation point: keep this RP entry separate from ReNU. They are allelic but distinct diseases.

5. Environmental Information

Not applicable. There are no environmental, lifestyle or infectious causes.

6. Mechanism / Pathophysiology

Causal chain

  1. A heterozygous variant arises — an insertion or substitution at the U4/U6 three-way junction (U4 n.18_19insA or n.56T>C; U6 n.55_56insG or n.56_57insG). It is present in every cell, but only some cells take part in the disease.
  2. This changes the duplex shape. It alters base-pairing in stem-I or stem-II, or the stem orientation of the U4/U6 duplex at the junction of stem-I, stem-II and the U4 5′ stem-loop. Evidence: structural modelling (computational).
  3. This disturbs the binding surface for PRPF31, PRPF3 and PRPF8. In tri-snRNP structures, PRPF31 makes hydrogen bonds with 8 duplex residues in this region and PRPF3 with 3. Evidence: computational, from published cryo-EM structures.
  4. Mutant snRNPs stall at the U4/U6 di-snRNP stage.
  5. MS2-tagged mutant U4 or U6 expressed in cultured cells pulls down more SART3 and PRPF31 (di-snRNP markers), and somewhat more SNRNP200 (a U5 marker).
  6. Benign controls and the ReNU variant do not do this.
  7. Evidence: in vitro (the preprint text does not name the cell line).
  8. Inferred: slower recycling or assembly of the tri-snRNP. Because the mutant RNAs sit alongside normal ones, the effect is dominant-negative or gain-of-function.
  9. Inferred: spliceosome capacity becomes limiting in high-demand cells. No splicing defect has been shown, either globally or for specific transcripts. The authors suggest RP variants are milder than ReNU variants, delaying assembly without measurable pre-mRNA processing defects.
  10. Inferred: rod photoreceptors degenerate, then cones. This is the same tissue selectivity seen in PRPF31-, PRPF8- and PRPF3-related RP, and is thought to reflect the very high transcriptional and splicing demand of photoreceptors and the RPE.
  11. Retina and RPE express much more of these RNAs than choroid: RNU4 about 2.45-fold and RNU6 about 6.24-fold higher.
  12. Clinical result: night blindness, then loss of peripheral field, then late loss of central vision. Cystoid macular edema and cataract are frequent extras.

Where the chain branches - The disease is either RNU4-2-driven (2 variants) or RNU6-driven (2 variants across 4 paralogs). Both converge on the same assembly intermediate. - Whether the primary cell injured is the photoreceptor or the RPE is not settled. In PRPF31 RP the RPE is thought to be affected early.

8. Temporal Development

  • Onset: insidious, in adolescence to early adulthood. It is earlier than most adRP forms.
  • Progression:
  • The course runs from night blindness, to constricted visual fields, to loss of central vision.
  • The pace is slow; central vision is lost late, "well beyond reproductive age".
  • The disease lasts for life.
  • Remission: none.
  • Critical windows for intervention:
  • before photoreceptors are lost, for any future therapy;
  • monitoring for cystoid macular edema, which is common and treatable.

9. Inheritance and Population

  • Inheritance: autosomal dominant (HP:0000006). There are frequent de novo cases.
  • In the preprint, 67% of cases were inherited, from either parent.
  • De novo origin was confirmed in 7 cases and suspected in 12 more pedigrees.
  • The somatic-mosaicism term does not apply to this disease.
  • Penetrance: incomplete.
  • 9 obligate carriers had no visual symptoms (4 of them deceased).
  • Non-penetrance was seen in 5 of 13 informative families (38%).
  • Of the living asymptomatic carriers who were examined, one was subclinically affected and one had no signs; three were not evaluated.
  • Expressivity: variable.
  • Anticipation: none. These are not repeat-expansion variants.
  • Founder effects: not described. The U6 n.55_56insG variant recurs in many unrelated families of diverse ancestry, which points to a mutational hotspot, not a founder.
  • Frequency among RP patients:
  • Preprint estimate: up to about 1.2% of undiagnosed RP and about 2.7% of undiagnosed adRP families. The basis was roughly 5,000 RP cases, about 40% undiagnosed, with about 30% of RP being adRP.
  • Published estimate: up to about 1.4% of otherwise undiagnosed RP.
  • Population prevalence: not estimated.
  • Parent RP prevalence is about 1 in 4,000. Multiplying this by the diagnostic-yield fraction would be an inference, not a sourced number. Do not enter a rate_per_100000.
  • Sex ratio: not reported. Expect it to be equal for an autosomal disease.

10. Diagnostics

Genetic testing - Exome sequencing and most standard RP panels miss these variants. snRNA genes are not exons, and the RNU6 paralogs are short, multi-copy and hard to map uniquely. - Recommended: - genome sequencing with snRNA loci deliberately analysed; - or targeted sequencing of RNU4-2 and RNU6-1/2/8/9, now on PanelApp's retinal disorders panel 307; - reanalysis of genome data from unsolved adRP and sporadic RP cases. - Read-mapping: U6 has more than 1,300 pseudogenes, so mapping must be paralog-aware, and confirmation (e.g., by Sanger sequencing with paralog-specific primers) is prudent. - Not useful here: chromosomal microarray, karyotype, FISH and mtDNA testing.

Clinical tests - full-field ERG (rod-cone pattern); - fundus photography and autofluorescence; - OCT (cystoid macular edema, ellipsoid zone loss); - visual fields (Goldmann or static); - slit-lamp exam for cataract. - Possible NCIT diagnosis terms: electroretinography, optical coherence tomography (look up CURIEs; do not use from memory).

Biomarkers: none.

Differential diagnosis - other adRP genes — especially PRPF31 (which shares incomplete penetrance), PRPF8, PRPF3, SNRNP200 and RHO; - ReNU syndrome (has neurodevelopmental features); - syndromic RP (e.g., Usher, Bardet–Biedl); - phenocopies (drug toxicity, autoimmune retinopathy).

Screening: cascade testing of relatives. Unaffected carriers need counselling because of incomplete penetrance.

11. Outcome / Prognosis

  • Life expectancy: normal. No mortality is attributable to the disease.
  • Morbidity: progressive visual disability, with central vision loss late in life. Cystoid macular edema (around 56%) and cataract (around 24%) cause additional, treatable vision loss.
  • Prognostic factors: none established. Comparisons by variant or gene (RNU4-2 vs RNU6) have not been reported.

Experimental therapy and trials - No trials target this disease. - Gene augmentation is conceptually unsuitable for a dominant-negative non-coding RNA. Allele-specific knockdown or editing of the mutant snRNA copy is a theoretical approach, but none has been reported. - Mutation-independent approaches tested in RP generally (e.g., optogenetics, neuroprotection) could in principle apply.

Pharmacogenomics: not applicable.

13. Prevention

  • Primary: genetic counselling. Each child of a carrier has a 50% chance of inheriting the variant, with incomplete penetrance. Prenatal testing or preimplantation genetic testing is possible once the familial variant is known.
  • Secondary: cascade testing of relatives. Carriers should be monitored by ophthalmology.
  • Tertiary: regular OCT to catch cystoid macular edema, and timely cataract surgery.

14. Other Species

  • Natural animal disease: none known. I found no entries in OMIA (Online Mendelian Inheritance in Animals).
  • Conservation: U4 and U6 snRNAs, and the three-way-junction proteins PRPF31, PRPF3 and PRPF8, are highly conserved across eukaryotes from yeast to human. The U6 sequence is among the most conserved snRNAs.

15. Model Organisms

  • No animal or iPSC models of RNU4-2 or RNU6 RP have been reported.
  • Only functional model so far: ectopic expression of tagged mutant snRNA in cultured cells, followed by co-IP (in vitro; cell line not named in the preprint).
  • Related models that could serve as proxies, with caveats:
  • Prpf31 mouse models show weak retinal phenotypes. This is a known human–model mismatch, and a HUMAN_MODEL_MISMATCH discussion would be appropriate.
  • PRPF31 patient iPSC-derived RPE cells and retinal organoids do recapitulate splicing-factor RP. Equivalent organoids carrying snRNA variants would be the natural next model.
  • Practical difficulty: RNU6 is multi-copy, so engineering one paralog is hard to do in mouse. Mouse U6 gene organisation differs from human, which is a species-mismatch caveat.
