Lepromatous Leprosy

1. Disease Information

2026-11-27
Claude Code Model: claude-haiku-5-5, claude-opus-4-8, claude-opus-5-5 57 citations Prompt: disease_pathophysiology_research.md f452f0a9c7e3

1. Disease Information

Overview. Lepromatous leprosy (LL) is the "low-resistance," multibacillary pole of the leprosy (Hansen's disease) clinical spectrum. It is a chronic granulomatous infection of skin and peripheral nerves caused by M. leprae, occurring in individuals who mount an ineffective cell-mediated immune response, permitting uncontrolled bacillary proliferation. The U.S. CDC describes the lepromatous pole as the end of the spectrum where "the patient has essentially no cell-mediated immunity to the infection and the bacilli multiply uncontrollably. The highest concentrations of bacilli are found in the skin and nerves" (CDC Clinical Overview of Leprosy).

Key identifiers (confirmed this session): - MONDO: MONDO:0005127 ("lepromatous leprosy"), a subtype of leprosy (SAIL Concept Library/MONDO). - ICD-10-CM: A30.5 (Disease Ontology DOID:10887; cross-referenced from MONDO). - MeSH: D015440 ("Leprosy, Lepromatous"), scope note: "A chronic communicable infection which is a principal or polar form of LEPROSY" (MeSH record D015440). - Disease Ontology: DOID:10887 (synonym "type L leprosy") (DO). - ICD-11: parent code 1B20 (Leprosy). (I did not retrieve the exact ICD-11 subcode for the lepromatous form in this session; do not treat a specific ICD-11 extension code as verified.)

Synonyms / alternative names: type L leprosy; lepromatous Hansen's disease; MeSH entry terms include "Leprosy, Cutaneous" and "Leprosy, Nodular" (MeSH D015440). In the Ridley–Jopling scheme the polar form is LL; borderline-lepromatous is BL; diffuse lepromatous leprosy (DLL) is a variant strongly associated with M. lepromatosis (CDC EID systematic review).

Information provenance: Content here is derived from aggregated disease-level resources (ontologies, WHO/CDC surveillance, systematic reviews, GWAS, lesion-transcriptomics studies), not from individual EHR records.

2. Etiology

Primary cause (infectious). The causal agent is Mycobacterium leprae, an acid-fast, obligate intracellular bacillus with extreme tropism for Schwann cells and macrophages and a doubling time of ~14 days; it cannot be grown in axenic culture. Its genome has undergone massive reductive evolution: Cole et al. sequenced a 3.27-Mb genome (vs. 4.41 Mb in M. tuberculosis) containing ~1,604 protein-coding genes and ~1,116 pseudogenes (vs. 6 in M. tuberculosis), with downsizing attributed to recombination between dispersed repeats (Cole et al., Nature 2001, PMID 11234002). This gene decay underlies the obligate intracellular lifestyle, loss of many catabolic/respiratory functions, and culture failure (Genome Biology spotlight).

A second species, Mycobacterium lepromatosis, was identified in 2008 from two patients of Mexican origin who died of diffuse lepromatous leprosy and is strongly associated with DLL and Lucio's phenomenon (Han XY et al., Am J Clin Pathol 2008 — attribution via citing sources; the original abstract was not retrieved this session) (CDC EID systematic review 2023).

Why the lepromatous pole specifically. LL is not simply "infection"; it is the outcome in hosts whose immune response fails to contain the organism. This immunological determinant is the organizing principle of the whole spectrum (CDC).

Genetic risk factors (susceptibility; host, not causal)

Leprosy is one of the most heritable infectious diseases, and human genetics strongly influences both susceptibility and per-pole outcome: - PARK2/PACRG regulatory-region variants — identified by positional cloning in Vietnamese families and replicated in Brazil as common risk factors for leprosy (Mira et al., Nature 2004) (Nature 427:636-640). Replication is population-dependent (not confirmed in some Chinese/Indian samples). - NOD2, RIPK2, TNFSF15, CCDC122/C13orf31, HLA-DR, LRRK2 — Zhang et al. GWAS: variants in the NOD2-mediated innate-immunity signaling pathway confer susceptibility; associations at C13orf31, LRRK2, NOD2, RIPK2 were stronger in multibacillary than paucibacillary disease (i.e., tilt toward the lepromatous pole) (Zhang FR et al., N Engl J Med 2009;361:2609-18) (NEJM/JCI scholar). HLA-DR–DQ SNP rs602875 OR 0.67 (P=5.35×10⁻²⁷); NOD2 rs9302752 OR 1.59. - Additional loci: Liu et al. added BATF3, CCDC88B, CIITA-SOCS1 (three-stage Chinese GWAS, 8,313 cases/16,017 controls; "Discovery of six new susceptibility loci…", Nat Genet 2015) (Nat Genet ng.3212); Wang et al. added SYN2 (3p25.2), BBS9 (7p14.3), CTSB (8p23.1), MED30 (8q24.11) (Nat Commun 2016) (ncomms13760); IL23R/RAB32 were implicated earlier (2011). HLA-B/C region also appears (see §4 for its distinct role in drug hypersensitivity).

Environmental risk factors

  • Prolonged close/household contact with an untreated multibacillary case is the dominant exposure (transmission requires "prolonged, close contact … over many months") (CDC transmission page).
  • Zoonotic/animal reservoirs: nine-banded armadillos in the southern U.S. (M. leprae strain 3I-2-v1) — whole-genome evidence of near-identical strains in armadillos and locally-acquired U.S. patients (Truman RW et al., N Engl J Med 2011;364:1626-33, PMID 21524213) (PMC3138484); red squirrels in the British Isles (M. leprae and M. lepromatosis) (Avanzi C et al., Science 2016;354:744-747, PMID 27846605).
  • Sex: male predominance (~61% of patients male in a 32-study systematic review; males over-represented in multibacillary/lepromatous disease) (PMC6686771).
  • Lifestyle correlates of MB/lepromatous disease (higher alcohol use, hunting) reported in a 2025 NE-Brazil cohort (PMC10404493 table).

Protective factors

  • BCG vaccination: meta-analysis of experimental studies gives 26% protection (95% CI 14–37%); observational studies overestimate at 61%; a second BCG dose adds protection (Setia MS et al., Lancet Infect Dis 2006;6:162-70, PMID 16500597).
  • Single-dose rifampicin post-exposure prophylaxis (SDR-PEP): 57% reduction in incidence over 2 years among contacts (COLEP trial, Moet FJ et al., BMJ 2008;336:761-764) (PMC2287265).
  • Genetic protective alleles: protective-direction ORs at HLA-DR–DQ (rs602875 OR 0.67) (JCI scholar). I did not retrieve a dedicated gnomAD-based protective-variant analysis specific to LL this session.

Gene–environment interactions

The COLEP editorial noted a subgroup signal that chemoprophylaxis "is less effective in household contacts who are genetically related to the index case," i.e., shared host genetics modifies the environmental (exposure + prophylaxis) effect (BMJ editorial via PMC2287231). The core gene–environment axis is: innate-sensing genotype (NOD2 pathway, PARK2/PACRG) × M. leprae exposure → whether infection is cleared, contained (tuberculoid), or disseminates (lepromatous) (NEJM 2009; Nature 2004).

Reactional phenotypes (immunologic flares): - Erythema nodosum leprosum (ENL / Type 2 reaction): crops of painful subcutaneous nodules, fever, systemic inflammation; neutrophil/TNF-α/immune-complex driven. Affects a large fraction of LL patients — classic teaching ~50% of LL (and ~10% of BL), though a pooled analysis (Voorend) gives lower incidences of 15.4% in LL and 4.1% in BL (Walker/Lockwood review, Frontiers Immunol 2017). - Lucio's phenomenon: necrotizing vasculitis / type III hypersensitivity occurring in diffuse lepromatous leprosy, usually untreated, associated with M. lepromatosis (PMC4775876).

Quality-of-life impact: multibacillary patients are ~4× more likely to have physical (Grade 2) disability than paucibacillary patients (PMC6686771); disability drives stigma, loss of function, and employment impact. I did not retrieve LL-specific EQ-5D/SF-36 scores this session.

4. Genetic/Molecular Information

Because LL is infectious, "pathogenic variants" fall into two distinct categories:

(a) Host susceptibility/modifier loci (see §2): NOD2, RIPK2, TNFSF15, CCDC122, HLA-DRB1/DQ, LRRK2, PARK2/PACRG, BATF3, CCDC88B, CIITA-SOCS1, IL23R, RAB32, SYN2, BBS9, CTSB, MED30. These are germline common variants (mostly regulatory/intronic; some missense, e.g., IL23R rs76418789 G149R). The NOD2-pathway signal is enriched in MB/lepromatous disease (NEJM 2009; Nat Genet 2015). Functional consequence is altered innate antimycobacterial sensing/response rather than classical loss/gain-of-function of a single protein.

(b) Pharmacogenomic variant — clinically actionable: - HLA-B*13:01 is a strong risk factor for dapsone hypersensitivity syndrome (DHS) in leprosy patients (OR 20.53; P=6.84×10⁻²⁵; sensitivity 85.5%, specificity 85.7%) (Zhang FR et al., N Engl J Med 2013, PMID 24152261). Allele frequency ~2–20% in Chinese, 1–12% in Indians, ~2–4% in SE Asians, near-absent in Europeans/Africans. An Indonesian/Papua validation gave OR 233.64 (sensitivity 91.2%, specificity 96.2%) (PMC7592909). CPIC/PharmGKB-style use: pre-therapy screening. Suggested NCIT/pharmacogenomic annotation applies here.

(c) Pathogen genomics: M. leprae reductive genome with ~1,116 pseudogenes (Cole 2001, PMID 11234002); drug-resistance determinants are somatic-equivalent pathogen mutations in rpoB (rifampicin), folP1 (dapsone), gyrA (ofloxacin) — see §10/§12 (Cambau et al. WHO surveillance, Clin Microbiol Infect 2018).

Modifier genes: HLA-DR–DQ and the MB-enriched loci act as outcome modifiers pushing toward the lepromatous pole (NEJM 2009). LTA (lymphotoxin-α) was reported as a major risk factor for early-onset leprosy (Alcaïs et al., Nat Genet 2007, cited via Mira 2004 follow-up summary).

Epigenetics: A Brazilian leukocyte methylome study (GEO GSE197331, Illumina EPIC array, n=35) reported a "deregulated methylation profile" in leprosy vs. controls, but did not stratify by lepromatous subtype (OmicsDI GSE197331). LL-specific methylation data were not retrievable this session (reported as a gap).

Chromosomal abnormalities: Not applicable (infectious disease).

Suggested ontology/gene annotations: HGNC symbols above; GO: GO:0032495 (response to muramyl dipeptide / NOD2), GO:0045087 (innate immune response).

6. Mechanism / Pathophysiology

Ordered causal chain (initiating event → clinical manifestation)

  1. Inhalation/close-contact exposure to M. leprae (respiratory droplets) leads to entry and hematogenous dissemination, with tropism for cooler body sites (skin, superficial nerves, nasal mucosa) (CDC). (well-established clinically)
  2. In a host with permissive innate-sensing genotype (NOD2-pathway/PARK2–PACRG variants), M. leprae results in failure to trigger an effective Th1/antimicrobial program (NEJM 2009; Nature 2004). (genetic association; mechanism partly inferred)
  3. Lesional cytokine milieu shifts toward Th2/regulatory — IL-4, IL-5, IL-10 predominate in multibacillary lesions (vs. IFN-γ/IL-2 in tuberculoid), leading to suppressed macrophage killing (Yamamura/Modlin/Bloom, Science 1991;254:277-279) (meddean PDF). (human lesion mRNA, in situ)
  4. Type I interferon (IFN-β) program induces IL-10, which inhibits the IFN-γ–driven antimicrobial response in macrophages — demonstrated in disseminated (lepromatous) lesions (Teles RMB et al., Science 2013;339:1448-53) (PMC3671607). (human lesion + in vitro)
  5. Macrophage program diverges: IL-10 drives a phagocytic/scavenger program (CD209, CD163, scavenger receptors) that ingests mycobacteria and oxidized LDL but does not kill, whereas IL-15 drives the vitamin-D–dependent antimicrobial program; the phagocytic pathway predominates in progressive multibacillary disease (Montoya D et al., Cell Host Microbe 2009;6:343-353, PMID 19837374). (human lesion + in vitro) — this is the branch point between the lepromatous (phagocytic, non-killing) and tuberculoid/reversal (antimicrobial, killing) poles.
  6. Non-killing macrophages accumulate bacilli and host-derived oxidized phospholipids (e.g., PEIPC), becoming foamy macrophages ("lepra cells"); oxidized phospholipids further suppress innate immunity, reversible by normal HDL but not LL-patient HDL — a lipid pattern "strikingly similar to atherosclerosis" (Cruz D et al., J Clin Invest 2008;118:2917-28) (PMC2467381). (human lesion + in vitro)
  7. Unchecked bacillary load (high bacterial index) results in disseminated symmetric skin infiltration and high anti-PGL-I IgM titers (SciELO RSBMT).
  8. Nerve-damage branch: M. leprae binds ErbB2 receptor tyrosine kinase on myelinated Schwann cells, activating MEK→Erk1/2 leading to early demyelination independent of neuregulin (Tapinos N, Rambukkana A, Nat Med 2006;12:961-966) (nm1433 PDF); in vivo (zebrafish), PGL-1 reprograms infected macrophages to overproduce nitric oxide (iNOS), and reactive nitrogen species injure axonal mitochondria → demyelination, even before glial infection (Madigan CA et al., Cell 2017) (PMC5848073). (in vitro + model organism)
  9. Niche-expansion branch: M. leprae reprograms adult Schwann cells to a progenitor/stem-like (pSLC), mesenchymal state, which secrete chemokines and disseminate bacteria to other tissues (Masaki T et al., Cell 2013;152:51-67 — via Nature news summary; original abstract not retrieved this session) (Nature news). (model organism/in vitro)
  10. Progressive sensory neuropathy → painless trauma, secondary infection, deformity and disability; late infiltration leads to leonine facies, madarosis, saddle-nose, hypogonadism (DermNet).
  11. Reactional branches: immune-complex deposition + neutrophil/TNF-α influx causes ENL (Type 2); in DLL, necrotizing vasculitis causes Lucio's phenomenon (Frontiers Immunol 2017; PMC4775876).

Upstream vs downstream: Upstream = host innate-sensing genotype + IFN-β/IL-10 axis → macrophage program divergence (steps 2–5). Downstream = foamy-macrophage bacterial persistence, nerve demyelination, dissemination, disability (steps 6–10). Reactions (step 11) are superimposed immunologic events.

Cell types (CL) involved: macrophage (CL:0000235), foamy/lepra macrophage, Schwann cell (CL:0002573), T cell (CL:0000084), keratinocyte, fibroblast, neutrophil (CL:0000775, in ENL). GO processes: GO:0045087 (innate immune response), GO:0032606 (type I interferon production), GO:0032623/IL-10 signaling, GO:0002244 regulation; demyelination GO:0032289. Pathways: NOD-like receptor signaling (KEGG hsa04621), vitamin-D antimicrobial pathway, ErbB/MAPK (Erk1/2), IFN/IL-10 signaling.

7. Anatomical Structures Affected

  • Primary organs: skin (UBERON:0002097) and peripheral nerves (UBERON:0000044 peripheral nervous system); nasal mucosa (UBERON:0001707). Highest bacillary concentrations are in skin and nerves (CDC).
  • Secondary/system involvement: eyes (uveitis/keratitis; UBERON:0000970), testes (UBERON:0000473; hypogonadism), upper respiratory tract, reticuloendothelial system (liver/spleen in heavy multibacillary load), bone (facial/nasal destruction).
  • Tissue/cell level: dermal macrophages become foamy "lepra cells"; Schwann cells of peripheral nerves are the signature neural target (demyelination, reprogramming) (Nat Med 2006; Cell 2013).
  • Subcellular (GO Cellular Component): bacilli reside in macrophage phagosome/phagolysosome (GO:0045335); axonal mitochondria (GO:0005739) are the injury target in PGL-1/NO-mediated nerve damage (Cell 2017).
  • Localization/lateralization: LL is characteristically symmetric and bilateral/diffuse (contrasting with the asymmetric, localized tuberculoid pole), with predilection for cooler acral and facial sites (CDC).

8. Temporal Development

  • Onset: adult-onset most common; long incubation, longer for lepromatous/MB disease (~10 years average for MB vs ~4 years for PB per NSW guideline; ranges 9 months–20+ years across sources) (NSW Health leprosy control guideline). Onset pattern is insidious/chronic.
  • Progression: untreated LL is slowly progressive with uncontrolled bacillary multiplication; chronic lifelong without treatment (CDC).
  • Course pattern: chronic-progressive baseline, punctuated by episodic reactions (ENL/Type 2; Lucio's). Reversal reactions represent upgrading from the lepromatous toward the tuberculoid pole, spontaneously or during chemotherapy, with acquisition of effective antimicrobial responses (Ma F et al., Nat Immunol 2021;22:839-850, DOI 10.1038/s41590-021-00956-8).
  • Remission: treatment-induced bacteriologic cure with MDT; nerve damage and deformity may be irreversible. Critical intervention window is before fixed nerve damage (WHO treatment page).

9. Inheritance and Population

  • Epidemiology: WHO reported 172,717 new leprosy cases globally in 2024 (9,397 children; 9,157 with Grade-2 disability; −5.5% vs 2023's 182,815); 188 countries reporting (WHO WER "Global leprosy update 2024"; figures via ILEP summary). Burden concentrated in SE-Asia > Americas > Africa (African Region 19,171 cases, 11.1%) (WHO AFRO). The lepromatous/MB pole is a subset of this total. (I did not retrieve LL-specific global prevalence or GBD DALY figures this session — reported as a gap.)
  • Inheritance: Not Mendelian. Susceptibility is multifactorial/polygenic with strong host-genetic and HLA contributions; pole determination (lepromatous vs tuberculoid) is itself partly genetically modified (NEJM 2009; Nature 2004).
  • Penetrance/expressivity: most infected individuals never develop disease; among those who do, the clinical pole varies — i.e., highly incomplete penetrance and variable expressivity of susceptibility genotypes.
  • Consanguinity/founder: COLEP data suggest genetic relatedness to index case modifies risk/prophylaxis response (BMJ editorial).
  • Demographics: male predominance (~61% overall; males over-represented in lepromatous/MB disease; ~2:1 in some series) (PMC6686771; Nigeria study PMID 12449892).
  • Geographic: endemic in India, Brazil, Indonesia and other tropical regions; autochthonous zoonotic cases in southern U.S. (armadillo-linked) (Truman 2011, PMID 21524213). M. lepromatosis is concentrated in the Americas (Mexico) but also reported in Asia (CDC EID review).

10. Diagnostics

  • Clinical/laboratory:
  • Slit-skin smear with Ziehl-Neelsen/Fite stain → high bacterial index defines multibacillary/lepromatous disease; WHO notes slit-skin smears as a common technique (WHO 2018 guidelines summary).
  • Skin biopsy / histopathology: dermal foamy macrophages (lepra cells) packed with acid-fast bacilli, grenz zone, scant lymphocytes (contrast with tuberculoid epithelioid granulomas).
  • Serology: anti-PGL-I IgM seropositive in ~80–100% of LL patients and correlates with bacterial index; useful for bacterial-load monitoring but not for paucibacillary detection (SciELO RSBMT review; PMC96023).
  • PCR for M. leprae/M. lepromatosis DNA (species discrimination, drug-resistance genotyping).
  • Genetic/resistance testing (pathogen): PCR-sequencing of rpoB (rifampicin), folP1 (dapsone), gyrA (ofloxacin) drug-resistance-determining regions — WHO surveillance network (Cambau 2018, PMC6286419).
  • Host pharmacogenetic testing: HLA-B*13:01 screening before dapsone to prevent DHS (Zhang 2013, PMID 24152261).
  • Clinical criteria: WHO cardinal signs — hypopigmented/reddish anesthetic skin lesion, thickened peripheral nerve, and/or positive slit-skin smear; operational classification MB vs PB. Ridley–Jopling classifies the immunologic spectrum (TT–BT–BB–BL–LL) (Ridley DS, Jopling WH, Int J Lepr 1966;34:255-73 — PMID 5950347; title only, no abstract available).
  • Differential diagnosis: other causes of nodular/infiltrative dermatoses, cutaneous leishmaniasis, sarcoidosis, mycosis fungoides, syphilis, neurofibromatosis.
  • Screening: WHO does not recommend testing asymptomatic people; contact tracing + SDR-PEP is the operational approach (WHO 2018 guidelines).

11. Outcome / Prognosis

  • Survival/mortality: leprosy itself is rarely directly fatal with treatment; mortality is driven by complications and by treatment of reactions. In an Ethiopian ALERT cohort, ENL carried significant mortality (8 ENL deaths vs 2 Type-1 reaction deaths, p=0.0168), attributed largely to chronic corticosteroid therapy (Walker SL et al., PLoS Negl Trop Dis 2014) (PMC3953021).
  • Morbidity/disability: multibacillary/lepromatous disease → ~4× odds of Grade-2 physical disability vs paucibacillary (PMC6686771); irreversible nerve damage, deformity, blindness, stigma.
  • Recovery: MDT achieves bacteriologic cure; nerve/function deficits present at diagnosis are often permanent, making early treatment the key prognostic lever.
  • Prognostic factors: bacterial index/anti-PGL-I titer (load), extent of nerve involvement at diagnosis, occurrence of reactions, drug resistance. Drug resistance (rifampicin in 5.1% of relapse / 2.0% of new MB cases in WHO survey) worsens prognosis (Cambau 2018).
  • Complications: recurrent ENL, Lucio's phenomenon (in DLL), secondary infection of anesthetic limbs, ocular damage. GBD DALY figures not retrieved this session (gap).

12. Treatment

Standard WHO multidrug therapy (MDT) — for multibacillary/lepromatous disease: rifampicin + dapsone + clofazimine for 12 months (three-drug regimen now recommended for all patients; PB = 6 months) (WHO 2018 guidelines; WHO NTD treatment page). - Rifampicin (NCIT: rifampin; bactericidal, RNA polymerase/rpoB), dapsone (dihydrofolate/folate pathway, folP1), clofazimine (anti-inflammatory riminophenazine). - Pharmacogenomics: screen HLA-B*13:01 before dapsone (DHS risk) (Zhang 2013).

Drug-resistant disease: WHO recommends two of {clarithromycin, minocycline, a quinolone} + clofazimine ×6 months, then clofazimine + one ×18 months; avoid fluoroquinolone if ofloxacin resistance present (expert-opinion based) (WHO 2018 guidelines summary).

Reaction management: - ENL (Type 2): thalidomide rapidly controls ENL and is superior to aspirin/pentoxifylline, but is teratogenic and restricted (Walker SL, Waters MFR, Lockwood DNJ, Lepr Rev 2007) (LSHTM record; Mahmoud & Walker 2019 review); corticosteroids and clofazimine also used (steroid toxicity is a mortality driver, Walker 2014). - Type 1/reversal reaction: corticosteroids.

Advanced/experimental: No gene/cell/RNA therapies apply (infectious disease). Vaccines: BCG (partial protection, Setia 2006); LepVax (LEP-F1 + GLA-SE) subunit vaccine — Phase 1 safe and well tolerated, 24 volunteers (Vaccine 2020;38:1700-1707; PMID 31899025; trial NCT03302897); Phase 1b/2a NCT03947437; Phase 1b NCT06627257 (Brazil).

Supportive/rehabilitative: wound/ulcer care for anesthetic limbs, physiotherapy, reconstructive surgery for deformity, ophthalmologic care, disability prevention and stigma/psychosocial support.

Treatment strategy / outcomes: MDT is highly effective bacteriologically; adherence and early initiation (before nerve damage) determine functional outcome. Suggested NCIT terms: Rifampin, Dapsone, Clofazimine, Thalidomide, Minocycline, Clarithromycin, Ofloxacin, BCG Vaccine.

13. Prevention

  • Primary prevention:
  • Chemoprophylaxis (SDR-PEP): single-dose rifampicin to contacts ≥2 years → 57% reduction over 2 years (COLEP, Moet 2008); WHO recommends it for contacts (WHO 2018). Single-dose rifapentine gave an 84% reduction over 4 years in a Chinese cluster-RCT (Wang L et al., N Engl J Med 2023;388:1843-1852, PMID 37195940) — a notable advance over rifampicin's ~2-year effect.
  • BCG vaccination (~26% protection experimentally; second dose adds benefit) (Setia 2006).
  • Secondary prevention: contact tracing/screening, early diagnosis and prompt MDT to interrupt transmission and prevent disability. WHO does not recommend screening asymptomatic persons with a test.
  • Tertiary prevention: disability prevention (self-care, footwear, ulcer management), early reaction treatment, ocular protection.
  • Counseling/public health: contact surveillance, health education, stigma reduction; HLA-B*13:01 pharmacogenetic counseling before dapsone in high-frequency populations (Zhang 2013).
  • Caveat on PEP: cost-effectiveness is debated — Lockwood and colleagues argued SDR "protects those who need it least" (Nepalese commentary citing the debate, PMC8031444).

14. Other Species / Natural Disease

  • Taxonomy of hosts: Humans (Homo sapiens); nine-banded armadillo (Dasypus novemcinctus) — the principal non-human natural host and a zoonotic reservoir in the southern U.S. (Truman 2011, PMID 21524213); red squirrel (Sciurus vulgaris) in the British Isles (Avanzi 2016, PMID 27846605). (NCBI Taxon IDs for these hosts not re-queried this session.)
  • Natural disease/comparative pathology: armadillos develop extensive neurological involvement, uniquely among lab animals, with bacilli on progressively demyelinating Schwann cells and electrophysiologically demonstrable nerve deficits — making them the premier model of leprosy neuropathy (Sharma R, …, Truman R, Dis Model Mech 2013 "The armadillo: a model for the neuropathy of leprosy") (DMM dmm.010215); red squirrels show both overt and subclinical infection (Avanzi 2016).
  • Transmission/zoonosis: direct armadillo→human zoonotic transmission is supported by near-identical whole genomes; ~⅓ of U.S. patients report no foreign residence (Truman 2011). Squirrel→human risk is considered low (EPFL/press coverage of Avanzi 2016).
  • Evolutionary note: British M. leprae strains resemble medieval European strains; British/Irish M. lepromatosis diverged from Mexican human strains ~27,000 years ago (Avanzi 2016).

15. Model Organisms

  • Mouse footpad (Shepard, 1960): M. leprae replicates in the mouse footpad but infection stays localized without nerve involvement — the standard model for drug-susceptibility/viability testing, not neuropathy (DMM 2013 review). Nude/immunodeficient mice support higher bacterial yields.
  • Nine-banded armadillo: the key model recapitulating disseminated multibacillary disease and peripheral neuropathy; used for antigen/bacillus production, pathogenesis, and nerve-function-impairment intervention studies (DMM 2013; armadillo NFI model, PMC9259846). Limitations: outbred, long timelines, limited genetic tools, no inbred reagents.
  • Zebrafish (Danio rerio) larvae: transparent model used to show, in vivo, that infected macrophages (via PGL-1 → iNOS/NO) initiate demyelination, enabling real-time imaging of nerve damage (Madigan 2017, PMC5848073; ZFIN ZDB-PUB-170827-7).
  • In vitro / primary cells: human primary Schwann cells (ErbB2 demyelination model, Herceptin/PKI-166 blockade; Nat Med 2006); Schwann-cell reprogramming to pSLCs (Cell 2013); monocyte-derived macrophages for IL-10/IL-15 program divergence (Montoya 2009); human lesion single-cell + spatial transcriptomics defining the granuloma antimicrobial network and reversal reaction (Ma et al., Nat Immunol 2021; 21,318 cells/10 biopsies; DOI 10.1038/s41590-021-00956-8).
  • Phenotype recapitulation summary: armadillo = best for systemic + neural LL phenotype; mouse footpad = bacillary viability/drug testing only; zebrafish = early macrophage-driven nerve injury mechanism; human lesion omics = authentic immunologic poles.
  • Resources: MGI (mouse), ZFIN (zebrafish); no dedicated armadillo genetic-model database (natural-host colonies at NHDP/USA).

  • LL-subtype-stratified DNA-methylation data (only whole-leprosy methylome GSE197331 found).
