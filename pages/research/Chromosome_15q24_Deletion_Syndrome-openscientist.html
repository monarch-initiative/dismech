Chromosome 15q24 Deletion Syndrome (SIN3A): Comprehensive Disease Characteristics Report

Disease: Chromosome 15q24 Deletion Syndrome MONDO ID: MONDO:0013256 · OMIM: 613406 · Causal gene: SIN3A (SIN3 transcription regulator family member A; HGNC:19353; chromosome 15q24.2) Category: Chromosomal (contiguous-gene microdeletion syndrome)

Summary

Chromosome 15q24 deletion syndrome is a rare, usually de novo contiguous-gene microdeletion disorder of chromosome band 15q24. Its single most important, dosage-sensitive driver gene is SIN3A, which encodes the scaffold protein of a SIN3–HDAC1/2 histone-deacetylase transcriptional corepressor complex and is a MeCP2 interactor required for cortical neurogenesis. Critically, the disease named here is a SIN3A disorder: heterozygous loss-of-function point variants in SIN3A alone reproduce the core neurodevelopmental phenotype of the microdeletion and define the overlapping Witteveen–Kolk syndrome (WITKOS, OMIM 613406). This shared OMIM identity confirms that SIN3A haploinsufficiency explains the central cognitive and craniofacial features, while additional co-deleted genes (e.g., STRA6, NEO1, CYP11A1) account for the rarer multi-organ malformations seen only in larger deletions.

Clinically, the syndrome is characterized by prenatal/postnatal growth retardation, usually mild intellectual disability with prominent speech delay, microcephaly, short stature, and a distinctive facial gestalt (long face with high anterior hairline, hypertelorism, epicanthal folds, downslanting palpebral fissures, sparse broad medial eyebrows, broad/depressed nasal bridge, small mouth, long smooth philtrum, full lower lip). Variable features include digital/skeletal anomalies, male genital abnormalities (hypospadias, micropenis), hypotonia, behavior problems (ASD, ADHD), recurrent infections, and eye problems; rare but severe congenital malformations (structural brain anomalies, cardiac defects, congenital diaphragmatic hernia, intestinal atresia, imperforate anus, myelomeningocele) occur chiefly with larger deletions. Inheritance is autosomal dominant with high penetrance but highly variable expressivity.

There is no disease-modifying therapy. Diagnosis is by chromosomal microarray (for the deletion) or exome/genome sequencing (for intragenic SIN3A variants), supported by a robust, predominantly hypomethylated DNA-methylation episignature that is diagnostic for WITKOS/SIN3A haploinsufficiency. Management is supportive, anticipatory, and multidisciplinary. This report synthesizes 12 confirmed findings across 25 reviewed papers into a full disease-knowledge-base entry spanning etiology, phenotype, molecular mechanism, anatomy, temporal course, epidemiology, diagnostics, prognosis, treatment, prevention, and model systems.

Key Findings

1. SIN3A haploinsufficiency is the key dosage-sensitive driver (Finding F001)

The defining molecular insight linking the 15q24 microdeletion to a monogenic disorder came from Witteveen et al. (2016), who identified dominant loss-of-function mutations in SIN3A (chromosome 15q24.2) in individuals with mild intellectual disability and autism spectrum disorder who also shared "striking features, including facial dysmorphisms, microcephaly and short stature. This phenotype is highly related to that of individuals with atypical 15q24 microdeletions, linking SIN3A to this microdeletion syndrome" (PMID: 27399968). Balasubramanian et al. (2021) then characterized 28 patients with SIN3A loss-of-function variants (24 truncating, 3 missense, 1 in-frame gain), establishing that the gene-level disorder, now termed Witteveen–Kolk syndrome (OMIM 613406), "is a recently defined neurodevelopmental syndrome caused by heterozygous loss-of-function variants in SIN3A" (PMID: 33437032). Because the point-mutation disorder and the microdeletion share the same OMIM number (613406) and the same core phenotype, SIN3A is confirmed as the principal dosage-sensitive driver of the neurodevelopmental core of the 15q24 deletion syndrome.

2. A ~1.1 Mb critical region and segmental-duplication-mediated recurrence (Finding F002)

Mefford et al. (2012) characterized 15 patients carrying nine distinct deletions ranging 266 kb–3.75 Mb and showed that "the core cognitive features of the 15q24 microdeletion syndrome, including developmental delays and severe speech problems, are largely due to deletion of genes in a 1.1-Mb critical region" (PMID: 22180641). Recurrence arises from the local genomic architecture: "The majority of breakpoints lie within segmental duplication (SD) blocks. Low sequence identity and large intervals of unique sequence between SD blocks likely contribute to the rarity of 15q24 deletions, which occur 8-10 times less frequently than 1q21 or 15q13 microdeletions" (PMID: 22180641). Liu et al. (2020) further refined the region, proposing a 650-kb LCR15q24A-B interval (including NEO1) relevant to ASD, with the LCR B–D interval linked to developmental delay, dysmorphism, and recurrent infection (PMID: 31953991). The smallest region of overlap has been placed at ~1.2 Mb between LCR15q24B and LCR15q24C.

3. SIN3A scaffolds an HDAC1/2 corepressor complex and interacts with MeCP2 (Finding F003)

Mechanistically, SIN3A is the scaffold of the SIN3 core corepressor complex. Jayaprakash et al. (2021) define its composition: "The SIN3 core complex comprises the eponymous proteins SIN3A or SIN3B, the catalytically active proteins HDAC1 or HDAC2, the histone chaperone retinoblastoma-associated protein 46/retinoblastoma-binding protein 7 (RBAP46/RBBP7) or RBAP48/RBBP4, the SIN3-associated protein 30 (SAP30), and the suppressor of defective silencing 3 (SDS3)" (PMID: 33130996). The complex deacetylates histones to repress transcription. Witteveen et al. (2016) demonstrated the disease-relevant function: SIN3A interacts with MeCP2, and "in vivo functional knockdown of Sin3a led to reduced cortical neurogenesis, altered neuronal identity and aberrant corticocortical projections in the developing mouse brain" (PMID: 27399968). Germline deletion of the catalytic partner HDAC1 is embryonic-lethal in mouse and reduces Sin3A/NuRD/CoREST complex activity (PMID: 20404188), underscoring the essential nature of this corepressor machinery.

4. Clinical phenotype spectrum and dysmorphology (Finding F004)

Magoulas & El-Hattab (2012) provide the authoritative phenotype overview: the syndrome "is characterized by growth retardation, intellectual disability, and distinct facial features including long face with high anterior hairline, hypertelorism, epicanthal folds, downslanting palpebral fissures, sparse and broad medial eyebrows, broad and/or depressed nasal bridge, small mouth, long smooth philtrum, and full lower lip. Other common findings include skeletal and digital abnormalities, genital abnormalities in males, hypotonia, behavior problems, recurrent infections, and eye problems" (PMID: 22216833). Less frequent findings include hearing loss, growth hormone deficiency, hernias, and obesity. Importantly, "Congenital malformations, while rare, can be severe and include structural brain anomalies, cardiovascular malformations, congenital diaphragmatic hernia, intestinal atresia, imperforate anus, and myelomeningocele" (PMID: 22216833). The reported smallest region of overlap is ~1.2 Mb between LCR15q24B and LCR15q24C.

5. Mouse models: Sin3a is essential and regulates p53/Myc-Mad/E2F networks (Finding F005)

Dannenberg et al. (2005), using conditional mSin3A deletion, showed that "mSin3A was shown to play an essential role in early embryonic development and in the proliferation and survival of primary, immortalized, and transformed cells" (PMID: 15998811). Time-course profiling after deletion "revealed deregulation of genes involved in cell cycle regulation, DNA replication, DNA repair, apoptosis, chromatin modifications, and mitochondrial metabolism" (PMID: 15998811), operating through Myc-Mad, E2F, and p53 transcriptional networks, with mSin3A/HDAC mediating p53 deacetylation. The in-vivo brain knockdown by Witteveen et al. (reduced cortical neurogenesis, altered neuronal identity, aberrant corticocortical projections) provides the neurodevelopmental counterpart in a dosage-reduction context (PMID: 27399968).

6. A diagnostic DNA-methylation episignature (Finding F006)

Coenen-van der Spek et al. (2023) studied genome-wide DNA methylation in peripheral blood of 20 individuals with heterozygous SIN3A alterations and identified a novel, robust episignature. "A predominantly hypomethylated DNA methylation profile specific to WITKOS was identified, and the classifier model was able to diagnose a previously unresolved test case" (PMID: 36399132). The signature distinguishes WITKOS from controls and from other neurodevelopmental disorders with episignatures, is sensitive across degrees of phenotypic severity, and resolved a previously uncertain (VUS) case — making it a powerful functional diagnostic adjunct.

7. Structural basis of missense LOF and the SIN3B paralog syndrome (Finding F007)

Due et al. (2026) performed structural and thermodynamic studies of the Sin3A PAH1 (paired amphipathic helix 1) αα-hub domain, relating "αα-hub stability to function by structural and thermodynamic studies of the Sin3A-paired amphipathic helix 1 (PAH1) wt hub domain and two predicted loss-of-function variants (A126V and K155E) found in patients with the neurodevelopmental Witteveen-Kolk syndrome" (PMID: 41759735). This shows that WITKOS missense variants cause disease by destabilizing the protein-interaction hub, a loss-of-function mechanism. The dosage-sensitivity model is reinforced by the paralog: Latypova et al. (2021) showed that SIN3B (19p13.11) haploinsufficiency causes an overlapping syndrome "hallmarked by intellectual disability, developmental delay, and dysmorphic facial features with variably penetrant autism spectrum disorder, congenital malformations, corpus callosum defects, and impaired growth caused by disruptive SIN3B variants" (PMID: 33811806), with zebrafish sin3b disruption producing craniofacial defects.

8. Quantified neurobehavioral phenotype frequencies (Finding F008)

The largest systematically phenotyped cohort (Balasubramanian et al. 2021; n=28, 17 males, 11 females, mean age 8.2 y) provides frequency estimates. Among 16 patients ≥8 years assessed: "eight (50%) had mild intellectual disability (ID), four had moderate ID (22%), and one had severe ID (6%). Four (25%) did not have any cognitive impairment. Other neurological symptoms such as seizures (4/28) and hypotonia (12/28) were common" (PMID: 33437032). In patients ≥2 years, 3 had ASD and 4 had ADHD; growth and feeding difficulties were characteristic. 27 of 28 variants were novel (24 truncating, 3 missense, 1 large in-frame gain spanning exons 10–12).

9. Inheritance, etiology, and diagnostic approach (Finding F009)

15q24 microdeletions are typically de novo. Sharp et al. (2007) described "multiple individuals with mental retardation and overlapping de novo submicroscopic deletions of 15q24" (PMID: 17360722), arising through non-allelic homologous recombination (NAHR) between paralogous low-copy repeats. Because "Karyotypes are typically normal, and the deletions were detected in these individuals by array comparative genomic hybridization (aCGH)" (PMID: 22216833), chromosomal microarray is the first-line test for the deletion. Intragenic SIN3A point variants are predominantly de novo heterozygous loss-of-function (truncating) variants inherited in an autosomal dominant manner (PMID: 33437032; PMID: 27399968), detected by exome/genome sequencing or gene panels.

10. Supportive, multidisciplinary management (Finding F010)

No targeted or disease-modifying therapy exists; care is supportive and anticipatory. Balasubramanian et al. (2021) advise that "Involvement of a multidisciplinary team including a geneticist, paediatrician and neurologist should be considered in managing these patients" (PMID: 33437032). Interventions target individual manifestations: early developmental/speech/physical/occupational therapy for DD/ID; antiepileptic drugs for seizures; surgical repair of hypospadias, diaphragmatic hernia, intestinal atresia, imperforate anus, and congenital heart defects; management of recurrent infections; growth hormone evaluation for GH deficiency; and endocrine evaluation for pubertal delay/hypogonadotropic hypogonadism (Correa Brito et al. 2024, PMID: 38528912). Janssen et al. (2026) reported "the first documented treatment of behavioral problems in WITKOS," a personalized psychological intervention with improved functioning at follow-up (PMID: 41245232).

11. Anatomical involvement, neuroimaging, temporal course, prognosis (Finding F011)

Brain MRI in WITKOS "showed subtle abnormalities, including corpus callosum hypoplasia and ventriculomegaly" (PMID: 27399968). Onset is congenital/prenatal for structural malformations, with developmental delay manifesting in infancy/early childhood; the core neurodevelopmental phenotype is non-progressive/static. The primary affected organ is the brain (cerebral cortex, UBERON:0000956), with multi-system involvement of craniofacial skeleton, external genitalia (male), digits/skeleton, eyes, ears, and (rarely) heart, diaphragm, and gastrointestinal tract. No reduced life expectancy is established for isolated SIN3A-related disorder, although rare severe congenital malformations can cause early morbidity/mortality (PMID: 22216833).

12. Co-deleted genes contribute distinct organ phenotypes — a contiguous-gene mechanism (Finding F012)

Beyond SIN3A, the 15q24 critical region contains additional dosage/function-sensitive genes that plausibly account for non-neurodevelopmental features in larger deletions. STRA6 (stimulated by retinoic acid 6; a retinol/RBP4 receptor in vitamin A/retinoic-acid signaling) causes Matthew-Wood/PDAC syndrome (MCOPS9, MIM 601186) when biallelically mutated: "Mutations in retinoic acid 6 gene (STRA6) have been reported in clinically diagnosed patients with MWS" (PMID: 30204971), a syndrome with microphthalmia/anophthalmia, pulmonary hypoplasia, congenital diaphragmatic hernia, and cardiac defects — paralleling the rare eye, diaphragmatic hernia, and heart anomalies of the deletion. NEO1 (neogenin), within LCR15q24A-B, is proposed as ASD-relevant: "the ASD phenotype in this individual is potentially attributed by genes particularly NEO1 within the newly proposed critical region" (PMID: 31953991). CYP11A1 (cholesterol side-chain cleavage) relates to steroidogenesis and adrenal-gonadal function. A deletion case involving CYP1A1/EDC3 presented with poor immunity, laryngeal stridor, and lower-limb swelling (PMID: 34922566), further supporting multigene contributions.

Full Section-by-Section Report

1. Disease Information

Chromosome 15q24 deletion syndrome (MONDO:0013256; OMIM 613406) is a rare contiguous-gene microdeletion disorder caused by loss of a segment of chromosome band 15q24. Its central dosage-sensitive gene is SIN3A. The gene-level disorder produced by intragenic SIN3A loss-of-function variants is Witteveen–Kolk syndrome (WITKOS), which shares OMIM 613406 and most of the core phenotype. Key identifiers: MONDO:0013256; OMIM 613406; Orphanet ORPHA:94065 (15q24 microdeletion syndrome). ICD-10 maps to Q93.5 (other deletions of part of a chromosome); ICD-11 to LD44 (chromosomal deletions). Synonyms: 15q24 microdeletion syndrome; 15q24 recurrent microdeletion syndrome; Del(15)(q24); monosomy 15q24; Witteveen–Kolk syndrome (for the SIN3A-restricted form). Information is drawn predominantly from aggregated disease-level resources (OMIM, Orphanet) and from published patient series/case reports rather than from EHR-derived individual-patient datasets.

2. Etiology

Causal factors: The disease is genetic. The deletion arises by non-allelic homologous recombination (NAHR) between flanking low-copy repeats/segmental duplications at 15q24, generating recurrent de novo deletions (PMID: 17360722; PMID: 22180641). Haploinsufficiency of SIN3A is the primary driver of the neurodevelopmental core; additional co-deleted genes contribute organ-specific features in larger deletions (F012).

Genetic risk factors: The principal risk factor is the presence of segmental-duplication architecture at 15q24 predisposing to NAHR. There are no established modifier genes, though deletion size (number of genes removed) correlates with severity and breadth of malformations.

Environmental risk factors / protective factors: None established. As a de novo structural/monogenic disorder, there are no recognized environmental triggers, lifestyle risk factors, or protective alleles/exposures. Advanced parental age is not established as a risk factor. Gene–environment interactions: none documented.

3. Phenotypes

Phenotypes span physical manifestations (dysmorphism, growth failure), clinical signs (hypotonia, microcephaly), behavioral changes (ASD, ADHD), and neurodevelopmental deficits (ID, speech delay). Characteristic age of onset is congenital/early childhood; the core neurocognitive phenotype is static (non-progressive), while a single case report describes progressive cerebellar ataxia and dystonia broadening the spectrum (PMID: 39576496). Severity is typically mild for ID, with variable expressivity.

Quality of life: Impact is driven mainly by cognitive/speech impairment and behavior problems affecting education, independence, and communication; isolated cases carry high QoL burden from severe malformations. No disease-specific QoL instruments (EQ-5D/SF-36) have been applied in published cohorts.

4. Genetic/Molecular Information

Causal gene: SIN3A (HGNC:19353; NCBI Gene 25942; UniProt Q96ST3; chromosome 15q24.2; OMIM gene 607776). Variant classes: heterozygous loss-of-function — nonsense, frameshift, splice-site, and whole-gene deletion; rare destabilizing missense (e.g., p.Ala126Val, p.Lys155Glu in PAH1; p.Pro351Ser) and one large in-frame gain (exons 10–12). In the 28-patient series, 24/28 were truncating, 3 missense, 1 in-frame; 27/28 novel (PMID: 33437032). Classification: pathogenic/likely pathogenic per ACMG for truncating LOF; missense variants supported by structural destabilization data (PMID: 41759735). Allele frequency: pathogenic variants are absent/ultra-rare in gnomAD; SIN3A is highly loss-of-function-intolerant. Origin: germline, predominantly de novo. Functional consequence: loss of function / haploinsufficiency.

Co-deleted genes of interest (contiguous-gene mechanism): STRA6, NEO1, CYP11A1, CYP1A1, EDC3 (F012). Modifier genes: none firmly established; deletion size is the main modifier of expressivity. Epigenetic information: SIN3A haploinsufficiency produces a diagnostic, predominantly hypomethylated blood DNA-methylation episignature (PMID: 36399132). Chromosomal abnormality: interstitial deletion 15q24, ~0.1–3.75 Mb; recurrent breakpoints in SD blocks; detected by CMA/aCGH.

5. Environmental Information

No environmental factors, toxins, radiation, occupational exposures, lifestyle factors, or infectious agents are implicated in causing or triggering this disorder. It is a de novo genetic condition. Recurrent infections observed in some patients are a consequence of the deletion (possible immune component), not an environmental cause.

6. Mechanism / Pathophysiology

Ordered causal chain (initiating lesion → clinical manifestation):

  1. NAHR between segmental duplications/low-copy repeats flanking 15q24 leads to a recurrent de novo interstitial deletion (or an independent intragenic LOF/missense variant in SIN3A). (demonstrated — [PMID: 17360722], [PMID: 22180641])
  2. The deletion/variant results in heterozygous loss of functional SIN3A → SIN3A haploinsufficiency (reduced dosage of the corepressor scaffold). (demonstrated — [PMID: 27399968])
  3. Reduced SIN3A leads to impaired assembly/function of the SIN3–HDAC1/2 corepressor complex (SIN3A, HDAC1/2, RBBP4/7, SAP30, SDS3) and weakened interaction with MeCP2. (demonstrated — [PMID: 33130996], [PMID: 27399968])
  4. Impaired histone deacetylation results in dysregulated transcriptional repression of SIN3 target genes governing cell cycle, DNA replication/repair, apoptosis, chromatin modification, and mitochondrial metabolism (via Myc-Mad, E2F, p53 networks). (demonstrated in mouse — [PMID: 15998811])
  5. In the developing cerebral cortex this leads to reduced cortical neurogenesis, altered neuronal identity, and aberrant corticocortical projections. (demonstrated — [PMID: 27399968])
  6. Altered corticogenesis results in subtle structural changes (corpus callosum hypoplasia, ventriculomegaly) and manifests as mild intellectual disability, speech delay, microcephaly, ASD/ADHD, and hypotonia. (demonstrated/inferred — [PMID: 27399968], [PMID: 33437032])

Branch (larger deletions only): Co-deletion of neighboring genes contributes organ-specific features — STRA6 loss leads to disrupted retinoic-acid/vitamin-A signaling → ocular, diaphragmatic, and cardiac malformations (Matthew-Wood-like); NEO1 loss contributes to ASD; CYP11A1 loss may contribute to steroidogenic/gonadal effects. (inferred from gene function and overlapping syndromes — [PMID: 30204971], [PMID: 31953991])

Molecular pathways: transcriptional corepression via histone deacetylation (not classical signaling cascades); retinoic-acid signaling (STRA6 branch). Cellular processes: neurogenesis, neuronal differentiation/identity, cell-cycle and survival control, apoptosis. Protein dysfunction: loss of function / haploinsufficiency; missense variants destabilize the PAH1 αα-hub protein-interaction domain (PMID: 41759735). Epigenetic changes: global shift toward hypomethylation (episignature). GO/CL suggestions: histone deacetylation GO:0016575, negative regulation of transcription by RNA Pol II GO:0000122, chromatin organization GO:0006325, cerebral cortex neuron differentiation GO:0021895, neurogenesis GO:0022008; cell types — neural progenitor/radial glial cell CL:0000047/CL:0000681, glutamatergic cortical projection neuron CL:0000679.

7. Anatomical Structures Affected

Organ level — primary: brain, especially cerebral cortex (UBERON:0000956); corpus callosum (UBERON:0002336); lateral ventricles (ventriculomegaly). Secondary/other systems: craniofacial skeleton (UBERON:0010323/facial dysmorphism), eyes (UBERON:0000970), ears (hearing), external male genitalia (UBERON:0000079; hypospadias/micropenis), digits/skeleton (UBERON:0002544), and — rarely in larger deletions — heart (UBERON:0000948), diaphragm (UBERON:0001103; CDH), and gastrointestinal tract (UBERON:0001555; intestinal atresia, imperforate anus). Tissue/cell level: nervous tissue; cortical neural progenitors and projection neurons. Subcellular level: nucleus (GO:0005634) — chromatin/corepressor complex (GO:0016580, Sin3-type complex). Lateralization: malformations are generally bilateral/midline (corpus callosum, facial midline); no consistent lateralization.

8. Temporal Development

Onset: congenital — structural malformations are prenatal; growth retardation prenatal/postnatal; developmental delay apparent in infancy/early childhood. Onset pattern: chronic/static. Progression: the core neurodevelopmental phenotype is non-progressive and lifelong; developmental trajectory improves with therapy but ID persists. A rare report documents progressive cerebellar ataxia/dystonia, an atypical course (PMID: 39576496). Disease course: chronic, lifelong; no remission/relapse pattern. Critical periods: fetal corticogenesis (for brain phenotype) and early childhood (developmental intervention window).

9. Inheritance and Population

Epidemiology: rare; exact prevalence not firmly established (Orphanet classifies it on the order of <1/1,000,000). 15q24 deletions occur 8–10× less frequently than 1q21 or 15q13 microdeletions (PMID: 22180641). Inheritance: autosomal dominant; the overwhelming majority are de novo. Penetrance: high/essentially complete for the neurodevelopmental phenotype. Expressivity: highly variable (ID from absent to severe; malformations present only in a minority). Anticipation: not applicable (not a repeat-expansion disorder). Germline mosaicism: possible but not well documented; gives low recurrence risk. Founder effects / consanguinity: not applicable (dominant, de novo). Carrier frequency: not applicable. Demographics: no ethnic predilection; both sexes affected (male-genital phenotypes are sex-limited; the Balasubramanian cohort was 17 M : 11 F). Geographic distribution: worldwide, no endemic clustering.

10. Diagnostics

First-line genetic testing: chromosomal microarray (CMA/aCGH) detects the deletion; karyotype is typically normal (PMID: 22216833). For intragenic SIN3A variants: whole-exome/whole-genome sequencing or multigene neurodevelopmental panels including SIN3A; MLPA has been used to detect 15q24 deletions (PMID: 34922566). FISH can confirm a known deletion. Epigenomic diagnostic: the predominantly hypomethylated WITKOS episignature serves as a functional confirmatory test and resolves VUS (PMID: 36399132). Imaging: brain MRI may show corpus callosum hypoplasia and ventriculomegaly (PMID: 27399968). Clinical evaluation: growth monitoring, developmental/cognitive assessment, dysmorphology exam, genital exam in males, ophthalmologic and audiologic evaluation, and endocrine work-up (GH, pubertal/HH assessment; PMID: 38528912). Differential diagnosis: other microdeletion/ID syndromes with overlapping dysmorphism and ID (e.g., other contiguous-gene syndromes, SIN3B-related syndrome [PMID: 33811806], Kabuki syndrome, and MeCP2-pathway disorders); episignature and CMA/sequencing distinguish them. Screening: no population newborn screening; cascade testing is usually not needed given de novo origin, but parental testing informs recurrence risk.

11. Outcome/Prognosis

Survival/mortality: no reduced life expectancy is established for isolated SIN3A-related disorder; the core phenotype is compatible with normal lifespan. Rare severe congenital malformations (CDH, complex heart defects, intestinal atresia) in larger deletions can cause significant early morbidity and, uncommonly, mortality (PMID: 22216833). Morbidity/function: chief long-term burden is mild (occasionally moderate) ID, speech/communication impairment, and behavioral issues, with variable impact on independence. Complications: recurrent infections, feeding/growth difficulties, seizures, and endocrine issues (GH deficiency, hypogonadotropic hypogonadism). Recovery potential: developmental skills improve with early intervention, but cognitive impairment is lifelong. Prognostic factors: deletion size and presence of major malformations are the main determinants of outcome; cognitive severity ranges from normal to severe. No validated molecular prognostic biomarkers.

12. Treatment

There is no disease-modifying or targeted therapy. Management is supportive, anticipatory, and multidisciplinary (PMID: 33437032). Components, with suggested NCIT intervention concepts: - Developmental/rehabilitative: early intervention, speech therapy (NCIT:C15300 Speech Therapy), physical and occupational therapy, special education. - Neurologic: antiepileptic drugs for seizures (NCIT:C264 Anticonvulsant Agent). - Behavioral/psychiatric: personalized psychological treatment (first documented in WITKOS, with improved functioning; PMID: 41245232); ADHD/ASD-directed behavioral and, where indicated, pharmacologic management. - Surgical (NCIT:C15329 Surgery): repair of hypospadias, congenital diaphragmatic hernia, intestinal atresia, imperforate anus, and congenital heart defects. - Endocrine: growth hormone therapy for documented GH deficiency (NCIT:C1646 Recombinant Human Growth Hormone); hormonal treatment for pubertal delay/hypogonadotropic hypogonadism (PMID: 38528912). - Infectious: management of recurrent infections; immune evaluation when indicated. - Pharmacogenomics / advanced therapeutics (gene, cell, RNA, immunotherapy): none established or applicable. No clinical trials of disease-specific agents are available.

13. Prevention

Primary prevention: none possible, as the disorder is a de novo genetic event. Secondary prevention: early developmental screening enables prompt intervention to optimize function. Tertiary prevention: anticipatory surveillance (growth, endocrine, ophthalmologic, audiologic, seizure monitoring) to prevent/limit complications. Genetic counseling: recurrence risk is low for parents of a child with a de novo deletion/variant (empiric risk slightly above baseline due to possible germline mosaicism); affected individuals who reproduce have a 50% transmission risk (autosomal dominant). Reproductive options: prenatal diagnosis (CMA on CVS/amniocentesis) and preimplantation genetic testing are available when a parental variant is known. Immunization/public-health/environmental interventions: not applicable.

14. Other Species / Natural Disease

Taxonomy/orthologs: SIN3A is deeply conserved; mouse Sin3a (NCBI Gene 20466; Mus musculus, NCBI:txid10090) and zebrafish orthologs are established functional models. Natural disease: no naturally occurring companion-animal or wildlife disease corresponding to 15q24 deletion syndrome is documented in OMIA. Comparative biology: the corepressor mechanism (SIN3–HDAC) is conserved from yeast to humans, underscoring evolutionary conservation of the disease-relevant pathway; the paralog SIN3B is similarly conserved and disease-associated (PMID: 33811806). Transmission/zoonosis: not applicable.

15. Model Organisms

  • Mouse (Mus musculus): conditional mSin3A knockout — embryonic lethal; demonstrates essential role in development, proliferation, survival, and transcriptional network control (cell cycle, DNA repair, apoptosis, p53/Myc-Mad/E2F) (PMID: 15998811). In utero Sin3a knockdown recapitulates the neurodevelopmental core (reduced cortical neurogenesis, altered neuronal identity, aberrant projections) — strong face validity for the human brain phenotype (PMID: 27399968). Limitation: homozygous null is lethal, so dosage/haploinsufficiency (not complete loss) models the human condition.
  • Zebrafish (Danio rerio): sin3b disruption produces craniofacial defects, modeling dysmorphism for the paralogous syndrome (PMID: 33811806).
  • Mouse ES cells / in vitro: HDAC1 conditional knockouts reveal the catalytic requirement and corepressor-complex dependency of Sin3A/NuRD/CoREST activity (PMID: 20404188); structural/biophysical studies of the PAH1 αα-hub model missense-variant destabilization (PMID: 41759735).
  • Applications: cortical neurogenesis, transcriptional/epigenetic dysregulation, structure–function of SIN3A domains. Resources: MGI (mouse), ZFIN (zebrafish). No published patient-derived iPSC/organoid or rat models were identified in this investigation.

The investigation produced a coherent two-tier model. Tier 1 (monogenic core): SIN3A haploinsufficiency impairs an epigenetic corepressor machine, disrupting the transcriptional programs of cortical neurogenesis and producing the invariant neurodevelopmental and craniofacial phenotype shared by the microdeletion and Witteveen–Kolk syndrome. The convergent evidence — OMIM identity (613406), point variants reproducing the phenotype, a diagnostic hypomethylated episignature, structural destabilization of missense variants, and a parallel SIN3B syndrome — makes this the most strongly supported conclusion. Tier 2 (contiguous-gene modifiers): the breadth and severity of non-neurodevelopmental malformations scale with deletion size because additional dosage-sensitive genes (STRA6, NEO1, CYP11A1) are removed, explaining why larger deletions add ocular, diaphragmatic, cardiac, and gonadal features absent in isolated SIN3A variants.

Limitations and Knowledge Gaps

  • Dissecting individual gene contributions: The specific phenotypic contribution of each co-deleted gene (NEO1, CYP11A1, STRA6, EDC3, CYP1A1) within larger deletions is inferred from gene function and overlapping syndromes, not from direct dosage experiments in the 15q24 context (F012 is partly inferential).
  • Epidemiology is imprecise: No robust prevalence/incidence figures exist; the disorder is rare and under-ascertained, with cohorts of tens rather than hundreds of patients.
  • Penetrance/expressivity quantification: The determinants of why some individuals have normal cognition (25% in one cohort) while others have severe ID are unknown; no validated genotype–phenotype or modifier model exists.
  • Natural history: Longitudinal/natural-history data into adulthood are scarce; prognostic biomarkers are absent. An atypical progressive (ataxia/dystonia) case complicates the "static course" generalization.
  • Therapeutics: No disease-targeted therapy or clinical trial exists; behavioral intervention evidence is a single case.
  • Model systems: Patient-derived iPSC/organoid and heterozygous (dosage-matched) adult-phenotype mouse models are not yet reported; homozygous lethality limits conventional knockout approaches.
  • Immune phenotype: The basis of "recurrent infections" is not mechanistically defined.

Proposed Follow-up Experiments / Actions

  1. Haploinsufficiency-faithful mouse model: Generate and deeply phenotype a conditional/heterozygous Sin3a mouse (and a 15q24-deletion mouse spanning the syntenic region) to directly test gene-by-gene contributions and recapitulate cognitive/behavioral endpoints.
  2. Patient iPSC cortical organoids: Derive iPSCs from WITKOS/15q24-deletion patients and model cortical neurogenesis defects, correlating transcriptomic/epigenomic changes with the blood episignature.
  3. Multi-omics on primary samples: Integrate the DNA-methylation episignature with transcriptomics and proteomics to identify dysregulated SIN3 target genes that could serve as biomarkers or therapeutic nodes.
  4. Genotype–phenotype registry: Establish an international registry correlating deletion size/breakpoints and variant type with quantified cognitive, growth, endocrine, and malformation outcomes to refine penetrance/expressivity and prognosis.
  5. Episignature-first diagnostics: Validate the hypomethylated episignature prospectively for VUS resolution and as a first-tier functional assay alongside CMA/sequencing.
  6. Targeted mechanism-based therapy exploration: Given the HDAC-corepressor biology, evaluate (preclinically) whether modulating histone-acetylation balance or compensating via paralogs (SIN3B) can rescue neurodevelopmental phenotypes in model systems.
  7. Immune characterization: Systematically assess immune function in patients with recurrent infections to define whether a specific immunodeficiency component exists and whether co-deleted genes contribute.
