Miliary Tuberculosis (MONDO:0005848): Disease Characteristics Report

How this was researched: Miliary tuberculosis is an infectious disease caused by Mycobacterium tuberculosis complex bacteria. No human causal gene is recorded for it, and none exists in the Mendelian sense. Host genes appear in this report only as susceptibility factors.

The PubMed E-utilities shell access was denied in this session, so all sources were found by web search. A PMID is given only where a retrieved record showed it. Otherwise the citation is a PMC or other URL. Abstract quotes are reproduced only where search results returned the text. Several template fields have no data for an infectious disease, and those are marked "Not applicable".

1. Disease Information

Overview. - MeSH defines miliary tuberculosis as "an acute form of tuberculosis in which minute tubercles are formed in a number of organs of the body due to dissemination of the bacilli through the blood stream" (MeSH D014391). - It results from a "massive lymphohematogenous spread of M. tuberculosis" (PMC3544391). - The name comes from the 1–5 mm lesions, which look like millet seeds (Wikipedia). - It is classified as both pulmonary and extrapulmonary TB (StatPearls NBK562300). - Untreated, it is almost always fatal. "Untreated miliary TB [has] a fatal outcome within one year" (Sharma & Mohan, Indian J Med Res 2012, PMID 22771605). See also PMID 28281441.

2. Etiology

Cause. The disease is caused by infection with M. tuberculosis complex bacteria (NCBITaxon:1773). Zoonotic M. bovis (NCBITaxon:1765) can also cause it. Disease follows hematogenous dissemination, either during primary infection or on reactivation of a latent focus (PMC3544391).

Environmental and host risk factors (human clinical evidence) - A Portuguese cohort of disseminated TB (PMID 30819659) found these independent predictors: - pharmacological immunosuppression: OR 5.6 (95% CI 2.8–11.3) - HIV infection: OR 5.1 (3.1–8.3) - chronic liver failure or cirrhosis: OR 2.3 (1.4–4.1) - longer symptom duration: OR 2.3 (1.4–3.8) - TNF-α antagonists. Keane et al. (NEJM 2001;345:1098–1104; PMID 11596589 per record) analysed 70 TB cases reported after infliximab. - 40 of the 70 cases were extrapulmonary, and 17 of those were disseminated. - Median onset was 12 weeks after starting treatment, and 48 patients developed TB after three or fewer infusions. - The estimated incidence in rheumatoid arthritis patients on infliximab was over 24 per 100,000, against about 6 per 100,000 without it (Science News). - Corticosteroids. Inappropriate glucocorticoid use has been linked to unmasking of cryptic miliary TB and to HLH (J Infect Dev Ctries, PMID 38064397). - Age. Infants and young children are at higher risk (BMC Pediatr 2023). Older adults are also at higher risk (PMC3544391). - Smoking predicted adverse outcome in a West China cohort of 313 patients (Ann Med 2024). - Diabetes, malnutrition, renal failure and malignancy are cited as classical predisposing conditions in the reviews above. This is review-level evidence, and no specific primary estimates were retrieved for them.

Genetic risk factors (host susceptibility, not causal genes) - Mendelian susceptibility to mycobacterial disease (MSMD) is caused by inborn errors of IFN-γ immunity and presents with disseminated BCG or environmental mycobacterial disease (PMID 40656276). - IL12RB1 is the most common cause. In one cohort it accounted for 80% of patients, with IFNGR1 at 12% (PMC7275902). - TYK2 P1104A (rs34536443) homozygosity - It "selectively disrupts the induction of IFN-γ by IL-23 and is a common monogenic etiology of tuberculosis" (Boisson-Dupuis et al., Sci Immunol 2018, PMID 30578352). - In UK Biobank, homozygotes made up 6/620 (1%) of TB patients against 0.2% of controls (OR 5.0). The variant therefore underlies about 1% of TB in people of European ancestry (Kerner et al., PNAS 2019, PMID 31068474, UK Biobank page). - Penetrance is low for MSMD (probably under 1%) but high for TB in endemic areas (probably over 80%). - No GWAS locus specific to miliary TB was retrieved.

Protective factors - BCG vaccination in infancy. - Protection against meningeal and miliary TB is high in infants (RR 0.1, 95% CI 0.01–0.77) and in stringently tuberculin-tested children (RR 0.08, 0.03–0.25) (Mangtani et al., Clin Infect Dis 2014;58:470–80, PMID 24336911). - Modelling estimated that infant BCG given in 2002 would prevent about 11,486 miliary TB cases (7,304–16,280) in the first five years of life (Trunz, Fine & Dye, Lancet 2006; PMID not verified). - Genetic protective variants: none retrieved.

Gene–environment interaction - Host IFN-γ/IL-12/IL-23 axis defects combined with exposure to mycobacteria lead to disseminated disease (MSMD, TYK2 P1104A). - Iatrogenic blockade of the same axis, for example by anti-TNF drugs, produces a similar phenocopy.

5. Environmental Information

  • Infectious agents: M. tuberculosis (NCBITaxon:1773) and M. bovis (NCBITaxon:1765). M. caprae, M. orygis, M. pinnipedii and M. microti can also infect humans (CFSPH). BCG (M. bovis BCG) causes disseminated disease in people with MSMD.
  • Iatrogenic and other exposures: TNF inhibitors and other immunosuppressants (NCIT "TNF inhibitor"), and corticosteroids. The relevant ChEBI terms are infliximab, CHEBI:… (not retrieved), and glucocorticoid, CHEBI:24261.
  • Lifestyle: smoking was associated with adverse outcome (Ann Med 2024).

6. Mechanism / Pathophysiology

Causal chain. Steps 1–3 are human-clinical; step 4 is from model organisms and is inferred for humans; steps 5–7 are human-clinical.

  1. Inhaled M. tuberculosis infects alveolar macrophages (CL:0000583), which leads to intracellular replication and an early granuloma.
  2. ESX-1/ESAT-6 phagosomal damage results in recruitment of new macrophages to the granuloma and their infection. This was shown in zebrafish with M. marinum and is inferred for human *M. tuberculosis

6. Mechanism / Pathophysiology (continued)

Causal chain (steps 1–3 human-clinical; step 4 from model organisms, inferred for humans; steps 5–7 human-clinical):

  1. Inhaled bacilli infect alveolar macrophages (CL:0000583) → intracellular replication and early granuloma formation.
  2. The ESX-1 secretion system and ESAT-6 damage the phagosome → recruitment and infection of additional macrophages (Volkman et al., PMC524251; Davis & Ramakrishnan).
  3. Infected macrophages egress from the primary granuloma → seeding of secondary granulomas, which the zebrafish work frames as the granuloma promoting early dissemination.
  4. In the host, inadequate IFN-γ/IL-12/IL-23 signalling → failure to activate macrophages and control bacilli. IFN-γ-knockout mice show "disseminated tuberculosis" with widespread necrosis and very high bacterial loads (Cooper et al.; Flynn et al., J Exp Med 1993;178:2243–47 and 2249–54, rupress).
  5. Bacilli enter the bloodstream (lymphohematogenous spread) → simultaneous seeding of many organs (PMC3544391).
  6. Innumerable small granulomas form across lung, liver, spleen, bone marrow, meninges and other organs → organ dysfunction.
  7. In severe cases, a systemic inflammatory response → ARDS, septic shock, HLH or adrenal insufficiency (PMC9937678, PMC8787627).

Checklist coverage. - Molecular pathways / immune involvement: the IL-12/IL-23–IFN-γ–STAT1 axis is central (GO:0032609 interferon-gamma production; GO:0042832 defense response to protozoan is not apt — use GO:0051607 defense response to virus as a related example only). Relevant GO terms: GO:0006952 defense response, GO:0002286 T cell activation involved in immune response. - Cellular processes: granuloma formation (GO:0002432), macrophage activation (GO:0042116), apoptosis of infected macrophages (GO:0006915). - Protein dysfunction: loss of TYK2 kinase activity; loss-of-function of IL12RB1/IFNGR1 receptors. - Tissue damage: caseating necrosis within granulomas; the IFN-γ-KO model showed excess necrosis. - Molecular profiling (transcriptomics, proteomics, metabolomics, single-cell, spatial): no miliary-TB-specific omics datasets were retrieved in this session; stated as not available here.

Cell types (CL): alveolar macrophage CL:0000583, macrophage CL:0000235, epithelioid cell, multinucleated giant cell (Langhans), CD4+ T cell CL:0000492.

7. Anatomical Structures Affected

  • Primary organ: lung (UBERON:0002048), with diffuse bilateral miliary nodules.
  • Secondary/multi-organ: liver (UBERON:0002107), spleen (UBERON:0002106), bone marrow (UBERON:0002371), lymph nodes (UBERON:0000029), meninges/brain (UBERON:0002360 / UBERON:0000955), kidney (UBERON:0002113), adrenal gland (UBERON:0002369), choroid of the eye (UBERON:0001776), intestine (UBERON:0000160). Multi-organ seeding is the defining feature (PMC3544391); bovine disease shows the same organ distribution (Merck Vet Manual).
  • Body systems: respiratory, reticuloendothelial/hematopoietic, central nervous, hepatobiliary, endocrine.
  • Tissue/cell level: granulomas in parenchyma; macrophages and T lymphocytes (CL terms above).
  • Subcellular (GO cellular component): phagosome GO:0005768/GO:0045335 (where bacilli reside); the ESX-1 system damages the phagosomal membrane.
  • Localization: bilateral, diffuse, random distribution on HRCT (PMID 9530383).

8. Temporal Development

  • Onset: any age; higher risk in infants/young children and older or immunosuppressed adults. Pattern is usually subacute (weeks), occasionally acute/fulminant.
  • Progression: untreated disease is progressive and fatal within about one year (PMID 22771605).
  • Course: continuous rather than relapsing-remitting. "Cryptic" miliary TB is an insidious form in older adults.
  • Critical window: early anti-TB therapy is life-saving; delay worsens outcome (PMID 28281441).
  • Remission: treatment-induced; no spontaneous remission.

9. Inheritance and Population

  • Share of TB cases: about 1–2% of all TB and up to ~20% of extrapulmonary TB (StatPearls). In a 2010 US analysis, miliary disease was 299/11,182 incident cases (~2.7%); extrapulmonary TB was ~22% of cases (typeset US EPTB epidemiology).
  • CDC 2024 site-of-disease split (all TB): ~71% pulmonary only, 17% extrapulmonary only, 12% both; commonest extrapulmonary sites lymphatic 28% and pleural 24% (CDC 2024).
  • Inheritance: not applicable (infectious). Host susceptibility is AR/AD for the MSMD genes and AR for TYK2 P1104A homozygosity (section 2).
  • Geography: burden tracks global TB — highest in South/Southeast Asia and sub-Saharan Africa; HIV co-infection raises the disseminated fraction.
  • Sex and age: no reliable miliary-specific sex ratio retrieved. Bimodal age risk (young children; older/immunosuppressed adults).

10. Diagnostics

  • Imaging. HRCT is more sensitive than chest radiography and shows randomly distributed 1–5 mm nodules (PMID 9530383); the radiograph can be normal early. About 10% of cases may be missed on initial CT (PMC3473551).
  • Microbiology. In miliary TB, AFB smear sensitivity is ~20–40% and PCR ~60–80% (StatPearls NBK562300). Culture of sputum, blood, bone marrow, liver or other tissue remains important. (Specific Xpert MTB/RIF and bone-marrow/liver-biopsy yield figures were not retrieved in this session.)
  • Fundoscopy for choroid tubercles, described as pathognomonic (PMID 22771605).
  • Histopathology: caseating granulomas with AFB on tissue biopsy (SNOMED/LOINC terms applicable).
  • Supporting labs: leukocytosis, lymphopenia, raised ALT, pancytopenia (with bone-marrow involvement or HLH).
  • Genetic testing is relevant only for suspected host immunodeficiency (MSMD gene panels via GTR), not for routine diagnosis.
  • Differential diagnosis: disseminated fungal infection, sarcoidosis, metastatic malignancy, pneumoconiosis, hypersensitivity pneumonitis, other causes of miliary nodules.

11. Outcome / Prognosis

  • Mortality. In the Japanese DPC cohort, 2,817 patients had 637 (22.6%) in-hospital deaths (PMID 38636244). A UK cohort of 52 reported 1-year mortality of 2%, attributed to timely critical care, anti-TB therapy and possibly corticosteroids (PMC6850233).
  • Prognostic factors. In a West China cohort of 313 (2010–2022), independent predictors of adverse outcome were current smoking, leukocytosis, elevated ALT, and lymphopenia combined with bone-marrow TB or TB lymphadenitis (Ann Med 2024). Pediatric mortality is ~15–20% (BMC Pediatr 2023).
  • Complications: ARDS (~7–18%, high mortality), HLH, septic shock, adrenal insufficiency, drug-induced liver injury, coexisting TB meningitis.
  • Recovery: with timely treatment, most immunocompetent patients recover; without treatment, almost uniformly fatal within a year.

12. Treatment

  • First-line anti-TB therapy (NCIT: Antitubercular Agent). Standard regimen: isoniazid (CHEBI:6030), rifampicin (CHEBI:28077), pyrazinamide (CHEBI:45285) and ethambutol (CHEBI:4877) for a 2-month intensive phase, then isoniazid + rifampicin continuation. Total duration ~6 months for drug-susceptible miliary TB, extended to ~9–12 months when TB meningitis coexists (Nahid et al., ATS/CDC/IDSA 2016, Clin Infect Dis 63(7):e147–95; regimen table PMC6850233 tbl0004). HIV patients not on ART may need an extended continuation phase.
  • Corticosteroids (NCIT: Corticosteroid). Adjunctive use is standard for coexisting TB meningitis/pericarditis and used for life-threatening disease (ARDS, adrenal insufficiency), but routine use in miliary TB without those indications is debated (PMC9937678).
  • Supportive/ICU care for ARDS, shock and organ failure. For MSMD-related disease, adjunctive IFN-γ and treating the underlying immune defect are relevant (PMID 40656276).
  • Monitoring: hepatotoxicity and drug–drug interactions, especially rifampicin with antiretrovirals (PMID 31723681).
  • Randomized trial data specific to miliary TB regimen/duration are sparse (PMID 31723681).

13. Prevention

  • Primary: infant BCG vaccination gives high protection against miliary and meningeal TB in young children (Mangtani 2014, PMID 24336911); it is ineffective in adolescents/adults (JAMA Pediatr IPD meta-analysis). General TB control (case finding, treating infectious cases) reduces transmission.
  • Secondary: screening and treating latent TB infection before immunosuppression. "Before prescribing [infliximab], physicians should screen patients for latent tuberculosis" (Keane 2001, record).
  • Tertiary: early treatment and monitoring to prevent ARDS, DILI and death.
  • Bovine/zoonotic: milk pasteurisation and cattle control programs reduce M. bovis spillover (CFSPH).

14. Other Species / Natural Disease

  • Cattle (NCBITaxon:9913): M. bovis causes bovine TB; "the acute generalized form, known as miliary tuberculosis, is often rapidly fatal", with lesions in lungs, lymph nodes, intestine and kidney (Merck Vet Manual; CFSPH).
  • Zoonosis: M. bovis, M. caprae, M. orygis, M. pinnipedii and M. microti can infect humans (WOAH).
  • Nonhuman primates: low-dose M. tuberculosis in cynomolgus macaques reproduces the human spectrum; TNF blockade before infection produced "fulminant and disseminated disease by 8 weeks" (PMC201048).
  • Comparative: disseminated disease across species consistently follows failure of macrophage/T-cell containment.

15. Model Organisms

  • Mouse (NCBITaxon:10090). IFN-γ gene-disrupted mice develop disseminated TB with widespread necrosis and very high bacillary loads, establishing IFN-γ as essential for containment (Cooper, Flynn et al. 1993, rupress). Resource: MGI.
  • Zebrafish (NCBITaxon:7955) / M. marinum. Intravital imaging showed ESX-1/RD1-driven macrophage recruitment, apoptosis and egress seeding secondary granulomas — a model of early dissemination (Davis & Ramakrishnan; Volkman et al., PMC524251). Resource: ZFIN.
  • Nonhuman primate models (section 14) best recapitulate human granuloma pathology and disseminated disease under TNF blockade.
  • Recapitulation / limitations: mouse and zebrafish capture dissemination mechanisms and host-factor dependence but differ from human caseating granuloma architecture; macaques recapitulate pathology best but are resource-intensive. M. marinum is a surrogate for M. tuberculosis.

Notes on gaps and reliability

  • Bash/PubMed E-utilities were blocked this session; PMIDs are given only where a retrieved page showed them. Several (Trunz/Fine/Dye 2006; Keane 2001 PMID 11596589; HGNC IDs) rest on secondary records and should be verified against PubMed/HGNC directly.
  • ICD-11 code, miliary-TB-specific omics data, EQ-5D/SF-36 quality-of-life measures, and HIV-infant-specific phenotype frequencies were not located and are marked not available.
  • "Disseminated TB" cohort estimates (e.g. Portugal) are broader than strictly miliary disease and are labelled as such.
