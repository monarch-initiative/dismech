Tuberculous Epididymitis (MONDO:0001537): Disease Characteristics Report

How I read the request: Tuberculous epididymitis is an infectious disease, not a Mendelian one. It has no causal human gene. The cause is infection with organisms of the Mycobacterium tuberculosis complex (MTBC). That is mostly M. tuberculosis, and occasionally M. bovis, including the BCG strain given into the bladder. Sections about genes, variants, inheritance and genetic testing are therefore marked as not applicable.

Limits of the sources: PubMed pages and E-utilities could not be reached directly in this session. Abstracts and full text came through Europe PMC, PMC and web search. Quotes marked "verbatim" were retrieved in this session. Where only a search summary was available, I say so.

1. Disease Information

Overview. MONDO defines the disease as "An urogenital tuberculosis involving a pathogenic inflammatory response in the epididymis" (OLS4, MONDO:0001537; https://www.ebi.ac.uk/ols4/api/ontologies/mondo/terms?iri=http://purl.obolibrary.org/obo/MONDO_0001537). - The epididymis is the genital organ most often affected in male genital tuberculosis (TB). - The disease often spreads to the testis, which is then called tuberculous epididymo-orchitis (TBEO). - Urogenital TB as a whole is "the second most frequent form of extrapulmonary tuberculosis" (Figueiredo 2017, PMID 28087922).

Source of evidence. The evidence is aggregated at disease level. It comes from retrospective single-centre case series, case reports, imaging series and narrative reviews. There are no registries or EHR cohorts specific to this disease.

2. Etiology

Causal factors. - Infection: MTBC organisms, mainly M. tuberculosis. M. bovis causes some cases, including after intravesical BCG for bladder cancer (Menke 2000, PMID 10772435; Okadome 2002, PMID 12056045; Mateos Colino 2003, PMID 12731446; Muttarak 2002, PMID 12060159). - Spread: usually secondary to blood-borne spread from a lung focus, or to spread down the urinary tract from the kidney or prostate. Figueiredo writes: "Starting with a pulmonary focus, 2 to 20% of patients develop urogenital tuberculosis through hematogenous spread" (PMID 28087922).

Risk factors. - Earlier or concurrent TB elsewhere. In Huang's series of 81 TBEO patients, 12.3% had a history of TB (lung 8, kidney 1, bone 1) and 7.4% had active lung TB on chest imaging (PMID 34654377, PMC8520285). Reviews report that more than half of men with male genital TB have lung and/or kidney TB (web review summary, PMC5422679). - HIV and other immunosuppression. In a Thai series of 25 patients, 16% had HIV and one third had concurrent lung TB (Suankwan 2012, Southeast Asian J Trop Med Public Health; https://www.tm.mahidol.ac.th/seameo/2012-43-4/17-5170-21.pdf; PMID not retrieved). - Intravesical BCG therapy. Epididymitis after BCG is rare. One source gives about 0.4% of treated patients (PMC6649823). Menke writes: "The incidence of BCG-associated epididymoorchitis is rare" (PMID 10772435). Recent instrumentation or catheterization and immunocompromise are listed as contributing factors (PMC3558260). - Age and sex. By definition only males are affected. Figueiredo: "predominant in males in their fourth and fifth decades" (PMID 28087922). - Living in or coming from a high-TB-burden country. - Genetic risk factors: none described specifically for this disease. Host-susceptibility genes for TB in general (for example the IL-12/IFN-γ axis in Mendelian susceptibility to mycobacterial disease) were not searched for this site and are not documented for it.

Protective factors. - Genetic: none documented. - Environmental: preventing and treating TB infection, and finding and treating lung or kidney TB early. BCG vaccination protects mainly against severe childhood TB. No evidence specific to the epididymis was retrieved.

Gene–environment interactions: none documented for this disease.

  • Onset: adult, with a mean age of 50.8 years (range 13–90) in Huang. The median was 32 years in the Indian series (PMID 16006701).
  • Course: subacute to chronic and slowly progressive. Tuberculous abscesses had a longer duration of symptoms and less scrotal tenderness than pyogenic ones (Yang 2001, AJR 177:1131; summary via QxMD https://www.qxmd.com/r/11641187).
  • Quality of life: no EQ-5D or SF-36 data were found. The main effects are on sexual function and fertility (Huang 2021).

4. Genetic / Molecular Information

  • Causal genes, pathogenic variants, modifier genes: none in the host. This is an infectious disease.
  • ClinVar, gnomAD and ACMG variant classification: not applicable.
  • Epigenetic findings and chromosomal abnormalities: none reported.
  • Pathogen genetics: relevant in practice.
  • Xpert MTB/RIF detects MTBC DNA and rpoB rifampicin-resistance mutations.
  • Species-specific PCR or whole-genome sequencing separates M. bovis BCG from M. tuberculosis in cases after BCG therapy (PMC6649823; Kyoto University case reports).
  • BCG strains are naturally resistant to pyrazinamide (general knowledge, not retrieved here). This matters when choosing drugs.

5. Environmental Information

  • Infectious agents:
  • Mycobacterium tuberculosis (NCBITaxon:1773)
  • Mycobacterium bovis (NCBITaxon:1765), including BCG (NCBITaxon:33892)
  • Exposures:
  • Exposure to people with infectious TB, especially in endemic countries.
  • Intravesical BCG therapy.
  • Unpasteurized milk, for zoonotic M. bovis.
  • Lifestyle factors (general TB risk factors; no epididymis-specific data were retrieved): smoking, alcohol use, malnutrition and diabetes.
  • Toxins, radiation, pollution: no data relevant to this disease.

6. Mechanism / Pathophysiology

Causal chain. 1. Infection. MTBC bacilli are inhaled and cause a primary lung infection, often latent. In the BCG pathway, live M. bovis BCG is instilled into the bladder instead. 2. Spread. Bacilli enter the blood and seed organs with a rich blood supply: the kidneys, prostate and epididymis (PMID 28087922; review summary PMC5422679). In the alternative branch, bacilli travel backward from infected urine or prostate down the vas deferens to the epididymis. This is the likely route after BCG and in kidney TB. It is inferred from clinical association rather than shown experimentally. 3. Seeding. Infection usually starts in the tail (cauda) of the epididymis, then spreads through the whole epididymis (PMC5960082 case review). 4. Immune response. Macrophages take up the bacilli, and a Th1/IFN-γ-driven cell-mediated response forms granulomas made of epithelioid histiocytes and Langhans giant cells. 5. Tissue damage. The granulomas undergo caseous necrosis, seen in 81/81 surgical specimens in Huang (PMID 34654377). This leads to an enlarged, mixed-echo epididymis, abscess formation and, when healing, fibrosis and calcification. 6. Spread to the testis. Disease extends directly from the epididymis to the testis, giving epididymo-orchitis. Isolated testicular TB is uncommon because the blood–testis barrier protects the testis (review summary PMC5422679). In the other direction, disease spreads outward to the tunica vaginalis and scrotal skin, causing hydrocele, abscess and draining scrotal sinuses (Chung, PMID 9282805). 7. Obstruction and long-term effects. Fibrosis or granulomas block the epididymis, vas or ejaculatory duct. This causes obstructive azoospermia or infertility, which can persist after successful drug treatment (PMC2684350; PMC5422679). Damage to the testis itself causes testicular loss, and chronic illness contributes to sexual dysfunction. 8. Systemic effects. In parallel, systemic inflammation causes weight loss, fever, anemia and raised ESR/CRP. Rarely the disease disseminates, for example to the psoas or adrenals, causing Addison disease (Skoutelis 2000, PMID 10879650).

Checklist detail. - Pathways. Standard TB immunity applies: TLR2-mediated recognition of the bacteria, IL-12 → IFN-γ → macrophage activation, and TNF-dependent maintenance of granulomas. None of this has been studied in the epididymis specifically. - Cell types (CL): - macrophage (CL:0000235) - epithelioid cell (CL:0000622-type term) - multinucleate giant cell (CL:0000647) - CD4+ T cell (CL:0000624) - epididymal principal cell (CL:1000154-type term) - spermatogenic cells in the testis (CL:0000586 germ cell) - GO biological processes: - granuloma formation (GO:0002432) - inflammatory response (GO:0006954) - response to bacterium (GO:0009617) - type II interferon-mediated signaling (GO:0060333) - necrotic cell death (GO:0070265) - phagocytosis (GO:0006909) - Omics, single-cell, spatial or CRISPR data: none found for this disease.

7. Anatomical Structures Affected

  • Primary site:
  • epididymis (UBERON:0001301)
  • most often the tail (cauda epididymis, UBERON:0004630); head and body less often
  • Secondary sites:
  • testis (UBERON:0000473)
  • tunica vaginalis (UBERON:0001338) and hydrocele
  • scrotum and scrotal skin (UBERON:0001300)
  • vas deferens (UBERON:0001000)
  • seminal vesicle (UBERON:0000998)
  • prostate (UBERON:0002367)
  • kidney (UBERON:0002113) and lung (UBERON:0002048) as associated foci
  • Body systems: male reproductive system and urinary system.
  • Distribution in Huang 2021 (surgical series): epididymis alone 38.3%, testis alone 28.4%, both 33.3%.
  • Laterality:
  • usually unilateral; bilateral in 5–22% depending on the series (PMIDs 34654377, 16006701, 9282805)
  • bilateral involvement favors TB over non-tuberculous epididymitis (Radiopaedia)
  • in Huang, the right side was more common than the left on exam (33% vs 20%)
  • Subcellular level: bacilli live inside macrophage phagosomes (GO:0045335) and lysosomes, and block phagosome maturation. This is general TB biology.

8. Temporal Development

  • Onset: in adulthood, mostly in the 30s to 50s. Onset is insidious and subacute. It can present acutely and be mistaken for bacterial epididymitis.
  • Latency: there can be years between the original infection and the scrotal disease. After BCG, epididymitis can appear long after treatment, in one report 35 months later. Menke concluded it "should be considered a late complication" (PMID 10772435).
  • Stages (descriptive, not formal):
  • early granulomatous epididymitis
  • caseation and abscess
  • spread to the testis and skin (sinus)
  • healed fibrocalcific stage with obstruction
  • Remission: treatment-induced. Viswaroop reported "Anti TB treatment resulted in a complete response in 10 and partial response in 18" (PMID 16006701). Spontaneous remission is not documented.
  • Window for intervention: early diagnosis before abscess or sinus formation, and before damage to both sides, may preserve the testis and fertility. Muneer: "Delay in diagnosis results in disease progression, irreversible tissue and organ damage" (PMID 31548730).

9. Inheritance and Population

  • Inheritance, penetrance, anticipation, founder effects, carrier frequency: not applicable.
  • Epidemiology:
  • No population incidence for tuberculous epididymitis was found.
  • In proportional terms, 15–40% of TB cases are extrapulmonary, and urogenital TB is the second most common extrapulmonary form (Muneer 2019, PMID 31548730; Figueiredo 2017, PMID 28087922).
  • The epididymis is involved in about 22–55% of men with genitourinary TB (review summary, PMC5422679).
  • Viswaroop found 54 of 187 men with genitourinary TB had epididymal involvement (PMID 16006701).
  • MONDO tags the disease as "rare".
  • Geography: mostly high-burden regions: South and Southeast Asia, China, Africa and South America. Series come from India, China, Thailand and Korea. In low-burden countries, cases occur in migrants and after BCG therapy.
  • Sex ratio: 100% male by definition. Female genital TB is a separate entity.
  • Age: wide range (13–90), with a peak at 30–50 (PMID 28087922; Gansu 47-case series via web summary).

10. Diagnostics

Laboratory tests. - Urinalysis for sterile pyuria (27%). - Early-morning urine for acid-fast bacilli (AFB) smear and mycobacterial culture. The yield is low: 0/16 positive in Huang, and "10 showed no evidence of tuberculous infection in their urine" in Ferrie (n=20, PMID 6683985). - ESR and CRP. - HIV testing. - Interferon-gamma release assay (IGRA) or tuberculin skin test, which are supportive only.

Molecular tests. - Xpert MTB/RIF or Ultra on tissue, pus or urine. Sensitivity against culture varies by specimen type. For pleural fluid, for example, it was "49.5% (39.8 to 59.9)" (Cochrane, Kohli 2021, PMID 33448348). - PCR on tissue was positive in 55.8% in Huang. - Combining urinary PCR with scrotal MRI "allowed prompt diagnosis" (Liu 2005, PMID 16110362).

Imaging. - Scrotal ultrasound with Doppler: - An enlarged epididymis that is hypoechoic in 13 of 22 hemiscrotums, hyperechoic in 2 and mixed in 7 (Chung, PMID 9282805). - Patterns: diffusely heterogeneous, diffusely homogeneous, or nodular heterogeneous (Muttarak 2001, PMID 11373214; no abstract available). - An enlarged, heterogeneous epididymis helps separate TB from non-TB epididymitis (Kim 1993, PMID 8510282). - Tuberculous abscesses are larger and have less blood flow at their edge than pyogenic ones (Yang 2001, AJR; QxMD 11641187). - A 2024 nomogram combining ultrasound and clinical features was built to separate epididymal TB from non-TB epididymitis (PMC11220132). - CT/MRI: CT showed infectious features in 87.8% in Huang. - Chest X-ray or CT to look for lung TB.

Pathology. - Caseating granulomas with Langhans giant cells (100% in Huang). - AFB stain positive in 80.5% of surgical specimens. - Fine-needle aspiration cytology (FNAC) is an option before surgery. - Culture is the reference standard and gives drug-susceptibility testing.

Genetic testing of the host: not applicable (no host gene, panel, exome or CMA testing is indicated). Pathogen genotyping is useful for resistance and to identify BCG.

Clinical criteria: - There are no formal criteria. Diagnosis rests on histology or microbiology. - Before surgery, only 37% were correctly diagnosed as TBEO. In 34.6%, a tumor was suspected (Huang).

Differential diagnosis: - Bacterial (pyogenic) epididymitis: acute and tender; responds to antibiotics. - Testicular tumor: a lesion confined to the testis with a normal epididymis suggests cancer. - Brucella epididymo-orchitis, which is endemic in overlapping regions. - Others: testicular torsion or infarction, sarcoidosis, fungal infection, and granulomatous orchitis.

Screening: no screening of asymptomatic people. Figueiredo advises "A systematic search for urogenital tuberculosis, regardless of symptoms" in TB patients (PMID 18836557). Routine semen PCR for men with obstructive azoospermia is debated (J Hum Reprod Sci 2015, via web summary).

11. Outcome / Prognosis

  • Mortality: low. In Huang there was 1 death (1.2%), from liver cirrhosis and unrelated to TB, and "no recurrence was observed" over a mean follow-up of 82 months (PMID 34654377).
  • Morbidity (Huang):
  • loss of the testis: epididymo-orchidectomy in 64%
  • sterility 8.6%
  • erectile dysfunction 4.9%
  • premature ejaculation 6.2%
  • Infertility: obstructive azoospermia can persist after cure (PMC2684350). Sperm retrieval with ICSI gives results similar to non-TB obstructive azoospermia (PMC3222923).
  • Complications:
  • abscess
  • scrotal sinus or fistula
  • spread to the testis
  • spread to the other side
  • disseminated TB: psoas abscesses and Addison disease (PMID 10879650)
  • Prognostic factors:
  • delay in diagnosis (PMID 31548730)
  • bilateral disease, which threatens fertility
  • abscess or sinus, which more often leads to surgery
  • HIV
  • drug resistance
  • Prognostic biomarkers: none validated.

12. Treatment

Drug treatment. - Standard first-line therapy: - isoniazid (CHEBI:6030; NCIT C574) - rifampicin (CHEBI:28077; NCIT C793) - pyrazinamide (CHEBI:45285; NCIT C772) - ethambutol (CHEBI:4877; NCIT C47518) - Schedule: 2 months of all four, then 4 months of isoniazid and rifampicin. - Duration: the ATS/CDC/IDSA 2016 guideline generally supports 6 months for most extrapulmonary sites (Nahid 2016, PMID 27516382). I could not retrieve its genitourinary-specific wording. - In practice: 2–4 weeks of treatment before surgery and 6–12 months after surgery in Huang. A Gansu series used 3–6 months after surgery. - After BCG: regimens omit pyrazinamide because BCG is naturally resistant to it (general knowledge, not retrieved here). - Pharmacogenomics: NAT2 acetylator status affects isoniazid exposure and liver toxicity (general CPIC/PharmGKB knowledge, not retrieved here). - NCIT intervention term: Antitubercular Agent (C281-type class).

Surgery (NCIT: Epididymectomy; Orchiectomy C15268): - Indicated for: - abscess - sinus - a mass that does not respond to drugs - an uncertain diagnosis or suspected tumor - In Huang: epididymectomy 33.3%, epididymo-orchidectomy 64.2%, orchiectomy 2.5%. The authors recommend "triple therapy" (chemotherapy + surgery + drugs) for advanced disease (PMID 34654377). - In Gansu (n=47): epididymectomy 55.3%, epididymectomy plus orchiectomy 44.7%, with no recurrence.

Supportive care and fertility: - pain relief - fertility counseling - sperm cryopreservation, or surgical sperm retrieval with ICSI

Advanced therapies: none apply (no gene, cell or RNA therapies). No clinical trials specific to this disease were found.

Response: - Viswaroop: complete response in 10 and partial response in 18 with drugs alone (PMID 16006701). - Recurrence after combined treatment is rare. - Side effects are the standard ones for these drugs: liver toxicity, peripheral neuropathy (isoniazid) and optic neuritis (ethambutol).

13. Prevention

  • Primary prevention:
  • TB control: case finding, contact tracing, and treatment of TB infection
  • BCG vaccination of infants, which mainly prevents severe childhood TB
  • pasteurization of milk to prevent M. bovis
  • aseptic, atraumatic bladder instillation of BCG, and not instilling after traumatic catheterization (PMC3558260)
  • Secondary prevention:
  • Examine the genitourinary tract and urine of TB patients (PMID 18836557).
  • Suspect TB in a chronic scrotal mass, a scrotal sinus, or "epididymitis" that does not respond to antibiotics, especially in endemic settings or after BCG.
  • Tertiary prevention:
  • Complete full drug treatment.
  • Check the other side.
  • Assess fertility and sexual function during follow-up (Huang).
  • Genetic counseling: not applicable.

14. Other Species / Natural Disease

  • Cattle (Bos taurus, NCBITaxon:9913):
  • Genital TB from M. bovis is rare in bulls.
  • Penile and preputial TB in a bull has been reported (J Comp Pathol 2020). The authors advise considering TB in granulomatous genital lesions of bulls.
  • Orchitis affects about 2–3% of bulls overall, from many causes including M. tuberculosis/M. bovis, Brucella abortus and BoHV-1 (MDPI review via web summary).
  • Zoonotic potential:
  • M. bovis infects people through milk and contact with animals.
  • BCG epididymitis is iatrogenic, not zoonotic.
  • OMIA, breeds and orthologous genes: not applicable.

15. Model Organisms

  • No animal model of tuberculous epididymitis specifically was found.
  • General TB models in mice, guinea pigs, rabbits and non-human primates, including M. tuberculosis aerosol infection, produce disseminated disease. Genital involvement has not been characterized as a model.
  • Limitation: these models do not reproduce epididymal tropism, obstruction, or infertility outcomes.
  • Research gap: there is a need for a model of epididymal or male reproductive tract mycobacterial infection, for example retrograde or intravesical BCG instillation in rodents. None was retrieved.
