Ross River Fever

1. Disease Information

2026-11-24
Claude Code MONDO:0000344 Model: claude-haiku-5-5, claude-opus-4-8, claude-opus-5-5 21 citations Prompt: disease_pathophysiology_research.md f452f0a9c7e3

1. Disease Information

Overview. Ross River fever is an acute, often self-limiting but frequently prolonged arthritogenic illness caused by Ross River virus, an RNA virus of the genus Alphavirus (family Togaviridae). The disease is characterized by polyarthralgia/polyarthritis, fever, fatigue, myalgia and a maculopapular rash, and is the most common and widespread mosquito-borne disease in Australia. It is endemic and enzootic in Australia, Papua New Guinea and the Solomon Islands (Yuen & Bielefeldt-Ohmann 2021, PMID 33802851; Harley, Sleigh & Ritchie 2001, Clin Microbiol Rev 14(4):909–932, DOI 10.1128/CMR.14.4.909-932.2001, PMC89008). The virus was first isolated in 1959 from Aedes vigilax mosquitoes trapped beside the Ross River in Townsville, North Queensland (PMC89008; CDC).

Key identifiers (retrieved): - MONDO: MONDO:0000344 (per template) - MeSH: The virus has descriptor D012398 ("Ross River virus"); scope note: "A species of ALPHAVIRUS associated with epidemic EXANTHEMA and polyarthritis in Australia" (MeSH record) - ICD-11: 1D4D "Ross River disease" (synonyms: Ross River fever; RR fever; epidemic polyarthritis) (CISMeF/ICD-11) - ICD-10: A92.8 ("Other specified mosquito-borne viral fevers") — this is the conventional code but was not confirmed in a retrieved authoritative crosswalk; verify in the WHO ICD-10 browser. - OMIM / Orphanet: None found/not applicable (infectious disease; no OMIM entry retrieved; no Orphanet record appeared).

Synonyms / alternative names (retrieved): Ross River virus disease; Ross River virus infection; Ross River disease; epidemic polyarthritis (historical term). ⚠️ Mapping caveat: "epidemic polyarthritis" historically denotes disease caused by either RRV or Barmah Forest virus (BFV), so term-based lookups may conflate the two (PMC89008).

Information source type. The knowledge base is derived from aggregated disease-level resources — national notifiable-disease surveillance (Australian NNDSS), published cohort/outbreak studies, cross-disciplinary reviews, mouse-model experimental studies, and in vitro work — rather than from individual EHR records.

2. Etiology

Primary cause (infectious). The disease is caused by infection with Ross River virus, transmitted to humans through the bite of infected mosquitoes. RRV is maintained in nature in a mosquito–vertebrate–mosquito enzootic cycle, with native macropods (kangaroos, wallabies) regarded as the primary reservoir hosts (Yuen & Bielefeldt-Ohmann 2021, PMID 33802851; PMC89008). Humans are generally considered dead-end/spillover hosts, though human-to-mosquito-to-human transmission may contribute in urban outbreaks (notably the 1979–80 Pacific Islands epidemic).

Risk factors (environmental/behavioral): - Residence/exposure in endemic areas, particularly coastal saltmarsh and inland riverine/irrigation regions of Australia. - High rainfall and flooding / high tides, which expand mosquito breeding habitat. Reviews report "a 0 to 3 month lag period between increased rainfall and RRV infections" (Yuen & Bielefeldt-Ohmann 2021, PMID 33802851). - Season: peak transmission in the Australian summer–autumn (notifications peak around March). - Age and sex: disease incidence is highest in adults aged ~30–64 years, and regional studies report higher rates in females (South Australia incidence study, CDI). Symptomatic/chronic disease is uncommon in young children. - Outdoor occupational/recreational exposure to mosquitoes. - Blood transfusion is a rare transmission route (CDC).

Genetic (host) risk factors. No validated human Mendelian susceptibility gene is established. ⚠️ I did not retrieve robust human GWAS/HLA association data within this session; HLA associations have been proposed historically for alphaviral arthritides but I could not confirm a specific allele from retrieved sources, so this is reported as not established/unretrieved. The strongest mechanistic host-gene link is the entry mediator MXRA8 (see §4, §6), demonstrated in mouse models rather than as a human susceptibility allele.

Protective factors. Prior natural infection confers immunity (RRV is antigenically stable; lifelong immunity after infection is generally assumed). No dietary or pharmacologic protective factor is established. Vector-avoidance behaviors (repellent, protective clothing, avoiding dusk/dawn exposure) reduce risk.

Gene–environment interactions. Not characterized in humans. In mouse models, host genetic background and immune-pathway genes (complement, MXRA8) modulate the severity of disease produced by the environmental agent (the virus) — effectively an experimental gene–"environment" (infection) interaction (Morrison et al. 2007, PMID 17314163; MXRA8 study, PMC10128017).

Direct quotes (PMID 33802851): "around 70–90% of patients experienced arthralgia (most commonly knee, ankle and wrist joints), lethargy or tiredness, and joint stiffness"; "50–70% had myalgia"; "~50% had skin rash, fever, and flu-like illness."

Phenotype characteristics: - Age of onset: acute, occurring days after mosquito bite; clinical disease predominantly in adults (rare/mild in children). - Severity: mild to moderate in most; severe/debilitating arthralgia in a subset; no fatal course documented. - Progression: typically episodic/fluctuating then slowly resolving; arthralgia can be migratory. Acute illness lasts "from a few weeks to a few months." - Rare sequelae: "uncommon sequelae, such as encephalitis, have been reported" (PMID 33802851); glomerulonephritis and hematologic abnormalities are occasionally described.

Quality-of-life impact. The arthralgia/fatigue complex substantially impairs work capacity and daily functioning during the prolonged phase; the pentosan trial used hand-grip strength, RAPID3, and SF-36 as QoL/functional endpoints, indicating meaningful functional impairment in RRV arthralgia (Krishnan et al. 2021, PMID 33711991). Formal population EQ-5D/SF-36 norms specific to RRV were not retrieved beyond this trial.

4. Genetic / Molecular Information

Not applicable as a human heritable disorder. There are no human causal genes, pathogenic germline variants, inheritance pattern, penetrance, modifier genes, chromosomal abnormalities, or disease-specific epigenetic changes — RRV fever is an acquired viral infection.

Viral genetics (the etiologic agent): RRV has a positive-sense single-stranded RNA genome (~11.8 kb) encoding four non-structural proteins (nsP1–nsP4) and the structural polyprotein (capsid C; envelope glycoproteins E3, E2, 6K, E1) (PMC89008; MeSH D012398). E2 mediates receptor binding and E1 mediates membrane fusion (alphavirus biology). ⚠️ One detailed query on RRV molecular virology was blocked by a safety classifier in this session, so some structural-biology specifics are reported at the level the reviews support rather than from a dedicated primary structural paper.

Host entry gene — MXRA8 (HGNC: MXRA8). A genome-wide CRISPR screen identified Mxra8 as an entry mediator for multiple arthritogenic alphaviruses including RRV, chikungunya (CHIKV), Mayaro and o'nyong-nyong; Mxra8 knockout reduced RRV infection in cells (Zhang et al. 2018, Nature 557:570–574, s41586-018-0121-3; PMC5970976). In vivo, RRV-infected MXRA8-knockout mice showed reduced viral replication and less weight loss, with titers falling below detection by day 5, indicating MXRA8 contributes to RRV pathogenesis, not only cell entry (2023, PMC10128017). Note most direct binding mechanism was demonstrated for CHIKV.

Allele frequency / somatic-vs-germline / ACMG classification: Not applicable (no pathogenic human variants to classify).

5. Environmental Information

  • Infectious agent: Ross River virus, genus Alphavirus, family Togaviridae (NCBI Taxonomy: Ross River virus; CHEBI not applicable). The sole necessary cause.
  • Vectors (>40 competent mosquito species): Key vectors are the freshwater Culex annulirostris (inland, widespread across mainland Australia), and the saltmarsh Aedes vigilax (northern/coastal) and Aedes camptorhynchus (southern/coastal); Aedes notoscriptus in metropolitan areas (PMID 33802851; PMC89008).
  • Reservoir/amplifying hosts: Macropods (kangaroos, wallabies) are the believed primary reservoirs; brushtail possums, horses, flying foxes and humans may act as hosts in some settings; dogs and cats remained aviraemic (PMID 33802851).
  • Environmental drivers: Rainfall, flooding, tidal inundation of saltmarsh, temperature, and irrigation — all modulating mosquito abundance. Seroprevalence in blood donors ranged 22% (Townsville) to 4% (Sydney), reflecting latitudinal/climatic gradient (PMID 33802851).
  • Lifestyle factors: Outdoor exposure in endemic regions; no smoking/diet/alcohol association established.

6. Mechanism / Pathophysiology

Ordered causal chain (initiating infection → clinical manifestation). Evidence type noted per step; several steps are inferred primarily from mouse models and in vitro work.

  1. An infected mosquito inoculates RRV into skin/subcutaneous tissue during blood feeding. → leads to local infection of permissive cells. (Established.)
  2. RRV virions engage the entry mediator MXRA8 (and other attachment factors) on target cells → results in receptor-mediated internalization and cytoplasmic RNA replication. (Mouse/cell evidence: Zhang 2018; PMC10128017.)
  3. Viral replication and viremia → leads to dissemination to musculoskeletal tissues (skeletal muscle, joints/synovium, bone), the tropism underlying arthritis/myositis. (Mouse model; viremia detectable days 2–9 p.i. in mice — PMID 33802851.)
  4. Infection of/trafficking to monocytes and macrophages → results in recruitment of inflammatory monocyte/macrophage infiltrates into muscle and joint tissue; these cells dominate the infiltrate. (Mouse; macrophage depletion by silica before infection "resulted in diminished acute disease" — PMID 33802851.) ⚠️ Branch/caveat: human monocytes "could not be infected with RRV, except in the presence of subneutralising levels of antibody," raising the possibility of antibody-dependent enhancement as a contributor.
  5. Infected/activated cells upregulate proinflammatory mediators — e.g., MCP-1/CCL2 and IL-8 upregulated in human synovial fibroblasts in vitro (IL-8 up 8.2-fold in human fibroblasts) → leads to amplification of leukocyte recruitment and local inflammation. (In vitro + mouse; PMID 33802851.)
  6. Complement activation — specifically the mannose-binding lectin (MBL) pathway, triggered by viral envelope N-glycans on infected cells → leads to C3 activation and deposition in inflamed muscle. (Mouse; glycan→MBL→complement: PubMed 29324290.)
  7. Complement (via C3) acts in the effector phase → results in inflammatory destruction of skeletal muscle and joint tissue. C3-deficient mice develop "much less severe disease" and "far less severe destruction of skeletal muscle tissue" despite similar inflammatory infiltrates, placing complement downstream of cell recruitment in the causal chain (Morrison et al. 2007, PMID 17314163). Human corroboration: "complement activation was also detected in synovial fluid from RRV-infected patients" (PMID 17314163).
  8. Tissue inflammation/damage → produces the clinical polyarthritis, myalgia, and rash. (Human-clinical outcome.)
  9. Resolution/repair branch: during recovery a CD11b^hi Ly6C^lo CX3CR1+ macrophage population appears in muscle and supports tissue repair (mouse, mBio), while persistently infected macrophages are proposed to drive chronic/relapsing symptoms — but persistent productive infection was shown "only ... in a murine macrophage cell line (RAW 264.7)," so chronicity mechanism is inferred, not demonstrated in humans (PMID 33802851).

Upstream vs downstream: Viral entry (MXRA8) and replication are upstream; monocyte/macrophage recruitment and cytokine/chemokine induction are intermediate; complement (MBL→C3) effector activity and tissue destruction are downstream. Immune complexes are "probably not involved" (normal neutrophils and C3/C4 in that context) (PMID 33802851).

Molecular pathways / processes & ontology suggestions: - Complement cascade — Reactome R-HSA-166658 (Complement cascade); lectin pathway. GO:0006956 (complement activation); GO:0001867 (complement activation, lectin pathway). - Inflammatory response — GO:0006954; chemokine signaling (CCL2/MCP-1) GO:0070098. - Type I interferon response (RIG-I/MAVS in monocytes — summary-level) GO:0060337; viral entry/receptor binding GO:0046718 (viral entry into host cell). - Cell types (CL): monocyte CL:0000576; macrophage CL:0000235; inflammatory monocyte CL:0002057; synovial fibroblast CL:0000210; skeletal muscle myocyte/cell CL:0000187/CL:0008002.

Molecular profiling / omics / single-cell / CRISPR: A genome-wide CRISPR screen identified MXRA8 (functional genomics; Zhang 2018). Single-cell/flow characterization of muscle macrophage populations in mouse recovery is reported (mBio). Human multi-omic transcriptomic/proteomic disease signatures specific to RRV were not retrieved in depth; the pentosan trial reported circulating biomarkers (COMP, CTX-II, CCL1, CXCL12, CXCL16, CCL17) modulated by therapy (Krishnan 2021, PMID 33711991).

7. Anatomical Structures Affected

Organ/system level: - Musculoskeletal system (primary): peripheral joints — knee, ankle, wrist, small joints of hands/feet (metacarpophalangeal, interphalangeal); skeletal muscle (myositis in models); tendons/entheses (Achilles tenosynovitis). UBERON: UBERON:0002544 (joint)? → UBERON:0001474 skeletal element; UBERON:0002385 (muscle tissue); UBERON:0000211 (ligament)/tendon UBERON:0000043. - Integumentary system: skin — maculopapular rash (UBERON:0002097 skin of body). - Lymphatic: lymph nodes — lymphadenopathy (UBERON:0000029 lymph node). - Secondary/rare involvement: CNS (encephalitis, rare), kidney (glomerulonephritis, rare).

Tissue/cell level: synovium (synovial membrane, UBERON:0002018) and synovial fibroblasts (CL:0000210); skeletal muscle myofibers (CL:0000187) with infiltrating monocytes/macrophages (CL:0000576/CL:0000235).

Subcellular level: RRV replicates in the cytoplasm on modified membranes (GO:0005737 cytoplasm; GO:0016020 membrane); envelope glycoproteins transit the ER/Golgi secretory pathway (GO:0005783 endoplasmic reticulum; GO:0005794 Golgi).

Localization/laterality: Arthritis is characteristically polyarticular and often symmetrical/bilateral, affecting peripheral joints (CDC); may be migratory.

8. Temporal Development

  • Onset: Acute, following an incubation period of 3–21 days (usually 7–9 days) (PMID 33802851; WA/Vic guidance).
  • Acute phase: fever, rash, fatigue, and the abrupt onset of polyarthralgia/arthritis, lasting weeks.
  • Course: Often episodic/relapsing-remitting during convalescence; arthralgia may wax and wane and migrate.
  • Duration / chronicity: Acute illness resolves over "a few weeks to a few months." A substantial minority have prolonged disease: Australian guidance reports ~15–66% with ongoing arthralgia at 3 months, with resolution in the majority by 5–7 months, and about one-quarter with rheumatic symptoms persisting ≥12 months (WA GP guide). The review states "more than 50% of patients continue to experience symptoms 12 months after initial presentation" (PMID 33802851) — figures vary by cohort/definition.
  • Remission: Generally spontaneous over months; no documented progression to destructive chronic arthritis in most patients. Reinfection is unusual (immunity).
  • Critical window: No defined therapeutic window established; symptomatic management throughout.

9. Inheritance and Population

Inheritance / genetics: Not applicable — infectious, not heritable. No inheritance pattern, penetrance, expressivity, anticipation, mosaicism, founder effect, consanguinity role, or carrier frequency.

Epidemiology: - RRV is the most common arboviral disease in Australia, with ~5,000 cases notified annually on average; annual notifications ranged from 5,653 (2010–11) to 10,074 (2014–15), totaling 28,768 over 2010–15 (PMID 33802851). National notification rate ~18.6–26.4 per 100,000/year in reported years (e.g., 22.6 per 100,000 in 2014; 18.6 in 2013) (Australian CDI NNDSS reports). - Geographic distribution: Endemic/enzootic in all Australian states, Papua New Guinea and the Solomon Islands (PMID 33802851); a major epidemic occurred in the Pacific (Fiji, Samoa, Cook Islands) in 1979–80. Rates highest in the Northern Territory (e.g., 124.4 per 100,000 in 2013) and northern/coastal Queensland and Western Australia (CDI). - Age/sex: Predominantly adults 30–64 years; regional data show slight female predominance; rare in young children (SA incidence study).

10. Diagnostics

Clinical/laboratory tests: - Serology (mainstay): RRV-specific IgM and IgG. Definitive recent infection requires IgG seroconversion or ≥4-fold rise in paired sera ~10–14 days apart, or detection of RRV-specific IgM in the presence of RRV IgG (Victoria guidance). ⚠️ IgM alone is unreliable — it can persist for months/years; "IgM serology cannot be relied upon to conclusively diagnose RRV disease" (WA GP guide). A single IgM+ within 7 days of onset gives a presumptive diagnosis to be confirmed (DVA guidance). - Nucleic acid detection (RT-PCR): useful early (brief viremia) — LOINC-codable RRV RNA assays. - Virus isolation: possible but rarely used clinically. - Nonspecific labs: often normal; mild lymphocytosis/raised ESR/CRP variably; synovial fluid is inflammatory but non-purulent (complement activation detectable — PMID 17314163).

Genetic testing: Not applicable (no WGS/WES/panel/karyotype/repeat-expansion/mtDNA role).

Imaging/electrophysiology: No specific findings; imaging used mainly to exclude alternatives.

Clinical criteria / differential diagnosis: Diagnosis integrates compatible clinical syndrome + epidemiologic exposure + serology. Differentials: Barmah Forest virus disease (very similar — key reason term "epidemic polyarthritis" is ambiguous), chikungunya and other alphaviral arthritides, dengue, parvovirus B19, rubella, rheumatoid arthritis, reactive/seronegative arthritis, and other viral arthritides (CDC; PMC89008).

Screening: No population or newborn screening (acquired infectious disease). Blood-donor relevance noted (rare transfusion transmission).

11. Outcome / Prognosis

  • Mortality: Essentially nil — "To date, there is no death reported that is directly attributed to RRV infection" (PMID 33802851). No survival-rate metric applies.
  • Morbidity / disability: The dominant burden is prolonged arthralgia, fatigue and reduced function lasting months in a substantial minority (see §8). Most recover fully.
  • Recovery: High likelihood of complete recovery; majority resolve by 5–7 months, with ~25% experiencing symptoms ≥12 months.
  • Complications: Rare encephalitis, glomerulonephritis; chronic fatigue-like syndromes have been debated.
  • Prognostic factors: Older age and greater initial symptom severity are associated with more prolonged illness (clinical guidance); no validated molecular prognostic biomarker, though inflammatory/cartilage markers (COMP, CTX-II, chemokines) track disease activity in trial settings (PMID 33711991).
  • QoL measures used: SF-36, RAPID3, grip strength (PMID 33711991).

12. Treatment

No approved antiviral or disease-specific therapy exists. Management is supportive/symptomatic (PMID 33802851; CDC).

Pharmacotherapy (symptomatic): - NSAIDs (e.g., ibuprofen, naproxen) and simple analgesics (paracetamol/acetaminophen) for arthralgia/arthritis — mainstay (NCIT: Nonsteroidal Anti-inflammatory Agent, NCIT:C198; Acetaminophen NCIT:C198? use NCIT C561 Ibuprofen, C62649 Acetaminophen). - DMARDs/corticosteroids: not established; reviews note other treatments "such as disease-modifying antirheumatic drugs (DMARDs) ... have not been proven effective in RRV clinical trials" and can carry serious adverse effects (PMID 33711991). - Pharmacogenomics: Not applicable.

Experimental / investigational: - Pentosan polysulfate sodium (PPS): Phase 2a randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial (PARA-004, ACTRN12617000893303; 20 subjects, 2 mg/kg SC twice weekly × 6 weeks). Reported improved grip strength (+6.99 kg, p=0.0189 at Day 15), RAPID3 pain/total improvements (p=0.0197/0.0101), and near-remission of joint symptoms in 61.5% (PPS) vs 14.3% (placebo); main AE was injection-site reactions. Authors concluded the results "warrant further evaluation" — but the trial is small/exploratory (Krishnan et al. 2021, PMID 33711991). NCIT: Pentosan Polysulfate Sodium (NCIT:C47707). - PG545 (pixatimod) and related heparan-sulfate mimetics: efficacy shown in mouse RRV/CHIKV disease (reduced infiltrates and proinflammatory factors) (PMID 33802851 summary). - Complement/MBL-pathway inhibition is a mechanistically rational but not yet clinical target (Morrison 2007, PMID 17314163; glycan/MBL work, PubMed 29324290).

Advanced therapeutics (gene/cell/RNA/targeted/immunotherapy): None approved or in routine use for RRV.

Surgical/rehabilitative: No surgery; physiotherapy and graded exercise support recovery of joint function (supportive care).

13. Prevention

  • Primary prevention = vector/bite avoidance (the mainstay): insect repellent (DEET/picaridin), protective clothing, avoiding peak mosquito activity, screening/netting, and mosquito control — larval source reduction, management of saltmarsh/wetland breeding sites, and public health education (NSW control guideline; PMID 33802851). Prevention "relies entirely" on bite prevention and breeding-site management.
  • Vaccine (investigational, none licensed): An inactivated, Vero cell-derived, alum-adjuvanted RRV vaccine completed a Phase 3 trial (2011–2012, 17 Australian sites, three 2.5 µg doses; 1,755 adults 16–59 y + 209 older adults), reported safe/well-tolerated and inducing "antibody titers associated with protection ... in the majority of individuals" (Phase 3, 2015, PMC4340901; Phase 1/2, PubMed 22001875). As of the retrieved sources, no RRV vaccine is licensed/available (PMID 33802851). NCIT: not applicable (investigational).
  • Secondary/tertiary prevention: Early symptomatic management; no screening program. Genetic counseling not applicable.
  • Public-health/environmental interventions: Integrated mosquito management, surveillance (sentinel chicken flocks/mosquito trapping), and climate/rainfall-based early-warning given the 0–3 month rainfall lag (PMID 33802851).

14. Other Species / Natural Disease

  • Natural disease — horses (Equus caballus, NCBI Taxon:9796): RRV causes naturally occurring, non-fatal arthritic/lethargic illness in horses; "most horses recovered several months after initial presentation"; equine signs include lethargy, muscle pain/stiffness, lameness, and submandibular lymphadenopathy; equine seroprevalence 21–91% (PMID 33802851). Horses are proposed as both a possible reservoir/amplifier and a natural disease model. ⚠️ Experimental infection was mild (1/11 horses showed clinical signs; to be "interpreted cautiously" owing to use of the old T48 prototype strain).
  • Reservoir hosts: Macropods (kangaroos/wallabies) — subclinical amplifying hosts; possums, flying foxes possibly involved. Dogs/cats remain aviraemic.
  • Zoonotic/transmission: The disease is mosquito-borne arboviral zoonosis; transmission to humans is via mosquitoes amplified on vertebrate hosts, not direct animal-to-human contact.
  • Comparative biology: RRV belongs to the arthritogenic alphavirus group (with CHIKV, Mayaro, o'nyong-nyong, Sindbis/Barmah Forest) sharing MXRA8-mediated entry and complement/monocyte-driven arthritis — a conserved pathogenic theme across these viruses (Zhang 2018; Morrison 2007).
  • Breed (VBO): No breed-specific predisposition identified.

15. Model Organisms

  • Mouse (Mus musculus, NCBI Taxon:10090) — principal model. RRV infection of mice (notably using strain T48) reproduces myositis and arthritis with monocyte/macrophage-dominated infiltrates; viremia detectable days 2–9 p.i.; severe disease can include hindlimb paralysis and death — outcomes not seen in humans/horses, a key limitation (PMID 33802851). The model established the roles of complement/C3 (C3⁻/⁻ mice: much milder disease, less muscle destruction despite similar infiltrates — Morrison et al. 2007, PMID 17314163), the MBL lectin pathway and viral N-glycans (PubMed 29324290), and MXRA8 (MXRA8-KO mice: reduced replication/disease — PMC10128017).
  • Genetic models used: gene-knockout mice — C3⁻/⁻, MBL-deficient, classical/alternative-pathway-component-deficient (retained severe disease), MXRA8-knockout, and chemokine-receptor (CCR2) studies.
  • In vitro models: murine macrophage line RAW 264.7 (persistent RRV infection), human synovial fibroblast lines (MCP-1/IL-8 induction), and CRISPR-screen cell lines (MXRA8 discovery).
  • Phenotype recapitulation: Good for acute arthritis/myositis and immunopathology; poor for (a) chronicity/relapse mechanisms in humans, (b) the lethal/paralytic outcomes that are mouse-specific, and (c) direct human monocyte infection (which requires sub-neutralizing antibody in vitro). The field's explicit caveat: human/equine immunopathogenesis "is mainly inferred from a murine model that only partially resembles clinical signs and pathology" (PMID 33802851).
  • Resources: MGI (mouse), ATCC (RAW 264.7, Cellosaurus CVCL_0493).

Terms whose name is worth a second look

The report's name for these is recognisably related to the term's own name without being one of them. A loose paraphrase reads the same way as a citation of the wrong sibling term - and so does a related synonym, which the ontology records precisely because it names something adjacent rather than the same thing - so these are listed rather than judged:

  • GO:0046718 (1 mention) - the report calls it "viral entry into host cell"; GO calls it symbiont entry into host cell, and lists "viral entry into host cell" among its other names
