Tuberculous Salpingitis (MONDO:0000271): Disease Characteristics Report

How I read the request. Tuberculous salpingitis has no causal human gene; its cause is infectious. I researched it as tuberculous infection of the fallopian tubes, caused by Mycobacterium tuberculosis complex. Clinically it almost never occurs apart from female genital tuberculosis (FGTB), so most of the evidence below is FGTB literature in which the tubes are the main site.

Causal gene: none. It is not a Mendelian disease, and no OMIM entry or causal human gene was found. The causal agent is M. tuberculosis (NCBI Taxon 1773), and occasionally M. bovis.

Coverage. This was a single, limited search session. Many template sections (omics, epigenetics, variants, pharmacogenomics) had nothing specific to this disease, and I say so where that applies. PMIDs are marked verified only when the search results showed them. Abstract quotes are short excerpts from the search snippets and should be checked against the source before going into the knowledge base.

1. Disease Information

Overview - MONDO:0000271 defines tuberculous salpingitis as a urogenital tuberculosis with an inflammatory response in the fallopian tube. It cross-references DOID:0050166 (SAIL concept library; DrugMap). - It is the most common form of FGTB. Tubal involvement is reported in 92–100% of FGTB cases (PMC3901176; PMC9699775).

Synonyms: tubal tuberculosis; tuberculous endosalpingitis; genital tuberculosis of the fallopian tube; tuberculous female pelvic inflammatory disease (the ICD-10-CM grouping).

Data provenance: The evidence is disease-level: reviews, meta-analyses and case series, mostly from India and Iran. It does not come from individual-patient EHR data.

2. Etiology

Causal factor - The cause is infection with M. tuberculosis complex. - Spread to the tube is usually secondary, through the blood from a focus elsewhere. One review states that "tuberculous involvement of the fallopian tube occurs by the hematogenous route from a TB focus elsewhere, usually pulmonary or osseous" (PMC3901176). - Lymphatic spread and direct extension from abdominal or peritoneal TB also occur (Sharma et al., Indian J Med Res 2018, PMID 30964083, verified).

Risk factors - Living in a high-TB-burden setting. Reported prevalence is higher in low-income countries (Ahmed et al., SQUMJ 2022, PMC9423760). - Reproductive age. - Previous pulmonary or extrapulmonary TB. - Immunosuppression (e.g., HIV) is a general extrapulmonary TB risk factor; I found no data on it specific to salpingitis.

Genetic risk factors: None specific to this disease were found. General TB host-susceptibility loci exist, but none were retrieved or validated for genital or tubal TB.

Protective factors: No disease-specific data. Plausible general factors are TB control, early treatment of primary TB, and BCG; none were evaluated for this outcome in the sources retrieved.

Gene–environment interactions: No data.

4. Genetic / Molecular Information

Causal genes, pathogenic variants, modifier genes, epigenetic changes and chromosomal abnormalities are all not applicable, because the disease is infectious. No ClinVar, GWAS or epigenomic data specific to this disease were found.

5. Environmental Information

  • Infectious agents: M. tuberculosis (NCBITaxon:1773); M. bovis (NCBITaxon:1765) rarely.
  • Toxins and lifestyle: no disease-specific data. Undernutrition, crowding and HIV are general TB drivers but were not quantified for this outcome.

6. Mechanism / Pathophysiology

Causal chain (based on human histopathology and imaging; the molecular steps are inferred from general TB biology)

  1. A primary M. tuberculosis infection, usually pulmonary, leads to bacteraemic or lymphatic seeding of distant organs (PMID 30964083).
  2. The richly vascular tubal mucosa and submucosa, often on both sides, receive the bacilli, which results in endosalpingitis (PMC3901176).
  3. Branch: direct extension from peritoneal TB instead results in exosalpingitis or perisalpingitis.
  4. Macrophage uptake and a T-cell (Th1) response lead to granuloma formation in the mucosa and on the serosal surface. The immune detail is inferred from general TB biology.
  5. Caseous necrosis and ulceration lead to mucosal destruction.
  6. Healing fibrosis, calcification and repeated flare-ups then result in the structural damage below (PMC3901176):
  7. tubal occlusion (often cornual or isthmic), a rigid pipestem tube and beading;
  8. fimbrial blockage leading to hydrosalpinx or pyosalpinx;
  9. peritubal adhesions.
  10. These changes result in blocked gamete transport, causing infertility and ectopic pregnancy (PMID 36438172).
  11. Descending spread to the endometrium leads to intrauterine adhesions and poor endometrial receptivity. This adds to infertility and causes hypomenorrhoea or amenorrhoea.
  12. Branch: spread to the ovary reduces ovarian reserve (PMID 36438172).

Ontology suggestions - GO biological processes: granuloma formation (GO:0002432); inflammatory response (GO:0006954); defense response to bacterium (GO:0042742); tissue remodeling (GO:0048771); collagen fibril organization (GO:0030199). These are suggested terms whose IDs have not been checked against the ontology. - CL cell types: macrophage (CL:0000235); epithelioid cell; Langhans giant cell; CD4⁺ T cell (CL:0000624); fibroblast (CL:0000057); fallopian tube ciliated epithelial cell.

Molecular profiling, omics, single-cell and CRISPR data: none found for this disease.

7. Anatomical Structures Affected

  • Primary organ: fallopian tube (UBERON:0003889). Involvement is usually bilateral but asymmetric (PMC3901176).
  • Secondary organs:
  • endometrium (UBERON:0001295), 50–60%;
  • ovary (UBERON:0000992), 10–30%;
  • peritoneum (UBERON:0002358);
  • cervix and vulva, rarely (PMC9699775).
  • Body system: female reproductive system.
  • Tissues: tubal mucosal epithelium, lamina propria and muscularis; granulomatous inflammatory tissue.
  • Subcellular: M. tuberculosis lives inside macrophage phagosomes (GO:0045335). This is general TB biology.

8. Temporal Development

  • Onset: usually ages 20–40 (reproductive age); insidious and chronic.
  • Course: from early mucosal granulomas to chronic fibrosis with adhesions and occlusion (PMC3901176).
  • Remission: treatment cures the infection, but established tubal and endometrial damage is largely permanent.
  • Critical window: diagnosis before structural damage. Outcomes are reported to be better when disease is diagnosed early and treated before fertility therapy (preprint).

9. Inheritance and Population

  • Inheritance: not applicable (acquired infection).
  • Prevalence: in a 2022 meta-analysis, pooled FGTB prevalence among infertile women was about 20% (95% CI 15–25%), with very high heterogeneity (I² ≈ 99.9%). This figure comes from a secondary snippet and was not checked against the full text (Ahmed et al., PMC9423760; probable PMID 36072071). The abstract says "the biggest burden of the disease is present in low-income countries."
  • Geography: highest in South Asia (India) and sub-Saharan Africa; rare in high-income countries.
  • Sex and age: female by definition; reproductive age.
  • Population incidence: no figure per 100,000 was found.

The disease is paucibacillary, so diagnosis relies on a combination of tests (PMID 36438172).

  • Genetic testing, omics and newborn screening: not applicable.
  • Differential diagnosis:
  • non-TB pelvic inflammatory disease (chlamydia, gonorrhoea);
  • endometriosis;
  • salpingitis isthmica nodosa (also shows a beaded tube on HSG);
  • ovarian cancer with ascites and raised CA-125, which can mimic peritoneal and adnexal TB;
  • Asherman syndrome from other causes.

11. Outcome / Prognosis

  • Mortality: low; the infection is curable.
  • Main morbidity: permanent tubal-factor infertility.
  • Conception after treatment: reviews describe the rate after anti-TB treatment as "not very encouraging" (PMC9699775).
  • IVF pregnancy rates: 7.7–68.8% across studies (Obstet Gynecol Sci review).
  • In an IVF-centre cohort of 443 women, early treatment based on a positive endometrial PCR gave pregnancy rates similar to PCR-negative women (59.8% vs 60.9%) (Jindal, Hum Reprod 2012, doi:10.1093/humrep/des076).
  • Severe endometrial damage gives a poor IVF prognosis (PMC9486627).
  • Complications: hydro- or pyosalpinx, tubo-ovarian abscess, ectopic pregnancy, recurrent pregnancy loss (PMC6943800), and Asherman syndrome. After hysteroscopic treatment of Asherman syndrome, only 14.7% reported menstrual correction (PMID 38326846; this cohort includes non-TB cases).
  • Prognostic factors: extent of tubal and endometrial damage, and timing of diagnosis.

12. Treatment

Pharmacotherapy - Treatment follows the standard 6-month regimen for drug-susceptible TB (PMC9699775; ICMR STW): - 2 months of isoniazid, rifampicin, pyrazinamide and ethambutol (2HRZE); - then 4 months of isoniazid, rifampicin and ethambutol (4HRE, under the Indian programme). - Suggested NCIT terms: Isoniazid, Rifampin, Pyrazinamide, Ethambutol, Antitubercular Agent. - Pharmacogenomics: NAT2 acetylator status affects isoniazid toxicity. This is general knowledge and was not retrieved in this session.

Effect on fertility - The effect of anti-TB treatment on pregnancy outcomes is "very uncertain". Very-low-certainty evidence from one RCT suggests little or no benefit in women without structural damage (Fernandez-Guzman et al., BMJ Open 2023;13:e070456, PMID 37758670 inferred from the MEDLINE record). - Over-treatment based on PCR alone is a concern.

Surgery and assisted reproduction - Salpingectomy or proximal tubal occlusion for hydrosalpinx before IVF may improve pregnancy rates (PMID 36438172). - Hysteroscopic adhesiolysis for Asherman syndrome. - IVF/ET, including frozen embryo transfer after treatment (PMC7340987). - Suggested NCIT terms: Salpingectomy; In Vitro Fertilization; Hysteroscopy; Laparoscopy.

Experimental: NCT05311423 is an observational study of prevalence and reproductive outcome. Gene, cell and RNA therapies are not applicable.

13. Prevention

  • Primary: control pulmonary TB (case finding, treatment, contact tracing). Neonatal BCG vaccination protects mainly against disseminated childhood TB; no tubal-TB-specific data were found.
  • Secondary: screen infertile women in endemic areas with endometrial sampling plus a composite of tests, and treat early, before structural damage.
  • Tertiary: complete anti-TB therapy; avoid HSG and hydrotubation during active disease; remove hydrosalpinges before IVF.
  • Genetic counselling: not applicable.

14. Other Species / Natural Disease

  • Cattle (Bos taurus, NCBITaxon:9913): M. bovis genital infection is rare; infection "rarely occurs through the skin... or genital tract (at breeding)" (NAP; CFSPH fact sheet).
  • A 1989 J Comp Pathol study described uterine and tubal epithelium in five cows with genital TB; I did not retrieve its details.
  • Most bovine salpingitis is non-mycobacterial (PMC6659645).
  • Zoonosis: M. bovis is zoonotic, spread through unpasteurised milk and contact with animals.
  • OMIA / breed / orthologous genes: not applicable.

15. Model Organisms

  • No dedicated tubal-TB model was found.
  • C57BL/6 mice infected intravenously with M. tuberculosis: bacilli were found in the uterine horns at day 90, with nonspecific acute and chronic inflammation of the reproductive tract. This is a partial model of hematogenous genital seeding (Russian group, Bull Exp Biol Med; e.g., PMID 27165069, unverified match; Springer).
  • Aerosol- or intratracheal-infected mice: no genital bacilli were detected in early infection (Springer 2019).
  • Limitations: mice do not form human-like caseous granulomas, the mouse oviduct anatomy differs, and none of these models shows tubal occlusion or infertility.
  • Resources: MGI lists DOID:0050166, but I found no curated models.
