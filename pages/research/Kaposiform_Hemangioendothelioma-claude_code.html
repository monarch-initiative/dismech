1. Disease Information

Overview

Kaposiform hemangioendothelioma is a rare, locally aggressive vascular tumour of intermediate (borderline) biological behaviour that arises almost exclusively in neonates, infants and young children, presenting either as an infiltrative blue–purple cutaneous/subcutaneous mass or as a deep (retroperitoneal, mediastinal, visceral, osseous) lesion. Its defining complication is the Kasabach–Merritt phenomenon — profound thrombocytopenia with consumptive coagulopathy.

Orphanet defines it as "a rare, locally aggressive, cutaneous or visceral vascular tumor characterized by either superficial lesions presenting as blue-purple infiltrated ill-defined plaques, or deep lesions usually manifesting with coagulation abnormality," associated "in about 3 out of 4 of cases with potentially lethal thrombocytopenia and consumption coagulopathy (Kasabach-Merritt phenomenon; KMP)" (Orphanet ORPHA:2122).

The authoritative modern review states that "the main pathological features of KHE are abnormal angiogenesis and lymphangiogenesis" and that KHE "may develop into a life-threatening thrombocytopenia and consumptive coagulopathy, known as the Kasabach-Merritt phenomenon" (Ji et al., Orphanet J Rare Dis 2020;15:39, PMID 32014025).

Classification

  • ISSVA 2018 classification: KHE sits in the category "locally aggressive or borderline vascular tumours" — i.e., between benign and malignant (ISSVA Classification 2018; Int J Mol Sci 2022;23:2358).
  • KHE and tufted angioma (TA) are widely regarded as one clinicopathological spectrum rather than two diseases (Ann Diagn Pathol 2020, PMID 31887709; PMC10173077; Clinical spectrum of KHE and TA, PMID 27607323).

2. Etiology

2.1 Disease causal factors

KHE is a sporadic, somatic-mosaic neoplasm. The initiating lesion is a post-zygotic activating mutation in an endothelial-lineage cell, with no evidence of inheritance, infection, or environmental trigger.

  • Primary established cause: somatic activating GNA14 p.(Gln205Leu). Lim et al. identified GNA14 Q205L in KHE alongside congenital tufted angiomas and childhood lobular capillary hemangiomas, and showed that "GNA14 and GNA11 mutations induced changes in cellular morphology and rendered cells growth-factor independent by upregulating the MAPK pathway" (in vitro) (PMID 27476652). The same hotspot causes tufted angioma with KMP (PMID 31423605).
  • Alternative/rare drivers: somatic PIK3CA (KHE reported as broadening the PIK3CA-related overgrowth spectrum — Carli et al., Clin Genet 2021, doi:10.1111/cge.14047; sirolimus-responsive KHE with PIK3CA mutation, PMID 37274236); somatic GNAQ (a previously undescribed GNAQ T96S detected by droplet digital PCR in primary tibial KHE — PMID 42103999); somatic RAD50 in 1 of 5 KHEs (PMID 31887709).
  • Mechanistic summary from the field's review: "Sporadic somatic mutations in genes such as GNA14, PIK3CA, and RAD50 have been identified in KHE," and these "are supposedly associated with abnormal VEGF-R activation and downstream PI3K/Akt/mTOR signaling in lymphatic endothelial cells (LECs), which trigger KHE growth" (inferred) (Tian et al., Pediatr Investig 2026, doi:10.1002/ped4.70041).
  • Important caveat: driver detection is incomplete. In the 7-lesion (epi)genetic series, "previously reported GNA14 mutations were not found," with drivers identified in only 2/7 (PMID 31887709). An unbiased exome study of one KHE found germline missense variants in TP53 and APC in both tumour and normal tissue plus 81 candidate tumour-specific somatic events (4 nonsense, 68 missense, 9 indels), highlighting ITGB2, IL32, DIDO1 — none of which has been replicated (PMID 28099595).
  • The precise pathogenesis "remains unclear" by the field's own assessment (doi:10.1002/ped4.70041).

2.2 Risk factors

Genetic risk factors - Causal somatic variants: as above (GNA14 Q205L principally). No germline susceptibility locus, GWAS signal, or modifier gene has been reported — I retrieved no GWAS Catalog, PheGenI, or ClinGen entry for KHE, and no familial clustering. - Co-occurring germline syndrome, gene level only (per scope rule): a single adult-onset KHE has been reported in a patient with neurofibromatosis type 1 (NF1); the authors note KHE "had not previously been reported in patients with neurofibromatosis," and that clinical suspicion was confounded because the skin findings mimicked neurofibromatosis-associated lesions (PMID 30466240). This is one case report and does not establish NF1 as a KHE risk gene. No NF2 alteration in KHE was retrieved. - Explicit negative: a somatic activating NRAS p.Q61R variant defines kaposiform lymphangiomatosis (KLA) and "was absent in KHE samples" — a useful molecular discriminator (PMC6612206).

Non-genetic/clinical risk factors These are risk factors for the severe KMP phenotype, not for tumour occurrence: - Deep anatomical location: "The risk of KMP increases dramatically when tumor infiltrates muscle or when KHE arises in the retroperitoneum or mediastinum" — retroperitoneal lesions 85% KMP, intrathoracic 100% (PMID 22871490). - Younger age at onset (≤1 month), lesion size ≥6 cm, deep or non-extremity location (PMC11032113). - Risk factors for chronic lymphedema: lesion diameter ≥10 cm, mixed morphology, extremity involvement (Orphanet J Rare Dis 2020, doi:10.1186/s13023-020-01595-2). - Risk factor for bone destruction: later diagnosis (median age at diagnosis 19.0 months vs earlier in those without) (J Pediatr Surg 2024). - Age: ~90% of KHE occurs under 1 year (Orphanet); 93% manifest in infancy, 60% as neonates (PMID 22871490). - Sex, family history, toxins, lifestyle, occupation, radiation: no association retrieved. Sex distribution is essentially equal (§9).

2.4 Gene–environment interactions

No gene–environment interaction has been described for KHE. I retrieved no CTD, PheGenI, or GxE-database entry. The only quasi-"environmental" modifier with evidence is pharmacological: PIK3CA- and mTOR-pathway-mutant lesions respond to sirolimus (PMID 37274236), and GNA14-driven MAPK activation was proposed by Lim et al. as a therapeutic target — i.e., genotype-by-drug interaction rather than genotype-by-environment (PMID 27476652).

3.2 Quality-of-life impact (per-phenotype where available)

A cross-sectional PedsQL™ 4.0 study of 91 children with KHE (Infant Scales, Generic Core Scales, Family Impact Module) found that "the scores of physiological and psychosocial domains in all age groups of patients with KHE were lower than those of normal children," and that KMP and activity dysfunction are independent risk factors for poor HRQOL (Ji group, PMID 35281237).

  • Pain / functional phenotype → daily activity: "the infiltration of muscles, connective tissues and joint structures can cause pain and functional limitations, which may affect a patient's abilities to perform routine daily activities" (doi:10.1002/ped4.70041).
  • Caregiver burden: family-impact scores were below 75 across physical, emotional, social, cognitive, communication, worry, daily-activity, family-relationship and financial domains (PMID 35281237).
  • Lymphedema phenotype → chronic disability: irreversible and sirolimus-resistant, managed by compression (doi:10.1186/s13023-020-01595-2); its clinical/management profile has been directly compared with primary lymphedema in children (PMC11869848).
  • EQ-5D / SF-36 / PROMIS data for KHE: none retrieved (KHE is a paediatric disease; PedsQL is the instrument in use).

4.2 Pathogenic variants

  • Canonical variant: GNA14 c.614A>T, p.(Gln205Leu) — a missense change; "All GNA14 mutations found were Q205L, with most caused by c.614A>T transversions" across vascular-neoplasm series. ClinVar entry VCV000548667 is annotated as reported "in several unrelated individuals with GLUT-1 negative vascular tumors, including kaposiform hemangioendothelioma" (ClinVar 548667).
  • Variant class / consequence: missense, gain of function (constitutive activation). Mechanistically, Gln205 is the catalytic glutamine of the Gα GTPase: "The conserved glutamine stabilizes the γ phosphate of GTP and orients a water molecule for nucleophilic attack," and "the Gln205Leu mutation shifts the residue 5.016 Å away from the catalytic water molecule, disrupting hydrolysis," so that the mutant is "predicted to impair GTP hydrolysis and lead to constitutive activation of the GTP-bound α-subunit" (Lim et al. 2016, AJHG PDF; mechanism review Springer 2026).
  • Somatic vs germline: somatic/mosaic, confined to lesional tissue. Detection typically requires high-sensitivity methods — ddPCR "can detect GNA11 or GNA14 mutant cells with mutant allelic frequencies ranging from 0.3 to 5.0%" and down to ~0.1% (search-retrieved ddPCR performance data; applied in KHE in PMID 42103999).
  • Population allele frequency: not applicable / not meaningful. As a somatic hotspot, Q205L is not expected in gnomAD/1000 Genomes constitutional data; I retrieved no population frequency and make no claim.
  • ACMG/AMP classification: KHE variants are somatic and therefore classified under somatic (oncogenicity) frameworks, not germline ACMG/AMP tiers. I retrieved the ClinVar record's reporting context but not an explicit germline ACMG classification, and do not assert one.
  • Broader context: GNA14 is "the most commonly mutated gene in vascular neoplasms" per an 85-case cherry-hemangioma study, and GNA14/GNA11/GNAQ mutations are "frequent in benign but not malignant cutaneous vascular tumors" (Nat/Mod Pathol 2019; PMC8141909) — consistent with KHE's borderline, non-metastasising behaviour.

4.4 Epigenetic information

This is one of the better-characterised molecular layers:

Genome-wide methylation profiling of 5 KHEs and 2 TAs: "Unsupervised hierarchical clustering analysis indicated a common methylation pattern of KHEs and TAs, which separated from the homogeneous methylation pattern of vascular malformations," concluding that "methylation profiling provided further evidence for KHEs and TAs potentially forming a spectrum of one entity" (ten Broek et al., Ann Diagn Pathol 2020, PMID 31887709).

No disease-specific DNA-methylation biomarker, histone modification, or chromatin-remodelling defect has been established beyond this classifier-style finding. I retrieved no ENCODE/Roadmap/DiseaseMeth KHE-specific entry.

4.5 Chromosomal abnormalities

No recurrent cytogenetic abnormality is established in KHE. A single refractory KHE has been reported in association with a balanced chromosomal translocation (Zhou et al., Pediatr Dev Pathol 2016, doi:10.2350/15-09-1707-cr.1); per this report's scope rules I do not restate the breakpoints. This observation is unreplicated and should not be treated as a diagnostic or classificatory feature. Chromosomal microarray and karyotyping have no established role in KHE diagnosis (see §10).

5. Environmental Information

  • Environmental factors: none identified. No toxin, radiation, pollutant, or occupational exposure has been linked to KHE in the retrieved literature; I found no CTD/TOXNET/EPA entry for this disease. Given the congenital/neonatal onset in 60–84% of cases (PMID 22871490; PMC11032113), a post-zygotic developmental mutational event is the operative model, leaving little temporal window for post-natal environmental causation (inferred).
  • Lifestyle factors: not applicable (paediatric, congenital-onset tumour). No maternal lifestyle association retrieved.
  • Infectious agents: explicitly excluded. KHE is HHV-8–negative: "HHV-8 immunohistochemical stain helps distinguish KS from KHE, as it is positive in KS while negative in KHE," with HHV8 negative in 7/7 cases in one series, and "the absence of human herpes virus 8 (HHV-8) in KHE indicates a different pathogenesis from Kaposi sarcoma despite the histological resemblance" (PMC7962213; PMC8633177). No bacterial, fungal or parasitic agent implicated. Secondary infection is a treatment-related complication, not an etiology (PMC11032113).

6. Mechanism / Pathophysiology

6.1 Ordered causal chain — initiating lesion → clinical manifestation

  1. A post-zygotic somatic activating mutation arises in an endothelial-lineage precursor in developing soft tissue — most often GNA14 c.614A>T p.(Gln205Leu); in individual cases PIK3CA, GNAQ (T96S), or RAD50 instead — which produces a mosaic clone of mutant endothelial cells restricted to the lesion. (human clinical + molecular: PMID 27476652, PMID 42103999, PMID 31887709)
  2. Substitution of the catalytic Gln205 displaces the catalytic water and abolishes GTP hydrolysis, which results in Gα14 being locked in its GTP-bound state → constitutive Gq-family signalling. (structural/biochemical inference from Gα mechanism: AJHG 2016)
  3. Constitutive Gα14 signalling leads to MAPK/ERK pathway activation, which causes altered endothelial-cell morphology and growth-factor-independent proliferation — demonstrated directly in transfected cells and in GNA14-Q205L endothelial xenografts, where "ECs with GNA14 p.Gln205Leu implants displayed significantly higher vascularization compared to wild-type controls." (in vitro + animal model: PMID 27476652; Front Cell Dev Biol 2025)
  4. In parallel, VEGF-C→VEGFR-3 and ANGPT2→TIE2 receptor signalling converge on PI3K–AKT–mTOR in lymphatic endothelial cells, which drives coupled dysregulated angiogenesis and lymphangiogenesis — the review states the "pathophysiological basis is dysregulated lymphatic and blood vascular angiogenesis, primarily mediated by the vascular endothelial growth factor-C (VEGF-C)/vascular endothelial growth factor receptor-3 (VEGFR-3) and angiopoietin-2 signaling pathways," and that "Ang-2 ligand-induced Tie-2 activation triggers Akt/mTOR signaling, which in LECs is mainly mediated by PIK3CA." This step is inferred, supported by pathway/expression data and by the therapeutic response to mTOR inhibition rather than by direct lineage-tracing in KHE. (doi:10.1002/ped4.70041; PMID 32014025)
  5. Clonal expansion of mutant endothelium produces the tumour mass: infiltrating nodules and sheets of variably spindled endothelial cells with slit-like vascular channels, glomeruloid nests, and malformed/abnormal lymphatic channels, which manifests as an ill-defined mass crossing multiple tissue planes. (histopathology: EyeWiki/KHE histology synthesis; PMC7962213)
  6. Hypoxic/HIF signalling amplifies the phenotype — in a 3-D spheroid KHE model and EOMA xenografts, "HIF-1α expression was significantly upregulated," which is proposed to further drive angiogenic output. (in vitro + animal model, mechanistically suggestive: Front Cell Dev Biol 2025)

Branch A — haematological (the Kasabach–Merritt step of this tumour; see its own entry):

  1. Podoplanin-expressing, lymphatic-type tumour endothelium is exposed to flowing blood, which causes platelet CLEC-2 to engage tumour podoplanin: "Binding of podoplanin to CLEC-2 transmits platelet activation signals via Src family kinases, which may account for platelet aggregation in kaposiform hemangioendothelioma," and "Platelet CLEC-2, tumor podoplanin interaction is considered the key mechanism of platelet clearance." High-flow arteriovenous shunting within the lesion provides an additional activating stimulus. (human clinical + in vitro: PMID 32014025; EyeWiki)
  2. Platelet activation and physical trapping lead to intralesional platelet–fibrin microthrombi, which results in secondary activation of the coagulation cascade and consumption of clotting factors → profound thrombocytopenia, hypofibrinogenemia, raised D-dimer, microangiopathic hemolytic anemia = KMP. (human clinical + histopathology: microthrombi and hemosiderin are standard histological findings)
  3. KMP leads to purpura and bleeding diathesis, transfusion dependence, and — untreated — death; it also feeds back on the tumour, since Ang-2 release both marks and plausibly worsens endothelial destabilisation: Ang-2 was "10-fold greater in KLA patients and 14-fold greater in KHE patients when the KMP coagulopathy was present but not when it was absent." (human clinical biomarker: PMID 27990590; PMID 38439088)
  4. Chronic CLEC-2/GPVI engagement depletes platelet surface receptors, which causes a superimposed qualitative platelet defect: "CLEC-2 receptors on the platelet surface were significantly decreased in patients with KHE/Kasabach-Merritt phenomenon compared with healthy donors," so that "platelet responses induced by CLEC-2 or GPVI activation are impaired because of the diminished number of receptors" — bleeding risk therefore exceeds what the platelet count alone predicts. (human clinical: Blood Adv 2023, PMID 37307200)
  5. Accompanying immune perturbation — neonates with KMP show reduced CD3+CD4+ helper T-cell and CD19+ B-cell fractions and lower IgG, with prolonged APTT and raised ferritin independently associated with severe thrombocytopenia, suggesting an inflammatory/immune-activation component layered onto the coagulopathy. (human clinical, associative: PMID 42445171)

Branch B — local structural/mechanical:

  1. Infiltrative growth into muscle, fascia, joint capsule and bone leads to cortical erosion and bone destruction (26.0% of patients), which results in periarticular and muscular fibrosis → chronic pain and decreased range of motion in ~70% of affected patients within 2 years, "primarily attributed to the tumor's invasive growth and consequent periarticular and muscular fibrosis." (human clinical: J Pediatr Surg 2024; PMID 30233248)

Branch C — lymphatic:

  1. Involvement and obstruction of regional lymphatics leads to lymphatic stasis — found in 100% of involved lesions on lymphoscintigraphy — which results in chronic, largely irreversible, sirolimus-resistant lymphedema (11.0% at 1 year). (human clinical: PMC13545610; doi:10.1186/s13023-020-01595-2)

Therapeutic interruption of the chain (step 4 is the druggable node):

  1. mTOR inhibition with sirolimus blocks the PI3K–AKT–mTOR node, which produces reduced endothelial proliferation and migration, anti-lymphangiogenic and anti-fibrotic effects, and reduced platelet activation/inflammation: "The prevention of platelet activation and inflammation, along with antiangiogenic effects, the inhibition of lymphangiogenesis, the attenuation of fibrosis, or a combination of all these effects, may be responsible for the therapeutic effects of sirolimus" (PMID 39369447). A direct cellular mechanism was identified in 2025: in CD31⁺ KHE cells isolated from patients, transcriptome sequencing "revealed downregulation of ATG9B after sirolimus treatment," and "sirolimus may inhibit the proliferation of CD31+ KHE cells by suppressing the expression of ATG9B," with reduced migration vs DMSO (in vitro, patient-derived: Li et al., J Cell Mol Med 2025, doi:10.1111/jcmm.70903).

6.2 Category checklist mapped onto the chain

  • Molecular pathways: Gq/Gα14 → MAPK/ERK (steps 2–3); VEGF-C/VEGFR-3 and ANGPT2/TIE2 → PI3K–AKT–mTOR (step 4); HIF-1α (step 6); CLEC-2 → Src/Syk platelet signalling (step 7). Loss of TSC2 and PTEN function has also been invoked as a route to abnormal mTOR activation in KHE (inferred, doi:10.1002/ped4.70041).
  • Cellular processes: growth-factor-independent proliferation; angiogenesis; lymphangiogenesis; endothelial migration; platelet activation/aggregation and clearance; fibroblast-mediated fibrosis; inflammation with macrophage recruitment (CD68⁺ macrophages as a source of VEGF; IL-6 and TNFα as pro-angiogenic cytokines) (partly general endothelial biology rather than KHE-specific — flagged as inferred); autophagy-related gene regulation (ATG9B).
  • Protein dysfunction: gain-of-function, GTPase-dead Gα14 (not misfolding/aggregation); constitutive GPCR-independent signalling output.
  • Metabolic changes: no KHE-specific metabolomic or energy-metabolism defect retrieved. HIF-1α upregulation implies a hypoxia/glycolytic shift (inferred only). Note the diagnostically relevant negative: lesional endothelium is GLUT1 (SLC2A1)-negative, distinguishing KHE from infantile hemangioma (PMC7962213).
  • Immune involvement: not autoimmune, not an immunodeficiency syndrome. Secondary findings: reduced helper-T/B fractions and IgG in neonatal KMP (PMID 42445171); treatment-induced immunosuppression from sirolimus/corticosteroids is clinically important (§12).
  • Tissue damage mechanisms: microthrombosis/ischemia within the lesion; fibrosis (periarticular, muscular) as the principal irreversible mechanism; bone erosion; haemorrhage.
  • Biochemical abnormalities: no enzyme deficiency, receptor loss, or channelopathy. The core biochemical lesion is defective GTP hydrolysis by a Gα subunit.
  • Epigenetic changes: shared KHE/TA methylation signature, distinct from vascular malformations (PMID 31887709).
  • Molecular profiling: Transcriptomics — bulk transcriptome sequencing of patient-derived CD31⁺ KHE cells ± sirolimus (ATG9B) (doi:10.1111/jcmm.70903). Proteomics, metabolomics, lipidomics: no KHE studies retrieved. Genomic structural features: no recurrent structural variant (§4.5).

Multi-omics integration and CRISPR/RNAi functional screens in KHE: none retrieved. 7. Anatomical Structures Affected

7.1 Organ level

Primary sites (most-to-least frequent, per retrieved series): "KHE most commonly involves the deep soft tissues of the extremities and the retroperitoneum, followed by the head and neck regions," with additional primary sites including pancreas and ischiorectal region (Springer Encyclopedia of Pathology entry; PMC7962213).

Documented primary/involved organ sites in the retrieved case literature: skin and subcutis; skeletal muscle; bone (humerus PMID 39031761; femur PMID 36405826; tibia PMID 42103999; cervical spine PMID 30896568; long bones, 12-case osseous series PMID 41680958); retroperitoneum (PMC10064051); mediastinum/thorax (JOVA 2024); pancreas (PMID 25755958, PMID 36072541); spleen (Am J Hematol, doi:10.1002/ajh.26370); GI tract (PMID 39709514, PMC6476061); liver, lung, bone marrow, brain (adult multifocal case, PMC10917883); skull base/cranial fossa with dural invasion (PMID 37354290, PMID 36545011); middle ear (ScienceDirect); nasal cavity/endonasal (PMC13212465, PMID 42026286); oral cavity, gingiva, tonsil, sublingual gland (PMC8633177, PMID 40281141, PMC3118378, PMID 37275074); eyelid (PMC10797699).

Secondary organ involvement / complications: haematopoietic system (KMP); regional lymphatics (lymphedema, lymphatic stasis); joints (destruction, contracture); kidney (hydronephrosis from mass effect); heart (pericardial effusion, high-output states); airway (obstruction); intestine (obstruction, volvulus, intestinal failure — PMC13365911) (PMC11032113).

Body systems involved: integumentary, musculoskeletal, cardiovascular (blood and lymphatic vasculature), haematological/coagulation, digestive, respiratory, and occasionally nervous (by local invasion). Endocrine: not involved.

7.2 Tissue and cell level

  • Tissues: dermis and subcutaneous adipose/connective tissue; skeletal muscle; fascia; bone (cortex, metaphysis, articular surface); synovium/periarticular soft tissue; visceral stroma.
  • Cell populations: the neoplastic cell is an endothelial cell with partial lymphatic differentiation. Immunophenotype: diffusely CD31+ and CD34+; focal/partial PROX1, D2-40 (podoplanin), LYVE-1 positivity; GLUT1-negative; HHV-8-negative (PMC13291430; PMC7962213). The original D2-40 study concluded that "D2-40 positivity suggests lymphothelial differentiation of the neoplastic component of kaposiform hemangioendothelioma" (Mod Pathol, doi:10.1038/3800444). Important nuance: in some cases D2-40/PROX1 expression is confined to peripheral lymphatic channels rather than tumour cells, and glomeruloid structures lack these antigens (PMC13222785; doi:10.1007/s00256-026-05245-9).
  • Non-neoplastic participants: platelets (trapped/consumed); erythrocytes (microangiopathic fragmentation); fibroblasts (fibrosis); macrophages (VEGF source).
  • Cell Ontology: CL:0002138 (endothelial cell of lymphatic vessel) — verified. Blood vessel endothelial cell, platelet, fibroblast, macrophage, erythrocyte — IDs not verified here.

7.4 Localization and lateralization

  • UBERON (verified): UBERON:0002097 — skin of body (Uberon).
  • UBERON terms suggested, IDs not verified here: subcutaneous adipose tissue; skeletal muscle tissue; retroperitoneal space; mediastinum; pancreas; spleen; long bone; synovial joint; lymphatic vessel.

8. Temporal Development

Onset

  • Age: "KHE manifested in infancy in 93% of cases, with 60% presenting as neonates" (PMID 22871490); Orphanet: "about 90% of KHE occur in children under 1 year of age," onset possible at all ages (Orphanet). In a Chinese cohort, 27% were neonates and lesions were present at birth in 84% (PMC11032113). Approximately half of cases are described as congenital (encyclopedia entry).
  • Median age at diagnosis: 6.5 months (range 0.3–14.0) in the comparison with KLA (PMC6612206); 19.0 months in those who develop bone destruction (J Pediatr Surg 2024).
  • Adult onset is genuinely rare but established: the first adult series comprised 3 patients aged 64, 55 and 48 (PMID 9322599); adult KHE lesions are smaller (two-thirds <2 cm) and do not show thrombocytopenia, in contrast to >50% of children (PMID 30466240); adult multifocal and multi-bone presentations are described (PMC10917883; PMID 37522676).
  • Prenatal onset: detectable antenatally; "The combination of tumor growth, infiltrative margins, hypervascularity, and T2-hyperintense signal on MRI consistently supported the antenatal suspicion of KHE," with 3 of 4 fetuses showing prenatal signs of evolving KMP (Fraissenon et al., Prenat Diagn 2026, doi:10.1002/pd.70180).
  • Onset pattern: typically acute to subacute — a rapidly enlarging mass with abrupt haematological decompensation; deep/visceral lesions can be insidious, e.g. splenic KHE "presenting as insidious consumptive coagulopathy" (doi:10.1002/ajh.26370).

Progression

  • No formal staging system (AJCC/WHO) exists for KHE. Instead, risk stratification by severity of thrombocytopenia is used: low-risk, high-risk, and extremely high-risk. In one cohort: 8 (11%) low-risk, 26 (37%) high-risk, 36 (52%) extremely high-risk; "Extremely high-risk cases have the highest mortality and the highest complication rate as well as irreversible functional damage; in contrast, patients in the low-risk group have minimal complications and the highest cure rate" (PMC11188338).
  • Progression rate: variable — rapid proliferative growth in infancy with acute KMP, then a slow chronic phase dominated by fibrosis. The tibial case showed "lesion progression with cortical erosion and soft tissue extension" over 11 months (PMID 42103999).
  • Course pattern: progressive, then chronic/stable with sequelae, punctuated by episodic KMP flares and post-treatment rebound. "Rebound growth occurred in 17.3% of patients upon sirolimus discontinuation" (PMID 39369447); a documented case of KMP rebound during sirolimus therapy exists (PMC9391052).
  • Duration: chronic, frequently lifelong at the level of residual tissue change: "residual lesions persist to some extent in all patients even after standard treatment" and "no intervention other than complete surgical excision can achieve a definitive 'cure'" (Zhou et al., Int J Cancer 2025, doi:10.1002/ijc.35344; doi:10.1002/ped4.70041). At median 56 months' follow-up of 269 patients, 17.4% had active disease and/or altered body structures (J Pediatr Surg 2024).
  • Treatment duration is severity-scaled: "patients without KMP or with small lesions typically require therapy for several months to a year, whereas those with extensive, infiltrative lesions complicated by KMP often require long-term management" (doi:10.1002/ijc.35344).

Patterns

  • Spontaneous remission: classically absent — "Unlike infantile hemangiomas, KHE rarely undergoes spontaneous regression" (PMC11032113) — but now documented: four infants with biopsy-proven KHE without KMP underwent spontaneous regression on conservative management, with serial MRI showing involution over 22–28 months and no KMP or complications; the authors argue this "challenges universal aggressive intervention, supporting observation for selected non-coagulopathic cases" (Xu et al., Pediatr Blood Cancer 2026, PMID 41834121). An older series followed five patients with cutaneous KHE long-term (PMID 11706068).
  • Treatment-induced remission: the norm with sirolimus ± prednisolone (§12).
  • Critical periods / windows of opportunity:
  • Neonatal–early infantile window for KMP control — KMP is the dominant mortality driver and responds within days–weeks to sirolimus + prednisolone (Ji et al., Blood 2022;139:1619–30, doi:10.1182/blood.2021014027).
  • The first ~2 years after diagnosis for musculoskeletal outcome — ~70% of patients with bone destruction develop chronic pain/dysfunction within that window (J Pediatr Surg 2024); delayed diagnosis predicts bone destruction, making early recognition the main modifiable determinant of disability.
  • A prenatal window now exists — first use of maternal sirolimus therapy for fetal KHE/KMP showed, on MRI 3 weeks after initiation, "decreased extent of infiltration and reduced T2-signal intensity," with postnatal platelet normalisation by 6 weeks on sirolimus + corticosteroid (doi:10.1002/pd.70180).

Genetic etiology parameters

Because KHE is caused by post-zygotic somatic mutation, the classical Mendelian parameters are not applicable, and I state this rather than inventing values:

  • Inheritance pattern: none — sporadic/somatic mosaic. No familial case or vertical transmission retrieved. (Orphanet's gene page for GNA14 exists as a gene record but does not establish Mendelian KHE inheritance — Orphanet GNA14.)
  • Penetrance / expressivity / anticipation / carrier frequency / consanguinity / founder effects: not applicable; none retrieved. (The exome study's incidental germline TP53/APC missense variants were present in both tumour and normal tissue of one patient and were not shown to be causal — PMID 28099595.)
  • Germline mosaicism: no evidence; KHE mutations are somatic and lesion-restricted and would not be expected to recur in siblings (inferred).
  • Geographic distribution of specific variants: none described; GNA14 Q205L has been reported across unrelated individuals without geographic clustering (ClinVar 548667).

Population demographics

  • Sex ratio: essentially 1:1. Two independent datasets: "Equal gender distribution was observed" (PMC7962213); and 32 girls : 38 boys (slight male excess, 0.84:1 F:M) in a 70-patient cohort (PMC11032113).
  • Age distribution: ~90% <1 year; 93% infantile; 60% neonatal; adult cases rare (Orphanet; PMID 22871490; PMID 9322599).
  • Ethnic / geographic variation: none established. Cohorts come from the USA, China, Poland, Egypt, Australia and Europe with broadly similar phenotypes (doi:10.1002/pbc.30779; PMID 41466529; doi:10.1186/s43054-025-00362-x; PMID 42246503). Differences in reported KMP frequency (40% US vs 71% Boston referral vs ~78% thrombocytopenia in China) most plausibly reflect referral and ascertainment bias, not population biology (inferred). One paper specifically addresses the under-recognised challenge of diagnosing vascular anomalies in patients with skin of colour (PMC11495159).

10. Diagnostics

10.1 Clinical/laboratory tests

Core laboratory panel (used for diagnosis, risk stratification, and monitoring): - Platelet count — KMP broadly defined as <100 ×10⁹/L (PMID 22871490); severity of thrombocytopenia drives risk group (PMC11188338). - Fibrinogen, D-dimer, PT/APTT — hypofibrinogenemia and raised D-dimer define the consumptive coagulopathy; fibrinogen stabilisation at week 4 was a trial endpoint (doi:10.1182/blood.2021014027). Prolonged APTT and raised ferritin independently associate with severe neonatal thrombocytopenia (PMID 42445171). - CBC with film — microangiopathic hemolytic anemia. - Platelet function testing — CLEC-2/GPVI-induced aggregation is impaired, with reduced surface CLEC-2 (PMID 37307200); currently a research-grade test. - LOINC codes: not retrieved in this session; standard codes for platelet count, fibrinogen, D-dimer, APTT should be assigned at ingestion.

Biomarkers - Serum angiopoietin-2 (Ang-2) is the best-supported circulating biomarker. Levels were "14-fold greater in KHE patients when the KMP coagulopathy was present but not when it was absent," and a cut-off of 10,000 pg/mL has been proposed to distinguish KLA and KHE-with-KMP from other vascular anomalies; after 12 months of sirolimus, Ang-2 falls, "correlating with normalization of platelet counts and D-dimer reductions" (Le Cras et al., PMID 27990590; Hammill et al., PMID 38439088; Blood 2025 abstract on broader use). Ang-1 is also informative. - Genetic markers: GNA14 Q205L in lesional tissue (see §10.2). - No FDA-qualified biomarker for KHE was retrieved.

Imaging - Ultrasound (first-line): "a poorly demarcated, heterogeneously mixed-echoic subcutaneous mass with rich vascularity, often displaying a characteristic 'branch-shape' flow pattern on 3-D color Doppler and increased stiffness on elastography" (PMC13291430). Contrast-enhanced ultrasound has been applied in multi-organ abdominal KHE (PMC12719487). - MRI (modality of choice): in a 64-case series, lesions are "hypointense or isointense compared with muscle on T1-weighted sequences, and hyperintense on T2-weighted or inversion-recovery sequences," with "an infiltrative morphologic pattern that involves multiple tissue planes and has poorly defined borders, along with stranding of the adjacent subcutaneous fat and cutaneous thickening," diffuse heterogeneous post-contrast enhancement, speckled/striated low-signal foci, and no significant diffusion restriction (BMC Pediatr 2021;21:98, PMID 33657997; PMID 29536769; PMID 39511620; PMID 40495592). - Ultrasound radiomics (emerging): diagnostic accuracy 91.41%, sensitivity 77.44%, specificity 97.21%, outperforming conventional ultrasound; "radiomics showed excellent efficacy in the diagnosis of KHE, which is of great help in improving the early diagnosis rate" (PMID 39553205). - Lymphoscintigraphy (emerging): "effectively characterizes lymphatic dysfunction in KHE, aiding in both initial diagnosis and long-term management of secondary lymphedema," with lymphatic stasis in 100% of involved lesions (PMC13545610). - Angiography: characterised for KHE with assessment of transcatheter arterial embolization value (PMID 35070833). - ¹⁸F-FDG PET/CT: reported in retroperitoneal KHE with spinal involvement (PMC9403054). - Dermoscopy: described for superficial lesions (PMID 40653948). - Prenatal imaging: fetal ultrasound (increased echogenicity, soft-tissue hypertrophy, hypervascularity), fetal MRI (T2-hyperintense infiltrative mass), plus MCA Doppler, fetal echocardiography for anemia/cardiac failure/hydrops (doi:10.1002/pd.70180; Egypt J Radiol Nucl Med 2023). - RadLex/DICOM codes: not retrieved.

Biopsy and pathology — the diagnostic gold standard "The diagnosis is mostly based on pathological biopsy" (Front Oncol 2024). - Histology: infiltrating nodules/sheets of variably spindled endothelial cells, slit-like vascular channels, glomeruloid nests, absence of mitoses or nuclear atypia, microthrombi, hemosiderin deposition, oedema, fibrosis, and abnormal lymphatic channels (histology synthesis; Pathology Outlines). - Immunohistochemistry panel: CD31+, CD34+; D2-40/podoplanin, PROX1, LYVE-1 focally or partially + (sometimes only in peripheral lymphatics); GLUT1 −; HHV-8 − (PMC13291430; PMC7962213; doi:10.1038/3800444). - A dedicated histological-differential review exists (Pathology/Histopathology review). - Functional tests and electrophysiology (EEG/EMG/ECG/NCS): no role in KHE diagnosis; not retrieved.

10.2 Genetic testing

  • Recommended approach: somatic (tumour-tissue) testing, not germline testing. Because variant allele fractions are low, high-sensitivity methods on lesional tissue are required — ddPCR detected the GNAQ T96S in tibial KHE where other assays did not (PMID 42103999), and ddPCR reaches ~0.1–5% VAF for GNA11/GNA14 (retrieved ddPCR performance data).
  • Targeted single-gene/hotspot testing: GNA14 c.614A>T; consider GNAQ, GNA11, PIK3CA (PMID 27476652; doi:10.1111/cge.14047).
  • Gene panels: vascular-anomaly / childhood-cancer somatic panels have been piloted in vascular anomalies (JOVA pilot study). No KHE-specific validated panel was retrieved.
  • WES/WGS: research utility demonstrated (identified GNA14 and the single-case candidate list) but low clinical yield given frequent driver-negative results (PMID 27476652; PMID 28099595; PMID 31887709).
  • Chromosomal microarray, karyotyping, FISH, mtDNA testing, repeat-expansion testing: no established role; nothing retrieved supporting their use in KHE.
  • Clinical significance: genetic testing is currently confirmatory/research-oriented, not required for diagnosis or treatment selection — sirolimus is given on clinical grounds.

10.3 Omics-based diagnostics

  • DNA methylation profiling can separate KHE/TA from vascular malformations and is the most promising classifier-style omics assay, though it remains research-grade (PMID 31887709).
  • RNA-seq: used mechanistically on patient-derived CD31⁺ cells, not diagnostically (doi:10.1111/jcmm.70903).
  • Proteomics, metabolomics, epigenomic clinical assays, liquid biopsy / circulating tumour DNA for KHE: none retrieved. Serum Ang-2 is the only blood-based assay with clinical traction and is a protein biomarker rather than a liquid biopsy.

10.4 Clinical criteria and differential diagnosis

There is no single validated diagnostic criterion set; diagnosis rests on the triad of clinical presentation + characteristic imaging + histopathology (with IHC), now formalised in three consensus documents: Drolet et al. 2013 (PMID 23796341), the 2025 Chinese/international consensus statement (doi:10.1002/ijc.35344) — developed by "28 nationally peer-recognized experts in vascular anomalies and an expert in evidence-based medicine" across three subgroups — and the European VASCERN-VASCA pathways (PMID 41389323), built using the nominal group technique.

10.5 Screening

No screening programme exists or is indicated. There is no newborn screening, carrier screening, or cascade screening for KHE — appropriate for a sporadic somatic neoplasm with no heritable risk. The only screening-like activity retrieved is prenatal imaging surveillance of an already-suspicious fetal soft-tissue mass, where "The diagnosis of Kaposiform Hemangioendothelioma should be suspected in prenatal period if there is unilateral hypertrophy of limb which has evolved rapidly over a short interval of time and shows findings suggestive of Kasabach–Merritt Phenomenon" (Egypt J Radiol Nucl Med 2023; doi:10.1002/pd.70180).

Morbidity and function

Morbidity — not mortality — is now the dominant outcome problem: - Residual disease is universal: "residual lesions persist to some extent in all patients even after standard treatment" (doi:10.1002/ijc.35344). - 17.4% had active disease and/or altered body structures at median 56 months (J Pediatr Surg 2024). - Bone destruction in 26.0%, with chronic pain and functional impairment in ~70% of those within 2 years, predominantly lower extremity and worse with articular-surface/metaphyseal involvement (J Pediatr Surg 2024). - Chronic lymphedema in 11.0% at one year, unilateral, mostly moderate, irreversible and sirolimus-resistant (doi:10.1186/s13023-020-01595-2). - Pain in 30–50%, with limb dysfunction from fibrosis/adhesion (PMC13291430). - HRQOL is reduced across physiological and psychosocial domains in all age bands, with KMP and activity dysfunction as independent risk factors; caregiver HRQOL is also impaired, including financial burden (PMID 35281237). No ICF-coded disability dataset retrieved.

Complications

Hemorrhage; severe infection (both disease- and treatment-related); hydronephrosis; anemia (32.8%); airway, intestinal, duodenal and pericardial compromise by site; bone/joint destruction; chronic lymphedema; intestinal failure (resolved with medical therapy plus near-fat-free oral feeding in one report — PMC13365911); sirolimus-related severe thrombocytopenia in progressive disease (PMID 36688881); rebound growth in 17.3% on sirolimus discontinuation (PMID 39369447); local recurrence ~15% after incomplete resection (PMC13291430).

Recovery potential

  • With treatment: haematological recovery is the rule — 35/37 achieved durable platelet response at week 4 on sirolimus + prednisolone (doi:10.1182/blood.2021014027); 92.2% (154 patients) had a durable response to sirolimus in long-term follow-up (Zhou et al., Int J Cancer 2023;153:600–608, doi:10.1002/ijc.34509). Complete anatomical cure requires complete excision, which is often not feasible.
  • Without treatment: spontaneous regression is possible in selected KMP-negative lesions (4 infants, MRI involution over 22–28 months) (PMID 41834121), but KHE "does not regress spontaneously" in the general case and "tends to metastasize locally as well as to the regional lymph nodes" (PMC7962213).
  • Low-risk group: "no patients who underwent surgery experienced recurrence during the maximum follow-up of five years"; this group has "minimal complications and the highest cure rate" (PMC11188338).

Prognostic factors and biomarkers

  • Adverse: presence/severity of KMP; deep lesions without cutaneous involvement, especially retroperitoneal or mediastinal; infiltration of muscle; large size (≥6 cm for KMP risk, ≥10 cm for lymphedema); later diagnosis (predicts bone destruction); extremely-high-risk stratum; delayed/inadequate treatment (PMID 22871490; PMC11032113; PMC13291430; PMC11188338).
  • Prognostic/monitoring biomarkers: platelet count (primary), fibrinogen, D-dimer, and serum Ang-2 (falls with response, concordant with sirolimus response) (PMID 27990590; PMID 38439088). In neonatal KMP, prolonged APTT and elevated ferritin independently predict severe thrombocytopenia (PMID 42445171).
  • No validated multivariable prognostic model or clinical calculator for KHE was retrieved.

12. Treatment

12.1 Pharmacotherapy

First-line: sirolimus (rapamycin), an mTOR inhibitor — NCIT term: Sirolimus (NCIT code not verified in this session).

  • "Sirolimus has been established as the first-line therapy for KHE," and its introduction "has fundamentally improved patient outcomes, reducing mortality to below 2%" (Front Oncol 2024; doi:10.1002/ijc.35344).
  • Mechanism: mTOR/PI3K/AKT inhibition with reduced VEGF production, inhibition of endothelial growth and angiogenesis, anti-lymphangiogenic and anti-fibrotic effects, reduced platelet activation and inflammation, plus T-cell inhibition; at cellular level, suppression of ATG9B in CD31⁺ KHE cells (PMID 39369447; doi:10.1111/jcmm.70903).
  • Pivotal prospective evidence: Adams et al. phase II in complicated vascular anomalies — 12 courses of 28 days; "sirolimus was efficacious and well tolerated in these study patients with complicated vascular anomalies, with clinical activity reported in the majority of the disorders" (PMID 26783326). Earlier KHE-specific experience: PMID 26864138; long-term single cases PMID 30002799.
  • Dosing (as used in the randomised trial): sirolimus 0.8 mg/m² twice daily, adjusted monthly to trough 10–15 ng/mL (NCT03188068; doi:10.1182/blood.2021014027).
  • Dose intensity is now being de-escalated: a randomised clinical trial compared high- vs low-dose sirolimus in KHE (PMID 40107509); low-dose regimens have been reported successful in neonates/infants (PMC12284785).
  • Topical sirolimus has been tested in a randomised phase II trial for cutaneous vascular anomalies (PMID 40040594) and used with oral propranolol in superficial KHE (PMC11180431).

Corticosteroids — NCIT term: Prednisolone. - Sirolimus + prednisolone is superior to sirolimus alone for KHE with KMP. In the randomised trial (n=73): "At week 4, a durable platelet response was achieved by 35 of 37 patients given sirolimus and prednisolone compared with 24 of 36 patients given sirolimus monotherapy (difference 27.9%; 95% confidence interval, 10.0-44.7)," with better median platelet counts weeks 1–4, more fibrinogen stabilisation at week 4, and better objective lesion response at month 12; the authors conclude "sirolimus plus prednisolone should be considered a valid treatment of KHE with KMP" (Ji et al., Blood 2022;139:1619–30). Prednisolone 2 mg/kg once daily, taperable and stoppable within 4–6 weeks on satisfactory response (NCT03188068). - Corticosteroid-to-sirolimus sequential therapy has been described as a strategy in infantile KHE with KMP (PMID 41141660); sirolimus + glucocorticoid combinations are reported (Medicine 2024).

Vincristine — NCIT: Vincristine (NCIT C933, per retrieved NCIT reference). - The 2013 expert consensus recommended vincristine (± corticosteroids) as first-line for KHE with KMP (PMID 23796341). - It remains important: a 19-year single-centre series (Sydney) found "Vincristine remains a highly effective first-line treatment for kaposiform haemangioendothelioma with an acceptable safety profile" — 16/21 (76%) received vincristine, 3 (14%) escalated to sirolimus; complications were "common but mild in most cases" (Kirk et al., J Paediatr Child Health 2026, PMID 42246503). - In the US multicentre cohort, vincristine gave higher 3-month radiologic response than sirolimus (72.7% vs 20%, p = .03) but no difference at 6 months and no difference in recurrence/progression (doi:10.1002/pbc.30779). - VASCERN guidance: "In cases where there is a life-threatening localisation or failure to respond to sirolimus and corticosteroids, vincristine should be added to the therapeutic regimen" (PMID 41389323).

Other agents reported in use (Front Oncol 2024; PMC13291430): propranolol / oral β-blockers, aspirin (low-dose; three-case series in KHE with coagulopathy — PMID 41895209), intravenous immunoglobulin, platelet transfusion and cryoprecipitate (supportive), anti-inflammatory therapy, haemostatic therapy. Topical tacrolimus with compression bandaging succeeded in one case (PMID 35700108). - β-blocker vs sirolimus comparison (2026, Beijing): 13 β-blocker vs 11 sirolimus patients; at 9 months, >25%/>50%/>75% regression in 76.9%/46.2%/23.1% (β-blocker) vs 100.0%/72.7%/18.2% (sirolimus); discontinuation in 9/13 vs 3/11 (median 40.0 vs 18.0 months) with no recurrence after cessation; safety events "reversible and manageable," but "baseline imbalances limit direct comparison" (PMID 43053660).

Pharmacogenomics No KHE-specific pharmacogenomic study was retrieved. General sirolimus pharmacogenetics are directly relevant: sirolimus "is a substrate of the ABCB1 pump (p-glycoprotein) and metabolized by the CYP3A4 and CYP3A5 enzymes," and weight plus CYP3A5 genotype are the covariates affecting clearance; a paediatric model recommended 0.20 mg/kg/day split BID for CYP3A5*3/*3 vs 0.23 mg/kg/day for CYP3A5*1 carriers (5–30 kg) (PMC7300220; PMC4628029). ABCB1 SNP effects were not significant in one transplant study. Model-informed precision dosing in children is an active area (PMC10069443). This is extrapolated from non-KHE sirolimus populations — flagged as inferred.

12.2 Advanced therapeutics

  • Gene therapy, cell therapy, RNA-based therapy (ASO/siRNA/mRNA), CAR-T, checkpoint inhibitors, therapeutic monoclonal antibodies: none exist for KHE; none retrieved. No clinical trial of any of these for KHE was found.
  • Targeted therapy in KHE currently means mTOR inhibition. A rational but as-yet-untested extension follows from the biology: Lim et al. framed GNA14-driven MAPK activation as identifying "potential targets for therapeutic intervention" (PMID 27476652), and MEK inhibition is being used in adjacent complex lymphatic anomalies (trametinib efficacy/safety study — PMID 41116710). I found no trial or case series of MEK inhibition specifically in KHE, and make no efficacy claim.

12.3 Surgical and interventional

  • Surgical resection is the only modality that can cure: "complete surgical resection has been touted as the gold standard for cure of KHE," but "total resection is often not a viable option given the high risk of morbidity and mortality," and incomplete resection carries ~15% local recurrence with progression risk (PMC13291430).
  • Indications: "small, well-localized lesions that are not complicated by KMP, or as an adjunct to systemic pharmacotherapy in the management of refractory disease"; pharmacotherapy failure lowers the threshold for resecting a cosmetically or functionally problematic lesion (PMC13291430; doi:10.1002/ijc.35344).
  • Risk-group practice: surgery was primary for low-risk patients (no recurrence to 5 years) and used in 92% of the high-risk group; "Early surgical intervention may be the most definitive treatment option for most patients with KHE" (PMC11188338). Curative operations for visceral disease are documented, e.g. a neonate with pancreatic KHE and duodenal obstruction "cured by Whipple operation" (PMID 25755958); chest-wall resection (doi:10.1177/2050313x16684742); congenital intracranial KHE resected (PMC9012330).
  • Transarterial embolization (TAE): "can be considered as a temporary measure for life-threatening KMP or as a preoperative adjunct," but is "relatively contraindicated for lesions involving critical regions such as the intracranial space or hepatic hilum" (PMC13291430); angiographic characteristics and TAE value have been formally analysed (PMID 35070833); commentary on TAE in neonatal KMP (PMC9251004).
  • NCIT intervention terms suggested (codes not verified here): Surgical Resection, Therapeutic Embolization, Compression Therapy, Platelet Transfusion.

12.4 Supportive and rehabilitative

  • Haematological support: platelet transfusion, cryoprecipitate, immunoglobulin — used judiciously, as transfused platelets are consumed by the same trapping mechanism (mechanistic inference from §6 Branch A) (Front Oncol 2024).
  • Pain control for the 30–50% with pain (PMC13291430).
  • Compression therapy is the mainstay for chronic lymphedema besides oral sirolimus (doi:10.1186/s13023-020-01595-2); management parallels and differs from primary paediatric lymphedema (PMC11869848).
  • Nutritional support: near-fat-free oral feeding plus medical therapy resolved KHE-related intestinal failure in one report (PMC13365911).
  • Physical/occupational therapy: indicated for joint contracture and decreased range of motion given the ~70% rate of chronic functional impairment with bone destruction (J Pediatr Surg 2024; PMID 30233248) — I retrieved no KHE-specific rehabilitation protocol or trial, so this is a clinical extrapolation.

12.6 Treatment outcomes and adverse events

  • Response rates: overall response >70% (PMC11188338); >60% at 3 months and >70% at 6 months in the multicentre cohort (doi:10.1002/pbc.30779); 92.2% durable response to sirolimus long-term (doi:10.1002/ijc.34509).
  • Sirolimus toxicity (phase II, grade ≥3 attributable): blood/bone marrow 27%, gastrointestinal 3%, metabolic/laboratory 3% (PMID 26783326). Paradoxical sirolimus-related severe thrombocytopenia in progressive KHE is reported (PMID 36688881); a systematic review of oral sirolimus safety in childhood diseases is available (PMC9497617). Infection risk and vaccination timing are live clinical issues (NCT05351216).
  • Vincristine toxicity: complications "common but mild in most cases," with one proven bloodstream infection and one central-line replacement in 16 treated patients (PMID 42246503).
  • Resistance/relapse: rebound growth in 17.3% on discontinuation; sirolimus-resistance mechanisms remain unresolved (PMID 39369447).
  • No FAERS-based KHE-specific adverse-event analysis was retrieved.

12.7 Treatment strategy and algorithm

Synthesising the three consensus documents and the risk-group data:

  1. Confirm diagnosis (clinical + MRI/US + biopsy with IHC; consider Ang-2) (doi:10.1002/ijc.35344; PMID 41389323).
  2. Risk-stratify by thrombocytopenia severity and lesion extent/location (low / high / extremely high) (PMC11188338).
  3. KMP-negative, small, resectable → surgery, or observation in selected cases given documented spontaneous regression (PMC11188338; PMID 41834121).
  4. KMP-positive → sirolimus + prednisolone as the evidence-based combination (doi:10.1182/blood.2021014027); vincristine remains a legitimate first-line in some centres and is added for life-threatening localisation or sirolimus/steroid failure (PMID 42246503; PMID 41389323).
  5. Extremely high-risk → multimodality therapy is "the best choice," including interventional options; TAE as a bridge (PMC11188338; PMC13291430).
  6. Maintenance and withdrawal individualised by severity; months-to-a-year for small/KMP-negative disease, long-term for extensive KMP disease; taper carefully given 17.3% rebound (doi:10.1002/ijc.35344; PMID 39369447; NCT07131644).
  7. Lifelong sequelae management: compression for lymphedema, pain management, orthopaedic/rehabilitation follow-up, residual-lesion surveillance (doi:10.1186/s13023-020-01595-2; J Pediatr Surg 2024).

Personalised/genotype-guided medicine: not yet practised in KHE. Genotype currently does not alter therapy; sirolimus is used empirically regardless of GNA14/PIK3CA status, although PIK3CA-mutant KHE responding to sirolimus is mechanistically coherent (PMID 37274236). No OncoKB/CIViC/My Cancer Genome KHE entry was retrieved.

13. Prevention

Framing caveat stated plainly: KHE arises from a sporadic somatic mutation during development. Primary prevention is not possible, and no preventive intervention exists. What the literature supports is almost entirely secondary and tertiary prevention — earlier recognition and complication avoidance.

  • Primary prevention: none. No modifiable risk factor, no vaccine-preventable or exposure-mediated cause (§2, §5). No USPSTF/CDC/WHO recommendation exists for KHE.
  • Immunization: no preventive vaccine. Vaccination is nonetheless a clinical concern during treatment because of sirolimus-induced immunosuppression — the subject of a dedicated trial (NCT05351216).
  • Secondary prevention (early detection) — the real opportunity:
  • Early diagnosis is the main modifiable determinant of disability. Patients who develop bone destruction were "diagnosed later than those without bone destruction" (median 19.0 months) (J Pediatr Surg 2024); the field notes "there is still a lack of early diagnosis approaches for KHE" (Front Oncol 2024).
  • Ultrasound radiomics is explicitly positioned as an early-detection tool: 91.41% accuracy, "of great help in improving the early diagnosis rate" (PMID 39553205).
  • Prenatal recognition permits planned delivery and, in a first report, maternal sirolimus therapy before birth (doi:10.1002/pd.70180).
  • Awareness of atypical presentations prevents missed diagnosis: 11% lack cutaneous findings and older children present with musculoskeletal complaints (PMID 22871490).
  • Tertiary prevention (preventing complications in diagnosed patients): prompt sirolimus ± prednisolone to abort KMP (doi:10.1182/blood.2021014027); early intervention to limit infiltration before fibrosis and bone erosion become irreversible (J Pediatr Surg 2024); compression to limit lymphedema progression (doi:10.1186/s13023-020-01595-2); careful tapering to avoid the 17.3% rebound (PMID 39369447).
  • Genetic screening / carrier screening / PGD / prenatal genetic testing: not indicated. Because the driver is somatic and non-heritable, there is no carrier state, no PGD indication, and no cascade testing role. No ACMG/ACOG recommendation for KHE was retrieved.
  • Genetic counselling: the appropriate content is reassurance about non-heritability and negligible recurrence risk in future pregnancies — a reasonable inference from the somatic-mosaic etiology, though I retrieved no KHE-specific counselling guideline and flag it as inferred rather than sourced.
  • Risk stratification for prevention: the clinical risk-group scheme serves this purpose (PMC11188338); KMP risk prediction uses age ≤1 month, size ≥6 cm, deep/non-extremity location (PMC11032113).
  • Behavioural interventions, public-health interventions, environmental interventions, prophylactic medication: none applicable; none retrieved. (Infection prophylaxis during immunosuppression is standard supportive care but I retrieved no KHE-specific prophylaxis protocol and do not state one.)

14. Other Species / Natural Disease

Taxonomy and natural occurrence

KHE does occur naturally in non-human animals, though it is exceptionally rare.

  • Dog (Canis lupus familiaris) — the first reported case of KHE in any non-human animal species: a 10-year-old male dog; "The lesion was completely reexcised, and the dog remained healthy 6 months later without evidence of recurrence or metastasis" (Vincek, Zaulyanov & Mirzabeigi, Vet Pathol 2004;41:695, doi:10.1354/vp.41-6-695).
  • Cat (Felis catus) — gastroduodenal junction KHE, described as "the first documented case involving the alimentary tract in animals," with pathological findings and follow-up (Vet Res Commun 2026;50:256, doi:10.1007/s11259-026-11195-3).
  • NCBI Taxon IDs for these species were not retrieved in this session and should be assigned at ingestion.

Breed

No breed predisposition reported. Both published animal cases are single animals; no VBO identifier applies.

Orthologous genes

Not retrieved. I did not retrieve NCBI Gene IDs for GNA14 orthologues in dog, cat, mouse, rat or zebrafish in this session, and no animal KHE case has been genotyped for GNA14. This is a clear gap: no molecular confirmation exists that spontaneous animal KHE shares the human driver.

Comparative biology and veterinary relevance

  • Classification parallel: "The spectrum of blood vessel neoplasms comprises benign (hemangioma), intermediate (hemangioendothelioma), and malignant (hemangiosarcoma) tumors," and in veterinary medicine hemangioendothelioma "is classified as an intermediate vascular tumor and is rare among animal species, with a low number of documented cases in dogs, cattle, and one bird" — mirroring KHE's borderline position in ISSVA (Vet Res Commun 2026; Merck Veterinary Manual).
  • Veterinary importance is low: "vascular tumors are common in dogs, occasionally observed in cats, but rare in other domestic or wild animals," yet hemangiosarcoma dominates, and hemangioendothelioma "seems rare in most species," reported occasionally in canine skin, spleen, lung and brain (Vet Res Commun 2026; review of CNS proliferative vascular disorders).
  • Key comparative difference: the two animal KHE cases were adult-onset and not reported with KMP, whereas human KHE is an infantile tumour defined by KMP — i.e., the animal lesions reproduce the histological entity but not the haematological syndrome.
  • Evolutionary conservation: the Gα/GTPase catalytic-glutamine mechanism is deeply conserved across Gα subunits and species (mechanistic reviews), making a conserved oncogenic route plausible (inferred, not demonstrated in animals).
  • OMIA: no OMIA entry for hemangioendothelioma/KHE was retrieved; I searched and the results explicitly did not contain OMIA records.

Transmission

Zoonotic potential: none. KHE is a non-infectious somatic neoplasm, HHV-8-negative (§5), with no transmissible agent and therefore no zoonotic or cross-species susceptibility.

15.3 Phenotype recapitulation and limitations

  • Best-recapitulated feature: KMP. Both the patient-derived xenograft (PMID 39025210) and EOMA models reproduce consumptive thrombocytopenia/bleeding diathesis — the single most clinically important phenotype.
  • Best-recapitulated molecular feature: GNA14-driven MAPK activation and hypervascularisation (PMID 27476652).
  • Not recapitulated: the infiltrative growth across tissue planes, bone destruction, periarticular/muscular fibrosis, chronic lymphedema, and the partial lymphatic (D2-40/PROX1) immunophenotype of human KHE — none of these has been demonstrated in any retrieved model. There is also no model with a defined human driver genotype that also produces KMP (the GNA14 models give vascularisation; the KMP models are driver-undefined).
  • Translational use demonstrated: sirolimus mechanism dissection in patient-derived CD31⁺ cells (doi:10.1111/jcmm.70903); anti-angiogenic/immunomodulatory intervention testing in EOMA mice (topical imiquimod — J Invest Dermatol; IL-12 gene therapy — J Invest Dermatol; MCP-1 angiogenic role — Am J Physiol 2003).

Evidence quality summary and principal gaps

Strong evidence (human clinical, prospective/randomised): sirolimus efficacy (PMID 26783326); superiority of sirolimus + prednisolone for KMP (doi:10.1182/blood.2021014027); high- vs low-dose sirolimus RCT (PMID 40107509).

Strong evidence (large cohorts / consensus): KMP frequency and risk factors (PMID 22871490); mortality reduction to ~1.3–2% (doi:10.1002/pbc.30779; doi:10.1002/ijc.35344); bone destruction 26% and its functional consequences (J Pediatr Surg 2024); HRQOL impairment (PMID 35281237).

Moderate evidence: GNA14 Q205L as driver (functionally validated in vitro, but reported in small numbers and absent in some series); Ang-2 as biomarker; methylation-based KHE/TA unification.

Weak or absent — the real gaps for a knowledge base to flag: 1. Driver detection is incomplete — a majority of sequenced KHEs in at least one series had no identified driver (PMID 31887709). 2. No single-cell, spatial, proteomic, metabolomic, or lipidomic data on KHE at all (searched; none retrieved). 3. No functional genomics screen (CRISPR/RNAi) in KHE. 4. No model combining a defined human driver genotype with the KMP phenotype, and no germline/conditional mouse. 5. No population-level epidemiology beyond one US region; no ICD-11 or KHE-specific MeSH code retrieved. 6. No therapeutic alternative to mTOR inhibition in trials — the MAPK node implied by GNA14 biology is untested in KHE. 7. Formal HPO annotation set for ORPHA:2122/MONDO:0016236 could not be retrieved directly; MalaCards reports 14 HPO terms for KHE but the full list was not retrievable in this session, so the phenotype table above proposes terms rather than reproducing the official annotation file.
