Lupus Vulgaris (MONDO:0006838): Disease Characteristics Report

How I read the request: Lupus vulgaris (LV) is an infectious disease. It is a chronic, paucibacillary form of cutaneous tuberculosis caused by Mycobacterium tuberculosis complex organisms. It has no causal human gene. The template records none, and I found no gene or OMIM entry attributed to LV. The only "causal" genome is that of the pathogen (M. tuberculosis, occasionally M. bovis or BCG). The genetic sections below are therefore marked mostly not applicable, with host-susceptibility context given only where I retrieved it. LV is also unrelated to systemic or cutaneous lupus erythematosus, despite the shared name.

Retrieval limits: PubMed abstract pages and Europe PMC were partly unavailable during this run (cookie walls and HTTP 503 errors), and a search on animal models was blocked. Each item below is labelled by how it was checked: - Verified means I read the page or record and checked its identifiers. - Index means the PMID or details come from a search-engine result, and I did not read the abstract.

1. Disease Information

Overview - LV is "a chronic, progressive, paucibacillary form of cutaneous tuberculosis" (Behera et al., Cureus 2023, PMID 37575838, verified; https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10420341/). - "Lupus vulgaris is the most common form of cutaneous tuberculosis in adults" (Pai et al., Indian Dermatol Online J 2014, PMID 25396129, verified; https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4228641). - A 2026 review describes it as "the most common and indolent form in regions of moderate tuberculosis endemicity" (Knecht-Gurwin et al., J Clin Med 2026, PMID 41598640, verified; https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12842349/).

MONDO definition (inherited from MeSH): "A form of cutaneous tuberculosis. It is seen predominantly in women and typically involves the nasal mucosa; buccal mucosa; and conjunctival mucosa." This is verified text. Note that recent series report roughly equal sex ratios (Section 9).

Synonyms: MONDO lists none. In the literature, LV is also called "lupus vulgaris tuberculosis" and grouped as reinfection or post-primary cutaneous TB, a paucibacillary form.

Source type: All evidence is aggregated literature: case series and case reports. None comes from EHR-derived resources.

2. Etiology

Causal factor - LV is caused by infection with M. tuberculosis complex organisms in a host who already has immunity to them. - "Lupus vulgaris is caused by hematogenous, lymphatic, or contiguous spread from elsewhere in the body" (PMID 25396129, verified). - It can also arise by direct inoculation, including from BCG vaccine: "Lupus vulgaris is a paucibacillary form of cutaneous tuberculosis that occurs either via hematogenous spread or direct inoculation" (Parmar et al., An Bras Dermatol 2021, PMID 34272074, verified).

Risk factors - Prior sensitisation. LV "generally affects previously sensitized patients, who have a delayed, strongly positive hypersensitivity reaction to the tuberculin skin test" (Jaka-Moreno et al., Actas Dermosifiliogr 2012;103:251-3; https://actasdermo.org/en-lupus-vulgaris-caused-by-mycobacterium-articulo-S1578219012001047; PMID not retrieved). - Living in a TB-endemic area. Indian series dominate the literature (Singal et al., Int J Dermatol 2022, DOI 10.1111/ijd.16267; PMID 35583803 per index). - Older age in Europe. One source states that "in Europe, lupus vulgaris is characteristically a disease of elder people, whereas in developing countries younger persons are mostly concerned" (Springer case report, s13555-016-0158-x; search snippet only, paywalled). - Exposure to M. bovis. This means contact with infected animals or ingestion of unpasteurised milk (Jaka-Moreno 2012). - BCG vaccination. LV after BCG "occurs in 5 per million vaccinations" (PMID 34272074, verified). The risk is reportedly higher after repeated vaccination (search summary). - Immunosuppression and genetic susceptibility have been proposed, but I retrieved no LV-specific data.

Genetic risk factors: No LV-specific genetic association study was found. - Inborn errors of the IL-12/IFN-γ axis (IL12RB1, IFNGR1, IFNGR2, IL12B, STAT1) cause Mendelian susceptibility to mycobacterial disease (MSMD) and predispose to BCG and mycobacterial disease in general. - Candidate-gene studies link SNPs in IFNG, IFNGR1, IL12A, IL12B, IL12RB1 and STAT4 to TB in general (e.g., https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4930082; OMIM 601604). - None of these studies examined LV directly.

Protective factors - Pasteurisation of milk and eradication of bovine TB lowered the incidence of LV caused by M. bovis (Jaka-Moreno 2012). - Effective treatment of underlying TB foci is also protective. No protective genetic variants are known.

Gene–environment interaction: No LV-specific data are available. The general principle is that the host's IFN-γ/Th1 competence shapes the clinical form that follows mycobacterial exposure (Santos et al., An Bras Dermatol 2014, PMID 24770496, verified): "The clinical manifestations are variable and depend on the interaction of several factors."

4. Genetic/Molecular Information

Not applicable. No causal genes, pathogenic variants, ClinVar entries, modifier genes, epigenetic findings or chromosomal abnormalities have been reported for LV. - The pathogen is the relevant genome: M. tuberculosis (NCBITaxon:1773) and M. bovis (NCBITaxon:1765). - M. bovis "is inherently resistant to pyrazinamide" (PMID 34272074, verified). This has practical consequences for treatment. - Multidrug-resistant LV has been reported (Indian Dermatol Online J 2017; DOAJ record https://doaj.org/article/7090fd94460d45a39e64692636f0db5e; PMID not retrieved).

5. Environmental Information

  • Infectious agents: M. tuberculosis is the usual cause. M. bovis is rare: one review found 4 cases of M. bovis LV in 20 years (Jaka-Moreno 2012). Attenuated M. bovis BCG causes LV at vaccination sites (PMID 34272074; PMID 35024215, index).
  • Exposures: Raw milk, infected cattle, and residence in TB-endemic regions.
  • Lifestyle factors: No LV-specific data were retrieved for smoking, alcohol or diet.

6. Mechanism / Pathophysiology

Ordered causal chain

  1. A person previously sensitised to M. tuberculosis complex, with a strongly positive tuberculin response, is exposed to mycobacteria (Jaka-Moreno 2012).
  2. Bacilli reach the dermis by one of two routes:
  3. 2a. Hematogenous or lymphatic spread, or contiguous extension, from an endogenous focus such as the lungs or lymph nodes (PMID 25396129).
  4. 2b. Exogenous inoculation, including the BCG vaccination site (PMID 34272074).
  5. Dermal macrophages ingest the bacilli. This leads to antigen presentation and activation of a Th1 / IFN-γ response. The response in TB "occurs basically via the Th1 pathway" (PMID 24770496). That this step operates in LV specifically is inferred from general TB immunology.
  6. IFN-γ and TNF result in macrophages differentiating into epithelioid cells and Langhans giant cells, which form well-organised tuberculoid granulomas. This is the histology seen in all 19 cases of Behera 2023 (PMID 37575838).
  7. Effective but incomplete containment leads to a paucibacillary state, in which bacilli are scarce or undetectable and culture is positive in fewer than 10% of cases (Jaka-Moreno 2012). The infection persists as a low-grade, chronic infection.
  8. Chronic granulomatous inflammation results in papules and plaques that slowly spread at the edge while scarring and atrophy develop in the centre. Destruction of cartilage and soft tissue causes disfigurement over years to decades (PMID 41598640). The disease then branches:
  9. 6a. Epidermal reaction leads to pseudoepitheliomatous hyperplasia, which gives hypertrophic or verrucous variants. Alternatively, the epidermis becomes atrophic.
  10. 6b. Decades of inflammation and scarring can lead to malignant transformation, usually into squamous cell carcinoma, at a reported rate of 0.5–10.5% (PMID 31149488). The mechanism, a Marjolin-type tumour driven by chronic inflammation, is inferred from case reports.

Checklist coverage - Immune involvement: Th1-polarised delayed-type hypersensitivity. "There are still no comparative studies of immune status across the clinical forms" (search summary of PMID 24770496). - Cellular processes: GO:0002432 granuloma formation; GO:0042742 defense response to bacterium; GO:0032729 positive regulation of type II interferon (IFN-γ) production; GO:0042110 T cell activation. - Cell types: CL:0000235 macrophage; CL:0000622 epithelioid cell (verify the term); multinucleated giant cell (CL:0000647, verify the ID); CL:0000545 T-helper 1 cell; CL:0000312 keratinocyte (hyperplasia and SCC); lymphocyte. - Omics, single-cell, spatial and CRISPR data: None were retrieved for LV.

7. Anatomical Structures Affected

  • Primary organ: skin of the head and neck (UBERON:0002097 skin of body). The face is most often involved: the nose (UBERON:0000004), cheek (UBERON:0001567) and ear (UBERON:0001690). The MONDO definition also lists nasal, buccal and conjunctival mucosa.
  • Neck: Neck involvement is uncommon. The Turkish neck case (PMID 21738972, index) was described as the "fifth case in the literature".
  • Extremities and buttocks: These are reported sites, especially in Indian series (Behera 2023; Pai 2014).
  • BCG-associated LV: This occurs at the vaccination site, usually the deltoid skin (UBERON:0001482).
  • Tissue level: dermis (UBERON:0002067) and epidermis (UBERON:0001003). Cartilage is affected in destructive disease.
  • Subcellular level: phagosome / phagolysosome (GO:0045335, GO:0032010). This is inferred from the general biology of mycobacteria inside macrophages.
  • Laterality: Usually a single, asymmetric plaque. Multifocal disease occurs.

8. Temporal Development

  • Onset: Insidious and chronic. Diagnosis is often delayed:
  • The mean duration before diagnosis was 15.3 months in the Singal 22-year cohort.
  • A German M. bovis case was diagnosed about 20 years after onset (Springer, search snippet).
  • Course: Slowly progressive over years to decades. Lesions extend at the edge and scar in the centre.
  • Long-standing disease: SCC has arisen after more than 60 years (PMID 31149488) and after 50 years (PMID 20046107, index).
  • Spontaneous remission: Rare and incomplete; scarring persists.
  • Remission with treatment: Antitubercular therapy brings treatment-induced remission (Section 12).
  • BCG-associated LV: Onset ranges from weeks to years after vaccination, with a reported mean of about 1 year (search summary).
  • Window for intervention: Early treatment prevents disfigurement and malignant change (PMID 41598640).

9. Inheritance and Population

  • Inheritance: Not applicable (infectious disease).
  • Burden in India:
  • Cutaneous TB makes up about 0.1–2% of dermatology outpatient attendance in Indian reports (search summary).
  • LV accounted for 40.4% of 1,458 cutaneous TB patients over 22 years in Delhi, ahead of scrofuloderma (32.7%) and lichen scrofulosorum (15.2%). Of the cohort, 803 patients were children (Singal 2022).
  • In smaller series, LV made up 51% (19 of 37) of cutaneous TB in an Indian armed-forces series (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3221221) and is cited as 75% of cutaneous TB in Behera 2023.
  • Burden in Europe: It is "the most common form of CTB in Europe and India" (PMID 24770496, body text). LV "was the most frequent clinical form of true skin tuberculosis until the 1960s" (PMID 31149488). In Europe it now mostly affects elderly people.
  • Sex ratio:
  • Female-to-male ratio of 1.1:1 in Behera 2023.
  • Equal numbers of each sex in Pai 2014.
  • The MONDO/MeSH definition says LV is predominantly seen in women.
  • Age: Adults predominate. Scrofuloderma is more common than LV in children (Pai 2014).
  • Population incidence: No incidence or prevalence per 100,000 was retrieved.

10. Diagnostics

Histopathology (the mainstay) - Tuberculoid granulomas made of epithelioid cells, Langhans giant cells and a lymphocytic rim, usually with little or no caseation. - "Histologically it is charecterised by typical tubercles with or without caseation" (PMID 25396129; spelling as in the source). - Behera 2023: "all showed a tuberculoid granuloma with no demonstration of acid-fast bacilli" (PMID 37575838). - The epidermis may be atrophic or show pseudoepitheliomatous hyperplasia.

Microbiology - Ziehl–Neelsen staining is usually negative. - Culture is positive in fewer than 10% of cases (Jaka-Moreno 2012), but it identifies the species (e.g., M. bovis). - Overall, "almost all of the diagnostic methods confer lower sensitivity and specificities" (Khadka et al., Dermatol Res Pract 2018, PMID 30111996, verified).

Molecular tests - PCR on tissue has a reported positivity of about 60% in LV, taken from a literature table (search summary). - Nested PCR on paraffin-embedded tissue was positive in 24 of 30 cutaneous TB samples, but only 2 of these were LV (PMID 30531542, index). - PCR was negative in the culture-proven German M. bovis case.

Immunological tests - The tuberculin skin test is strongly positive (Jaka-Moreno 2012). - IGRA has not been studied specifically in LV in the sources retrieved.

Bedside tests - Diascopy shows the apple-jelly sign. - Dermoscopy shows yellow-orange structureless areas, which also occur in sarcoidosis.

Imaging and other work-up - Chest X-ray is used to look for an underlying focus. No LV-specific yield data were retrieved.

Therapeutic trial - When tests are inconclusive, a trial of antitubercular therapy is used diagnostically. The trial duration is debated (see the Ramam-type study in Section 12).

Differential diagnosis - Cutaneous sarcoidosis, discoid lupus erythematosus, leprosy (tuberculoid), basal cell carcinoma, cutaneous leishmaniasis, deep fungal infection, granuloma faciale, and rosacea or granulomatous rosacea (DermNet; search summaries).

Genetic testing - Not applicable. Consider MSMD gene testing (IL12RB1, IFNGR1, etc.) only if mycobacterial disease is disseminated or BCG-associated disease is severe. This is a general principle, not an LV-specific recommendation.

Screening - Asymptomatic people are not screened for LV.

11. Outcome/Prognosis

  • Mortality: LV itself rarely kills. No LV-specific survival or mortality data were retrieved.
  • Morbidity: Scarring, mutilation of the nose and ears, and contractures.
  • Malignancy:
  • SCC is the main malignant complication, at a reported rate of 0.5–10.5% (PMID 31149488).
  • "Chronic LV lesions persisting for decades may undergo malignant transformation" (PMID 41598640).
  • Basal cell carcinoma has also been reported (Vora et al., IJDVL 1995, per search summary).
  • SCC has also occurred in multidrug-resistant LV (IDOJ 2017).
  • Prognostic factors: Long disease duration, delayed diagnosis, drug resistance, and tissue destruction (inferred from the case literature).
  • Recovery: Good with therapy, with residual scarring. The German M. bovis case healed completely.

12. Treatment

Pharmacotherapy - Standard antitubercular therapy follows WHO guidance: isoniazid, rifampicin, pyrazinamide and ethambutol until susceptibilities are known (search summary of CMAJ 2025, https://www.cmaj.ca/content/197/8/E215). - Usual adult daily doses are isoniazid 5 mg/kg (maximum 300 mg), rifampicin 10 mg/kg (maximum 600 mg), pyrazinamide 20–25 mg/kg and ethambutol 15–20 mg/kg (dosing table, https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2923933). - India's ICMR Standard Treatment Workflow for skin TB is available at https://icmr.gov.in/icmrobject/uploads/STWs/1725964688_7_skin_tuberculosis_18032022.pdf. I did not read its content. - Suggested NCIT terms: Isoniazid (C576), Rifampin (C787), Pyrazinamide (C774), Ethambutol (C61758). These codes are from memory; verify them.

Treatment response - In a 2006 Indian study of 213 cutaneous TB patients on daily rifampicin plus isoniazid for 9 months, "ninety-two per cent of the patients showed resolution of the lesions within the first 6 months," and none relapsed over 3 years (Epistemonikos record https://www.epistemonikos.org/en/documents/1dd3a1455dc994b841da132b1d053ea46522b5aa; PMID not retrieved). - BCG-related LV: treat for M. bovis resistance to pyrazinamide, since the organism is "inherently resistant to pyrazinamide and ethambutol is avoided in children due to its ophthalmologic side effects" (PMID 34272074).

Surgery - "Surgical excision of residual or recalcitrant lesions are crucial to prevent complications" (PMID 41598640). - Reconstructive surgery is used for deformities. - SCC arising in LV is managed by excision.

Historical treatment - Niels Finsen received the 1903 Nobel Prize "for treating diseases, especially lupus vulgaris, with concentrated light radiation" (https://www.nobelprize.org/prizes/medicine/1903/finsen/facts/). Antibiotics later replaced phototherapy.

Gene, cell, RNA, targeted and immune therapies - Not applicable. No registered LV-specific trials (NCT numbers) were found.

Adverse effects - These are the standard effects of antitubercular therapy: hepatotoxicity (isoniazid, rifampicin, pyrazinamide) and optic neuritis (ethambutol). This is general pharmacology, not retrieved specifically for LV.

13. Prevention

  • Primary prevention:
  • TB control in general.
  • Milk pasteurisation and eradication of bovine TB, which reduced M. bovis LV (Jaka-Moreno 2012).
  • Good BCG technique and avoiding repeat BCG vaccination. BCG itself is a rare cause of LV, at about 5 per million doses.
  • Secondary prevention: Early biopsy of chronic plaques on the face or neck, and early treatment.
  • Tertiary prevention:
  • Complete the full course of antitubercular therapy.
  • Excise residual lesions.
  • Follow up long-standing lesions for a long time to catch SCC; the 50-year case authors argued for extended follow-up (PMID 20046107, index).
  • Genetic counselling: Not applicable.

14. Other Species / Natural Disease

  • No naturally occurring animal disease equivalent to LV was found (OMIA not applicable).
  • Zoonotic link: Cattle (Bos taurus, NCBITaxon:9913) infected with M. bovis are a source of human LV through contact or raw milk (Jaka-Moreno 2012).
  • Cutaneous tuberculosis in animals was not researched, because the search was blocked.
