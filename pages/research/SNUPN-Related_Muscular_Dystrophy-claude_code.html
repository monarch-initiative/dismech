SNUPN-Related Muscular Dystrophy (LGMDR29): Disease Characteristics Report

1. Disease Information

Overview. SNUPN‑related muscular dystrophy is an autosomal recessive muscular dystrophy caused by biallelic hypomorphic variants in SNUPN. The gene encodes snurportin‑1 (SPN1), the adaptor that imports spliceosomal U‑snRNPs into the nucleus. - Clinical picture. Patients have: - early proximal and axial weakness; - joint contractures and a rigid spine; - restrictive respiratory insufficiency; - raised creatine kinase (CK); - in some patients, features outside muscle: cerebellar atrophy, thin corpus callosum and congenital cataracts. - Biopsy and mechanism. Muscle biopsy often shows myofibrillar‑like pathology. The mechanism is defective snRNP biogenesis with widespread mis‑splicing.

Phenotypic range. The 2026 cohort describes the entity as running "from a congenital or childhood onset pure muscular dystrophy to more complex phenotypes" with neurodevelopmental features, cataracts or spinocerebellar ataxia (Muelas et al., Ann Clin Transl Neurol 2026;13(2):285‑295, PMID 41054283, DOI 10.1002/acn3.70211; abstract paraphrased).

2. Etiology

Cause. The disease is monogenic and autosomal recessive. It is caused by biallelic germline SNUPN variants, mostly hypomorphic. - Nashabat et al. found that "Nine hypomorphic SNUPN biallelic variants, predominantly clustered in the last coding exon, are ascertained to segregate with the disease" (Nat Commun 2024;15:1758, PMID 38413582, DOI 10.1038/s41467-024-45933-5, PMCID PMC10899626). - Iruzubieta et al. concluded that "variants in SNUPN are the cause of a new type of LGMD according to current definition" (Brain 2024;147(8):2867‑2883, PMID 38366623, DOI 10.1093/brain/awae046).

Genetic risk factors: - Homozygosity. Consanguinity was present in 4/18 patients (22%) in the Nashabat cohort. - Founder haplotypes. These were seen for c.926T>G (families F1 and F9), c.902_903delAT (F13/F14) and c.899_900delAC (F6, F7, F12) (PMID 38413582). - Variant class and severity. Neurodegeneration and cataracts were more frequent with protein‑truncating variants than with missense variants. This comes from the Discussion of PMID 38413582, which was available only as a model summary, so treat it as provisional.

Environmental risk factors. None are known.

Protective factors. None are known, genetic or environmental.

Gene–environment interactions. None have been reported.

Alport syndrome was seen in all three sisters of Iruzubieta family 2. It is very likely a coincidental second diagnosis.

Adult‑onset form. Muelas et al. 2026 (PMID 41054283) describe two siblings who were compound heterozygous for p.Arg27Cys and p.Cys174Tyr. - They had adult‑onset proximo‑distal and axial weakness with early respiratory involvement. - Muscle MRI showed involvement of the paravertebral, triceps, sartorius and gracilis muscles. - Some features resembled inclusion body myositis.

Quality of life. No EQ‑5D, SF‑36 or disease‑specific instrument has been reported. The main burdens are inferred from the clinical data: - loss of walking in about 60%; - dependence on night‑time ventilation; - contractures and spinal deformity; - vision problems from cataracts.

4. Genetic / Molecular Information

Gene. - SNUPN (snurportin 1), 15q24.2, nine exons. - The protein, SPN1 (O95149, 360 amino acids), has an N‑terminal importin‑β‑binding (IBB) domain, an m3G‑cap (TMG)‑binding domain and a C‑terminal region needed for self‑association (PMID 38413582). - The HGNC ID and NCBI Gene ID were not retrieved.

Caveats on the table: - The transcript is NM_001042581 per the Brain paper; it was printed as "NM_0011042581", so verify. - "Eight out of nine variants cluster in the last coding exon," a 198‑nucleotide stretch. Because the variants cluster in this last exon, truncating changes would likely escape nonsense‑mediated decay and produce C‑terminally truncated protein. That is my inference, consistent with the authors calling the variants hypomorphic. - In the Nashabat Results text, the list of compound‑heterozygous pairs is garbled, so check the supplement.

Classification. - At discovery, c.926T>G was a VUS on exome sequencing. Its predictors were CADD 28.9 and AlphaMissense 0.9, and it was predicted damaging (PMID 38366623). - ClinVar and ClinGen classifications were not retrieved.

Allele frequencies: - c.926T>G: gnomAD v4 0.000005472 (PMID 38366623). - c.902_903delAT: homozygous in 22 individuals in EVS, MAF ≤10⁻⁴, "clinical significance was unknown". This is an odd observation that should be checked. - c.760‑1G>A, c.958delA, c.899_900delAC and c.787C>T: absent from gnomAD, BRAVO/TOPMed and EVS (PMID 38413582).

Functional consequence. Partial loss of function (hypomorphic): - Truncating C‑terminal variants and Ile309Ser impair SPN1 oligomerization (truncations completely, Ile309Ser partly). - The variants reduce importin‑β binding and impair folding (PMIDs 41054283, 41705730). - Germline origin only; no somatic role in this disease.

Modifier genes, epigenetics, chromosomal abnormalities. None reported.

6. Mechanism / Pathophysiology

Causal chain

  1. Biallelic hypomorphic SNUPN variants, mostly in exon 9 (the C‑terminus), lead to an SPN1 protein that misfolds, cannot oligomerize and binds importin‑β less well. Truncating variants abolish co‑immunoprecipitation with wild‑type SPN1, and Ile309Ser reduces it (PMID 38413582). Reduced importin‑β binding and impaired folding were also shown (PMID 41054283; PMID 41705730: "Mutated snurportin-1 displayed impaired nuclear transport and reduced binding to importin beta").
  2. Defective SPN1 then fails to form or move the m3G‑capped U‑snRNP–SPN1–importin‑β import complex. Mutant SPN1 builds up around the nucleus in patient fibroblasts (PMID 38413582).
  3. As a result, Sm‑core snRNPs are held in the cytoplasm and nuclear snRNP levels fall. This was demonstrated in patient fibroblasts and muscle (SmB/B′ aggregates) and in CRISPR HeLa lines. Iruzubieta et al. report "cytoplasmic accumulation of snRNP components" with unchanged total levels (PMID 38366623).
  4. Next, nuclear spliceosome maturation is disrupted. COILIN moves into nucleoli, Cajal bodies are abnormal ("breakdown of Cajal bodies," abstract of PMID 38413582), and spliceosome‑component transcripts are themselves mis‑spliced (DDX39B, SF3A3, U2AF2, DDX5; PMID 38366623).
  5. This leads to widespread mis‑splicing:
  6. in mutant cells, 519 skipped exons and 88 retained introns;
  7. in patient muscle, 714 events in 555 genes.

It is not yet shown whether all of these events cause disease, and the authors note that some may be downstream of degeneration (PMID 38366623). 6. The pathway then branches: - 6a. Muscle sarcolemma and extracellular matrix (ECM). Mis‑splicing and reduced levels of SGCA (exon 3 skipping), LAMA5, COL4A5/COL IV, COL6A1/2, COL12A1 and POSTN lead to reduced and abnormally cleaved β‑dystroglycan (DAG1) and destabilize the dystrophin‑glycoprotein complex. ITGB2 and SFRP2 go up (PMID 38413582). - 6b. Muscle calcium handling and cytoskeleton. Mis‑splicing of PPP3CC, ANK2 and ATP2A1 (a shift to the neonatal SERCA1b isoform) is a probable contributor, which is inferred (PMID 38366623). - 6c. Cerebellum. In Purkinje cells, global mis‑splicing lowers expression of neuronal development and synaptic genes, leaves the cytoskeleton immature and reduces sonic hedgehog secretion. That in turn disturbs granule‑cell migration and interneuron development, causing cerebellar atrophy and ataxia (knock‑in mouse, PMID 41705730). - 6d. Lens. Cataracts are reported, but no mechanism has been studied. 7. Next, cytoskeletal disorganization and protein aggregation follow: - F‑actin and β‑tubulin are disorganized in mutant fibroblasts, and vimentin and vinculin increase; - desmin, αB‑crystallin and myotilin aggregate in patient muscle; - the Z‑disc streams, and p62/LC3 accumulate, implicating autophagy (PMIDs 38413582, 38366623).

The authors' model is "(1) aberrant splicing, (2) disassembly of DGC (SGCA & DAG) complexes, and finally (3) cytoskeleton aggregation" (PMID 38413582). 8. This results in fibre degeneration and necrosis, endomysial fibrosis and fatty replacement. Clinically that appears as progressive proximal and axial weakness, contractures, restrictive respiratory failure and raised CK.

Pathways and processes, with suggested GO terms: - snRNA import into nucleus (GO:0061015) - spliceosomal snRNP assembly (GO:0000387) - mRNA splicing via spliceosome (GO:0000398) - protein import into nucleus (GO:0006606) - extracellular matrix organization (GO:0030198) - cytoskeleton organization (GO:0007010) - autophagy (GO:0006914) - cerebellar Purkinje cell layer development (GO:0021680)

Molecular profiling: - Muscle RNA‑seq (2 patients): 511 genes up and 125 down. SPP1 was among the most increased, FOS and EGR1 among the most decreased, and STMN2 appeared only in patients (PMID 38366623). - Fibroblast cytosolic proteomics: 102 proteins up and 28 down, enriched for mitochondrial, snRNP and spliceosome terms (PMID 38366623). - Single‑cell RNA‑seq of mouse cerebellum (PMID 41705730). - Not reported: metabolomics, lipidomics, epigenomics, immune studies or CRISPR screens. No inflammation was seen (STIR MRI was negative).

7. Anatomical Structures Affected

Primary: skeletal muscle (UBERON:0001134). - Thigh (Iru MRI): gluteus medius, involved in all; vasti; adductor magnus; sartorius; gracilis. The posterior thigh is relatively spared. - Paravertebral muscles. - Lower leg: peroneus longus, medial gastrocnemius. - Arm (adult form): triceps.

Secondary: - respiratory muscles and diaphragm (UBERON:0001103), giving restrictive respiratory insufficiency; - cerebellum (UBERON:0002037); - corpus callosum (UBERON:0002336); - lens (UBERON:0000965); - joints and spine (contractures, rigid spine); - possibly heart (RBBB in 1 patient).

Body systems: musculoskeletal, respiratory, nervous and visual.

Tissue, cell and subcellular level: see Section 6.

Lateralization: bilateral and symmetric. The cataracts were bilateral.

8. Temporal Development

Onset: - usually congenital to childhood, from birth to 10 years; - 67% before walking or before age 2 (PMID 38413582); - Iruzubieta cohort: 1–9 years; - a rarer adult‑onset form exists (PMID 41054283); - the onset pattern is insidious and chronic.

Progression. The course is slowly to moderately progressive and lifelong: - ambulation is often lost in childhood or adolescence; - respiratory insufficiency can be early; - two children died of respiratory failure before age 15.

No formal staging, remission or natural‑history study exists.

Critical period. The early respiratory decline is a window in which monitoring matters. This is inferred from the clinical data.

9. Inheritance and Population

Inheritance and penetrance: - autosomal recessive; - penetrance appears complete in biallelic carriers; - heterozygous parents were unaffected; - expressivity is variable, from pure LGMD to multisystem disease, and from congenital to adult onset; - no anticipation or germline mosaicism has been reported.

Patient count. About 27 published patients: - 18 from 15 families (PMID 38413582); - 5 from 2 families, Romanian and Macedonian (PMID 38366623); - 2 Spanish siblings (PMID 41054283); - 2 families in Japan (PMID 41705730).

There is probably some overlap, because the Iruzubieta and Nashabat cohorts share c.926T>G.

Geography and populations: - The Nashabat cohort spans three continents. Author institutions are in Turkey, Iran, Europe, the USA and Colombia, but the individual patients' origins were mostly not available to me. - Founder haplotypes exist for c.926T>G (Balkan: Kosovo, Romania, North Macedonia) and for two exon‑9 frameshifts.

Sex ratio: 10 male to 8 female.

Epidemiology: prevalence, incidence and carrier frequency are unknown.

10. Diagnostics

Laboratory: - CK is raised in most patients, usually 1,500–8,000 U/L, but can be only mildly raised (about 300 U/L). - EMG is myopathic. - Nerve conduction is usually normal; one patient had reduced CMAP amplitude.

Muscle MRI: - gluteus medius; - paravertebral muscles; - anterior and medial thigh (vasti, adductor magnus, sartorius, gracilis), with the posterior thigh relatively spared; - peroneus and medial gastrocnemius; - no oedema on STIR.

Brain MRI: cerebellar atrophy and/or thin corpus callosum.

Ophthalmology: slit‑lamp examination for cataracts.

Respiratory: pulmonary function tests show a restrictive pattern.

Muscle biopsy (PMIDs 38366623, 38413582, 41054283): - dystrophic changes with endomysial fibrosis; - type I fibre predominance; - internal nuclei and necrotic fibres; - minicore‑like and whorled fibres; - occasional rimmed vacuoles; - myotilin, desmin, αB‑crystallin and p62/LC3 aggregates, with Z‑disc streaming on electron microscopy, i.e. myofibrillar‑like features ("Muscle biopsy showed myofibrillar-like features consisting of myotilin deposits and Z-disc disorganization," PMID 38366623); - reduced α‑sarcoglycan and dystroglycan; - cytoplasmic SmB/B′ aggregates, a possible tissue marker; - in the adult form, granule and vesicle proliferation with remodelling of the nuclear envelope and sarcolemma.

Genetic testing: - Exome or genome sequencing, or an LGMD/myopathy panel that includes SNUPN. The authors advise that SNUPN be included in genetic testing for myopathies (PMID 38366623). - Muelas et al. advise testing in adults with late‑onset proximo‑distal and axial weakness with early respiratory impairment, especially when it mimics inclusion body myositis (PMID 41054283). - RNA studies can confirm splice variants (for example, intron‑8 retention with c.760‑1G>A). - Microarray, karyotype, FISH, mtDNA and repeat‑expansion tests have no direct role. They are useful only to exclude other diagnoses.

Differential diagnosis (by clinical reasoning; no cited source compares these): - Rigid‑spine and contracture myopathies: SEPN1, COL6, LMNA, EMD/FHL1. - Myofibrillar myopathies: DES, MYOT, CRYAB, FLNC, BAG3. - Sarcoglycanopathies and dystroglycanopathies. - Inclusion body myositis, for the adult form. - Marinesco–Sjögren syndrome (SIL1): congenital cataracts, cerebellar atrophy and myopathy. - Spinocerebellar ataxias, for the cerebellar form.

Screening: no newborn screening exists. Cascade carrier testing is possible once the familial variants are known.

11. Outcome / Prognosis

Mortality: - 2/18 children (11%) died of respiratory failure before 15 years (PMID 38413582); - the oldest reported patients are in their 20s to adulthood (Iru: 29 y); - the adult‑onset family is described as well; - survival curves and life expectancy are unavailable.

Morbidity: - about 60% lose ambulation; - about 60% develop respiratory insufficiency needing support; - contractures and scoliosis are common; - cataracts need surgery.

Prognostic factors: - Truncating variants may be linked to more neurological and lens involvement (provisional, from the summarized Discussion of PMID 38413582). - Early onset is linked to greater severity, which is inferred. - No prognostic biomarkers are known, and no QoL data exist.

12. Treatment

No disease‑specific therapy exists. No drugs, gene therapy, ASO or cell therapy have been tested. No ClinicalTrials.gov entries were identified, though a dedicated search was not run.

Nashabat et al. speculate that "targeting SPN1 or other snRNP partners may hold promise" (from a summarized section of PMID 38413582).

Supportive care. This follows general LGMD and congenital myopathy practice; nothing SNUPN‑specific is published. Suggested NCIT terms are not verified. - Respiratory: regular PFTs and nocturnal non‑invasive ventilation (NIV), which three Iruzubieta patients use. NCIT: Non‑Invasive Ventilation. - Physical therapy, stretching and orthoses for contractures. NCIT: Physical Therapy. - Scoliosis surveillance and surgery where needed. - Mobility aids. - Cataract surgery, done in all six affected children (NCIT: Cataract Extraction). - ECG and echocardiography given the single RBBB case. - Genetic counselling.

Pharmacogenomics: not applicable.

13. Prevention

  • Primary prevention: none.
  • Genetic counselling: 25% recurrence risk for sibs of an affected child.
  • Reproductive options: carrier testing of relatives, prenatal diagnosis or preimplantation genetic testing once the familial variants are known.
  • Founder variants: in founder populations (Balkans; families with exon‑9 frameshifts), targeted carrier testing may be informative.
  • Tertiary prevention: respiratory surveillance with timely NIV, contracture prevention, scoliosis monitoring and early detection of cataracts.
  • Vaccines, prophylaxis, environmental measures: not applicable.

14. Other Species / Natural Disease

  • No naturally occurring SNUPN disease in animals has been reported, and OMIA was not searched.
  • Orthologues have been studied: fly Snup (Natalizio et al., Mol Biol Cell 2013, PMID 23885126) and mouse Snupn (PMID 41705730).
  • SPN1's role in snRNP import is evolutionarily conserved; Drosophila Snurportin was characterized in PMID 23885126.
  • No zoonotic potential.
  • Ortholog NCBI Gene IDs were not retrieved.
