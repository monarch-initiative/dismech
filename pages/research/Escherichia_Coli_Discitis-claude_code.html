Escherichia coli discitis (MONDO:8000002): disease characteristics report

How I interpreted the request. MONDO:8000002 is an infectious disease, so it has no causal human gene. The cause is the bacterium Escherichia coli. Specific E. coli literature is scarce: it is mostly case reports, one small literature review, and Gram-negative subgroups inside larger pyogenic spondylodiscitis cohorts. Where only that general evidence exists, I use it and say so.

How I retrieved sources. Shell access to PubMed's E-utilities was blocked in this session, so all sources came from web search. Many items are cited by PMC ID or DOI because I could not retrieve their PMIDs, and I did not invent any.

Ontology identifiers. Only the MONDO and GARD identifiers below were retrieved. HPO, GO, CL, UBERON, CHEBI and NCIT terms are given as labels only. Their IDs need to be looked up and checked before curation.

1. Disease information

Overview. MONDO defines E. coli discitis as "discitis caused by infection with Escherichia coli" (SAIL concept library, MONDO:8000002). - It is an infection of the intervertebral disc space, which in practice almost always spreads to the neighbouring vertebral endplates. That combined form is called spondylodiscitis or vertebral osteomyelitis. - A 2019 review calls it "a relatively rare disease that usually has a urinary tract infection as its source," with symptoms "not distinguishable from vertebral osteomyelitis caused by other agents" (Salehi et al., Jundishapur J Microbiol 2019;e99694).

Nature of the evidence. All evidence comes from individual patients: case reports and retrospective hospital cohorts. There is no aggregated registry specific to this entity.

2. Etiology

Causal factor. Infection with E. coli, almost always reaching the spine through the bloodstream (hematogenous spread). - Pediatric strains have been characterised as extraintestinal pathogenic E. coli (ExPEC). One example is an O1:K1, phylogroup B2 strain carrying "virulence factors typical of highly virulent ExPEC strains" (Gaschignard et al., BMC Infect Dis 2017, DOI 10.1186/s12879-017-2486-6; PMC5452335).

Risk factors (all clinical, human evidence)

  • Genitourinary or intra-abdominal infection is the main risk factor.
  • A four-hospital Korean cohort found Gram-negative organisms in 18.0% of pyogenic spondylitis cases. E. coli was the most common of them (35 cases, 10.2% of all cases).
  • That cohort reported prior or concurrent genitourinary infection in 32.3% of Gram-negative cases versus 2.1% of Gram-positive cases.
  • The authors concluded: "GNB should be considered as the etiologic organism when infectious spondylitis develops in a patient with preexisting or synchronous genitourinary tract and intra-abdominal infection" (Kang et al., PLoS ONE 2015; PMC4433234).
  • Prior symptomatic urinary tract infection (UTI). In a comparison of 17 Gram-negative and 37 Gram-positive hematogenous cases, a previous symptomatic UTI was found in "41% vs. 3%, respectively; p = 0.03" (Lee et al., BMC Infect Dis 2016;16:735, DOI 10.1186/s12879-016-2071-4; PMC5139091).
  • Older age and a history of cancer were more common in Gram-negative cases in the same study (PMC5139091).
  • Age and sex. Incidence of pyogenic spondylodiscitis is highest in elderly people (Kehrer et al., J Infect 2014; PMID 24296494). E. coli cases are reported as more common in adults and in males. The 10-patient review had 6 males and 4 females, with a mean age of 52 and a range of 12–69 years (Salehi 2019).
  • Comorbidities seen in pyogenic vertebral osteomyelitis generally include diabetes, renal disease and heart failure (see §11).
  • Host immunosuppression. Authors recommend looking for it when E. coli is found, even though the pediatric index case had a normal immunological work-up (PMC5452335).

Genetic risk or protective factors. I found no human susceptibility loci, GWAS hits or protective variants.

Environmental protective factors. None specific are documented. Preventing and treating UTIs promptly is the logical protective measure, but this is inferred, not tested.

Gene–environment interactions. None documented.

4. Genetic / molecular information

Host side. Not applicable. There are no causal genes, pathogenic variants (ClinVar), modifier genes, epigenetic findings or chromosomal abnormalities for this infectious entity.

Pathogen side (E. coli)

  • Phylogroups. B2 predominates in bone and joint E. coli isolates:
  • 53.9% of prosthetic-joint-infection isolates were B2, with ST131 and ST69 the most common sequence types (PMC12795266).
  • In diabetic-foot osteomyelitis, "the majority of the strains belonged to the virulent B2 and D phylogenetic groups (82%)" (PMC7918245).
  • Virulence genes. Genes reported in bone-infection isolates include fimH (type 1 fimbrial adhesin), papC/papG (P fimbriae) and iucC (aerobactin siderophore). Some isolates were linked to lysis of osteoblasts in vitro (PMC4626605).
  • No virulence signature specific to bone infection has been established. One study found no molecular signature separating these isolates from uropathogenic strains.
  • Resistance genes. Cases with ESBL-producing strains (for example CTX-M-9) are reported (Kohlmann et al., BMC Infect Dis 2015; PMC4676899).

5. Environmental information

  • Infectious agent: Escherichia coli, usually from a urinary, and less often intra-abdominal, source. The NCBI Taxonomy ID was not retrieved in this session.
  • Hospital vs community: cases occur in both. A community-acquired case is reported in Bull Emerg Trauma 2016.
  • Lifestyle and toxin factors: none specific are documented.

6. Mechanism / pathophysiology

Causal chain. Steps marked inferred come from general osteomyelitis biology rather than E. coli-specific studies.

  1. A urinary tract (or intra-abdominal) infection with an ExPEC/uropathogenic E. coli strain leads to bacteria entering the bloodstream (bacteremia). This is documented in case series: positive blood cultures in 7 of 10 patients, urinary source in at least 50%.
  2. Bacteremia results in bacteria seeding the vertebral endplate. There are two proposed routes:
  3. Arterial route: bacteria lodge in endplate end-arterioles. This is inferred from general reviews.
  4. Venous route: retrograde flow through the valveless Batson vertebral venous plexus, which joins the pelvic veins to the internal vertebral venous plexus, especially when intra-abdominal pressure rises. This route is speculative and contested: one 2025 case report calls the mechanism "speculative," and an early experimental study concluded "our studies do not support the supposition that infection is likely to spread by this route" (Batson plexus summary; JBJS 41B:796).
  5. Bacterial adhesins and iron-scavenging factors (such as fimbriae and aerobactin) lead to persistence in bone. Osteoblast lysis has been shown in vitro (PMC4626605). Whether this is the key step in humans is inferred.
  6. Innate immune recognition of lipopolysaccharide (LPS) results in neutrophil and macrophage influx and suppurative inflammation. The cytokine details are inferred (I retrieved no disease-specific study).
  7. Inflammation and enzymatic and ischemic damage lead to destruction of the endplate. The infection then spreads into the avascular disc, which is discitis proper.
  8. From there the process branches:
  9. 6a. Local pain and loss of spinal stability lead to back pain and immobility.
  10. 6b. Spread into the soft tissues leads to paravertebral, psoas or epidural abscess. Epidural abscess is less common with Gram-negative organisms (PMC5139091). It can compress the cord or nerve roots and cause neurological deficit.
  11. 6c. Systemic signs: persistent or recurrent bacteremia, fever and weight loss.

Suggested terms (labels only) - GO biological processes: neutrophil chemotaxis; inflammatory response; response to lipopolysaccharide; cell adhesion involved in biofilm formation; siderophore biosynthetic process (pathogen); bone resorption. - CL cell types: neutrophil; macrophage; osteoblast; osteoclast; chondrocyte (nucleus pulposus and annulus fibrosus cells).

Not available: transcriptomic, proteomic, metabolomic, single-cell or spatial studies specific to E. coli discitis.

7. Anatomical structures affected

  • Primary site: intervertebral disc and adjacent vertebral bodies and endplates. Suggested UBERON labels: intervertebral disk; vertebral body; lumbar vertebra; thoracic vertebra.
  • Spinal level: lumbar disease is typical (L2–L3 in Salehi 2019). Thoracolumbar cases also occur (T11–T12 in PMC5452335; T11–L1 in Pan Afr Med J 2025). Cervical disease is reported with meningitis and retropharyngeal abscess (PMC4676899).
  • Secondary sites: epidural space; paravertebral and psoas muscles; meninges (rare); the urinary tract as the primary focus.
  • Tissues: bone, fibrocartilage and bone marrow.
  • Laterality: midline, so not lateralised. Usually one level is involved; several levels raise mortality (§11).

8. Temporal development

  • Onset: mainly adults and the elderly, although pediatric cases exist (12-year-old, PMC5452335). Onset is subacute or insidious, with diagnostic delays of weeks to months.
  • Course without treatment: progressive destruction and abscess formation.
  • Remission: induced by treatment. Cure rates are about 90% at one year with 6 weeks of antibiotics (in pyogenic vertebral osteomyelitis overall, §12).
  • Critical window: early diagnosis before neurological compromise or abscess formation.
  • Relapse: recurrent bacteremia can occur if the source is not controlled (PMC12707976).

9. Inheritance and population

Inheritance. Not applicable (acquired infection). Penetrance, anticipation, founder effects and carrier frequency do not apply.

Epidemiology - In Funen, Denmark (adults), incidence of all pyogenic spondylodiscitis rose from 2.2 to 5.8 per 100,000 person-years between 1995 and 2008. It was highest in older patients. The authors note it is unknown whether the rise "is real or is a result of improved diagnostic methods" (Kehrer 2014, PMID 24296494). - Gram-negative organisms cause 15–39% of cases in various series. E. coli accounted for about 10% of pyogenic spondylitis in the Korean cohort (PMC4433234). - Urogenic spondylodiscitis is about 6.2% of pyogenic discitis cases (quoted in case-report reviews). - I retrieved no incidence figure specific to E. coli.

Demographics - Sex: male predominance in the small review (6 males, 4 females). - Age: incidence rises with age. - Gram-negative infection is suspected more often in women with a urinary source (reviews). - No geographic or ethnic clustering is reported.

10. Diagnostics

Microbiology - Blood cultures are the key test; 7 of 10 were positive (Salehi 2019). - Urine culture looks for the source. - Image-guided or open biopsy when blood cultures are negative. In an Australian cohort, open biopsy yielded 70%, fine-needle aspirate 41% and core biopsy 30% (PMC5846292). - Antibiotic susceptibility testing, including for ESBL production.

Laboratory and imaging - ESR and CRP are useful; white cell count is less so. - MRI with gadolinium "is considered the best choice for image diagnosis of spondylodiscitis because of its high sensitivity and specificity" (PMC3282859).

Guidelines - The IDSA 2015 native vertebral osteomyelitis guideline says MRI is often needed, plain radiographs are insensitive early on, and empirical antibiotics should be withheld until a microbiological diagnosis unless the patient is septic or neurologically compromised (Berbari et al., Clin Infect Dis 2015;61:e26–46, PMID 26229122; executive summary PMID 26316526). - An EANM/ESNR/ESCMID consensus on diagnosing spine infection was published in 2019 (DOI 10.1007/s00259-019-04393-6).

Genetic testing. Not applicable for the host. Pathogen whole-genome sequencing and serotyping (for example O1:K1, phylogroup B2) are research tools.

Differential diagnosis - S. aureus and other pyogenic spondylodiscitis. Gram-negative cases present more with constitutional symptoms and have fewer epidural abscesses (PMC5139091). - Tuberculous (Pott) and Brucella spondylitis. - Erosive degenerative (Modic) changes. - Spinal metastasis.

Screening. No asymptomatic screening exists.

11. Outcome / prognosis

Comparison with Gram-positive disease - Outcomes are similar. "The mortality rate and clinical outcomes are not significantly different between pyogenic spondylitis caused by GNB and GPC" (PMC4433234). - Outcomes with combined surgery and antibiotics were "not different" (PMC5139091).

Prognostic factors in pyogenic vertebral osteomyelitis generally - Adverse outcome occurred in 28% of one cohort. Independent predictors were S. aureus infection, motor weakness and a sinus tract (PMC6680037). - One-year mortality rose with age (OR 1.07) and with the number of vertebral levels involved (OR 2.30). - In-hospital death was linked to renal disease (OR 1.85) and congestive heart failure (OR 1.52). - In 9,753 patients, diabetes with complications (OR 1.86) was associated with in-hospital death (PMC10381366).

Mortality and cure - Death was the most common adverse event in the Bernard trial: 8% in the 6-week arm and 7% in the 12-week arm (PMID 25468170). - The E. coli case reports mostly resolved with medical therapy alone.

Complications. Epidural abscess, neurological deficit, spinal instability or deformity, recurrent bacteremia, and meningitis (rare).

Quality of life. No E. coli-specific data were retrieved.

12. Treatment

Antibiotics (NCIT labels suggested: Antibiotic Therapy; Ceftriaxone; Ciprofloxacin; Meropenem) - Third-generation cephalosporins such as ceftriaxone. French guidance favours a third-generation cephalosporin combined with a fluoroquinolone for E. coli spondylodiscitis (PMC5452335). - Fluoroquinolones. Oral ciprofloxacin for a susceptible strain achieved cure after 8 weeks without surgery (Salehi 2019). - Carbapenems for ESBL strains. In a CTX-M-9 ESBL case, meningitis and osteomyelitis resolved "after surgery and meropenem therapy" (PMC4676899).

Duration - Six weeks was non-inferior to 12 weeks in pyogenic vertebral osteomyelitis: risk difference 0.05% (95% CI −6.2 to 6.3); about 90.9% vs 90.8% cured at one year (Bernard et al., Lancet 2015;385:875–82, PMID 25468170; NCT00764114). - Longer courses may be needed for drug-resistant organisms, extensive bone destruction or abscesses.

Route - Oral therapy was non-inferior to intravenous therapy in the first 6 weeks of bone and joint infection. Treatment failure was 13.2% oral vs 14.6% IV, and catheter complications were 1.0% vs 9.4% (OVIVA trial, NEJM 2019;380:425–36, PMID 30699315). - Highly bioavailable fluoroquinolones make an early switch to oral therapy practical for susceptible E. coli.

Surgery (NCIT labels: Debridement; Spinal Fusion; Decompression) - Indicated for neurological compromise, abscess, instability or failure of medical therapy. The 2025 EANS consensus describes surgery "to debride the infection, decompress neural structures, and stabilize the affected spinal segments."

Source control and supportive care - Treat the urinary or abdominal source. - Pain control, orthoses and mobilisation or rehabilitation.

Pharmacogenomics, gene therapy, cell therapy and other advanced therapeutics. Not applicable. No specific experimental trials were found.

13. Prevention

  • Primary: prevent and treat UTIs and intra-abdominal infections promptly. No E. coli vaccine is licensed.
  • Secondary: consider spondylodiscitis in patients with E. coli bacteremia plus back pain, or with persistent bacteremia or fever. Get MRI early.
  • Tertiary: an adequate antibiotic course, source control, and monitoring for relapse.
  • Not applicable: genetic counselling and screening.

14. Other species

  • Dogs. Canine discospondylitis is common in veterinary practice. Staphylococci dominate (23 of 38 positive blood cultures in 386 dogs; Cridge et al., JVIM 2023, PMID 37288966). Culture was positive in 42% of 120 dogs and was associated with higher bodyweight (Liverpool repository). I found no dog-specific E. coli counts.
  • Cats. E. coli was isolated (from urine) in a feline discospondylitis series (PMC11209358).
  • Zoonotic potential: none specific to this disease.
  • Orthologous genes and breeds: not applicable.

Evidence gaps

  • There are no prospective or E. coli-specific cohorts. Most specific data come from 10 or fewer case reports.
  • There is no host-genetic data.
  • The role of Batson's plexus in the mechanism is unresolved.
  • No incidence figure specific to E. coli was retrieved.
  • No E. coli animal model was found.
  • PMIDs for several sources (Kang 2015, Lee 2016, Gaschignard 2017, Salehi 2019, Zimmerli NEJM 2010, DOI 10.1056/NEJMcp0910753) were not retrieved, so they are cited by PMC ID or DOI.
