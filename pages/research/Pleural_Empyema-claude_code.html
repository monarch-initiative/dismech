Pleural Empyema (MONDO:0018667): Disease Characteristics Report

How I read the request: Pleural empyema is an acquired infectious disease, not a Mendelian one. The template lists no causal gene, and I found no evidence of one. No causal gene applies. Sections 4 and 9 that ask about genetics are marked not applicable. Where a PubMed ID (PMID) came up in my searches I cite it; otherwise I cite the URL or DOI. PubMed's E-utilities could not be queried directly in this session, so PMIDs are given only where a search surfaced them. All quotes are taken from search-result excerpts, not from full texts I read.

1. Disease Information

Overview. Pleural empyema is pus in the pleural space. Disease Ontology describes it as an "accumulation of pus in the pleural cavity", usually from pneumonia or a lung abscess spreading into that space, with cough, fever, chest pain, sweating and shortness of breath (DOID:3798). MeSH defines it as "suppurative inflammation of the pleural space" (MeSH D016724). It is the far end of the spectrum of pleural infection, which runs from simple parapneumonic effusion through complicated effusion to frank pus.

2. Etiology

  • Cause: bacterial invasion of the pleural space. The main routes are:
  • spread from pneumonia (parapneumonic, the most common route);
  • iatrogenic or post-surgical infection;
  • trauma;
  • oesophageal rupture;
  • spread of infection from below the diaphragm, or through the blood.
  • Risk factors in adults: "immunosuppression, diabetes, poor oral hygiene, gastro-oesophageal reflux, alcohol excess and intravenous drug use are known to independently predict increased risk of progression of pneumonia to pleural infection" (PMC4953430; PMC4466426).
  • Anaerobic infection usually follows aspiration or poor oral hygiene.
  • Some patients have no identifiable risk factor.
  • Comorbidity is common: a systematic review found a median comorbidity prevalence of 72% (Hassan et al. 2021, IJGM).
  • Age: incidence is skewed toward older adults (Cargill et al. 2019, ERJ Open, PMC6860993). There is a second peak in young children.
  • Genetic risk factors: none established. I retrieved no GWAS, ClinVar or ClinGen associations. Primary immunodeficiencies predispose to pyogenic infection in general, but I retrieved no empyema-specific data on them.
  • Protective factors:
  • Pneumococcal conjugate vaccination lowered empyema hospitalisations in US children (PMID 27832918).
  • Prompt antibiotic treatment of pneumonia is protective (inferred).
  • Gene–environment interactions: none described in the sources.

Onset and course: onset is acute or subacute. Anaerobic infections often run a more indolent course. Severity ranges from moderate to severe.

Quality of life: in MIST-3, the EQ-5D-5L utility index rose from 0.35 at baseline to 0.83 at 2 months in the intrapleural-enzyme arm. That is a severe acute quality-of-life burden that largely recovers (MIST-3, PMC10765402).

4. Genetic/Molecular Information

Not applicable. No causal genes, pathogenic variants, modifier genes, epigenetic signatures or chromosomal abnormalities have been described for human empyema in the sources I retrieved.

The host genes that matter here have been studied only in mouse models (Section 15): - SERPINE1 / PAI-1 (HGNC:8583). PAI-1-deficient mice "died within 4 days, had increased pleural inflammation" (Tucker et al. 2016, PMC4896893). - PLAUR / uPAR (HGNC:9053). uPAR-knockout mice "were protected from S. pneumoniae-mediated decrements in lung function" (Logan et al. 2021, Sci Rep).

5. Environmental Information

Lifestyle factors: alcohol excess, intravenous drug use, poor dental hygiene and aspiration risk (Section 2). I retrieved no occupational or toxic exposures.

Infectious agents. 16S rRNA next-generation sequencing of 243 pleural fluid samples from the PILOT cohort (the TORPIDS study) found the following (Kanellakis et al., Lancet Microbe 2022, PMC8967721): - "Pleural infection was predominately polymicrobial (192 [79%] of 243 samples), with mixed anaerobes and other Gram-negative bacteria dominating community-acquired polymicrobial infection." - Streptococcus pneumoniae (NCBITaxon:1313) dominated monomicrobial infection. - Survival was better when anaerobes were present (HR 0.46) and when the Streptococcus anginosus group (NCBITaxon:1328, the milleri group) was present (HR 0.43). - Survival was worse when Staphylococcus aureus (NCBITaxon:1280) or Enterobacteriaceae dominated.

Other studies agree: - Norwegian and Mayo Clinic series found that S. intermedius (NCBITaxon:1338) and Fusobacterium nucleatum (NCBITaxon:851), both oral bacteria, dominate community-acquired disease (PMC10474564; PMID 16840746). - A combined culture and sequencing study of 434 infections found about 50% of community-acquired cases were streptococcal and 20% included anaerobes.

Paediatric pathogens: after PCV13, S. pneumoniae serotype 3 became the leading pneumococcal cause in Germany (PMID 31735502), Portugal (EID 2018) and Australia (Thorax).

6. Mechanism / Pathophysiology

Causal chain (from reviews: PMC8286963; NBK534297; PMC4466426; Tucker 2016):

  1. Bacterial pneumonia, or another source (surgery, trauma, oesophageal rupture, spread through the blood), leads to inflammation of the visceral pleura next to the infection.
  2. Inflammation of the visceral pleura results in "increased permeability of the cells lining the pleural membrane", with "proinflammatory mediators, such as TNFα, IL6, and IL8" driving exudate formation. This is the exudative stage: sterile, clear, neutrophil-rich fluid.
  3. If the infection is not controlled, bacteria invade the pleural space, which "further enhances the inflammatory response and leads to the activation of the coagulation cascade, which in turn promotes fibrin deposition".
  4. Branch 3a: suppressed fibrinolysis. High pleural PAI-1 inhibits tPA and uPA, so fibrin persists. High pleural PAI-1 "is associated with loculation risk" (Komissarov, PMC5142452).
  5. Branch 3b: neutrophil recruitment and bacterial metabolism lead to glucose consumption, lactic acid and CO₂ production, and so to low pH and glucose and high LDH. This is the fibrinopurulent stage.
  6. Fibrin septation results in loculation. Loculation hampers drainage, and neutrophil-extracellular DNA together with cellular debris makes the pus more viscous. Breaking down this DNA is the inferred rationale for using DNase.
  7. If the infection persists, fibroblasts turn the fibrin membranes into "a thick nonelastic pleural peel" (TGF-β is implicated). This is the organising stage.
  8. The pleural peel leads to a trapped lung, restrictive lung function and fibrothorax. Alternatively, systemic spread leads to sepsis and death.

7. Anatomical Structures Affected

  • Primary site: pleural cavity (UBERON:0002402), visceral pleura (UBERON:0002401) and parietal pleura (UBERON:0002400).
  • Adjacent and secondary sites:
  • lung (UBERON:0002048);
  • chest wall (empyema necessitans);
  • systemic involvement through sepsis.
  • Body system: respiratory.
  • Tissue level: mesothelium (UBERON:0000042) and serous membrane connective tissue.
  • Subcellular level: the extracellular space (GO:0005615) is where fibrin and NET DNA accumulate.
  • Laterality: usually unilateral, on the same side as the pneumonia.

8. Temporal Development

  • Onset: acute to subacute.
  • Stages: the three stages (exudative, then fibrinopurulent, then organising) develop over days to weeks. One source says the exudative stage lasts "roughly 2 to 5 days" from the onset of pneumonia, while others describe progression within hours without treatment (PMC8286963).
  • Course: self-limited with treatment. Becoming chronic means progression to fibrothorax.
  • Critical window: early drainage, before organisation sets in. MIST-3 tested intervention within about 24 hours of standard care failing (PMC10765402).

9. Inheritance and Population

Inheritance: not applicable. The disease is acquired, and there is no penetrance, anticipation, founder effect or carrier frequency to report.

Incidence - United States, 1996–2008: parapneumonic empyema hospitalisations rose from 3.04 to 5.98 per 100,000, a 2.0-fold increase. Over the period there were about 157,094 hospitalisations, 18,200 of them in 2008 alone. Staphylococcal empyema rose 3.3-fold and non-pneumococcal streptococcal empyema 1.9-fold (Grijalva et al., Thorax 2011, PMID 21617169; PMC4820002). - US children under 5: rates rose 2.81-fold, from 3.7 to 10.3 per 100,000, between 1996–98 and 2005–07 (before PCV13). - Western countries in the 2010s: 6.7–9.9 per 100,000, nearly double the rate of the previous decade (Hassan et al. 2021). - Current trend: incidence "is increasing across the Western World", and the reason, particularly among older people, is unclear (Elsheikh et al., Breathe 2024).

Demographics - Mean age at diagnosis is 58–63 years in Europe, North America and Australia. - Men are reported to be affected more often, but I retrieved no exact sex ratio.

10. Diagnostics

Pleural fluid analysis. The BTS 2023 thresholds (PMC10790177 table) are:

Malignancy and other inflammatory causes can also push the pH below 7.2.

Microbiology: send ≥25 mL of fluid, ideally 50 mL, in both plain and blood-culture bottles (BTS 2023). 16S and next-generation sequencing pick up many organisms that culture misses (Section 5).

Imaging - Ultrasound shows septations and loculation. - Contrast CT shows the "split pleura" sign. - BTS advises against PET-CT. It recommends CT follow-up if symptoms persist, to exclude occult malignancy.

Genetic testing: not applicable.

Differential diagnosis: malignant effusion, tuberculous pleuritis, rheumatoid pleurisy and lung abscess.

11. Outcome/Prognosis

  • Mortality (PILOT, n=542): 10% at 3 months and 19% at 12 months (Corcoran et al., ERJ 2020;56:2000130).
  • US in-hospital case fatality: 8.0% in 1996 and 7.2% in 2008 (PMC4820002).
  • RAPID score (Renal function, Age, Purulence, Infection source, Dietary factors):
  • 3-month mortality by risk group was 2.3% for low risk (score 0–2), 9.2% for medium (3–4) and 29.3% for high (5–7).
  • Discrimination (C-statistic) was 0.78 at 3 months.
  • Microbiological prognostic factors: S. aureus or Enterobacteriaceae dominance predicts worse outcome; anaerobes predict better outcome (TORPIDS).
  • Complications: trapped lung or fibrothorax, bronchopleural fistula, empyema necessitans, sepsis, and the need for surgery.

12. Treatment

Antibiotics (NCIT: Antibiotic Therapy). These should cover anaerobes in community-acquired disease. In a rabbit empyema study, penicillin and metronidazole reached equilibrium with infected pleural fluid fastest (Chest 2000, qxmd 10858410).

Chest tube drainage (NCIT: Thoracostomy). BTS favours a small-bore tube, noting that tubes larger than 14F may cause more pain.

Intrapleural therapy - Streptokinase, MIST1 (n=454): death or surgery occurred in 31% with streptokinase versus 27% with placebo (RR 1.14, P=0.43), with more adverse events on streptokinase. The authors concluded that "intrapleural streptokinase does not improve mortality, the rate of surgery, or the length of the hospital stay" (Maskell et al., NEJM 2005;352:865, DOI 10.1056/NEJMoa042473). - tPA plus DNase, MIST2 (n=210, 2×2 factorial): radiographic opacity fell by about 29.5% with the combination versus 17.2% with placebo. The combination also reduced surgical referral and length of stay, while neither agent alone beat placebo (Rahman et al., NEJM 2011;365:518, PMID 21830966). - BTS 2023: recommends against single-agent tPA or DNase (Thorax 78:1143). - Suggested NCIT terms: Alteplase; Dornase Alfa.

Surgery (VATS or thoracotomy decortication) - Cochrane review (8 RCTs, n=391): no difference in mortality, but VATS shortened hospital stay (Redden et al. 2017, CD010651). - MIST-3 feasibility RCT (60 randomised, ISRCTN18192121): - median time to intervention was 1.0 day for enzyme therapy versus 3.5 days for VATS; - length of stay was about 7 days in both intervention arms versus 10 days with standard care; - EQ-5D improved more with enzyme therapy (PMC10765402).

Pharmacogenomics, gene therapy and cell therapy: not applicable.

Treatment sequence: 1. antibiotics; 2. drainage if the fluid is purulent or pH ≤7.2; 3. tPA/DNase if drainage fails; 4. surgery, or as an early alternative to step 3, as being tested in MIST-3.

13. Prevention

  • Primary prevention: pneumococcal conjugate vaccines. In the US, empyema hospitalisations fell after PCV13 (PMID 27832918). Serotype 19A was essentially eliminated in Mexican surveillance (PMC6448099). PCV13 works less well against serotype 3, which has become a growing share of remaining cases (PMID 31735502).
  • Secondary prevention: early pleural sampling in pneumonia with effusion.
  • Tertiary prevention: RAPID-based risk stratification, and timely drainage to prevent organisation.
  • Behavioural measures: dental hygiene and reducing alcohol and injection-drug use. These are inferred from the risk factors; no trials were retrieved.
  • Genetic counselling: not applicable.

14. Other Species / Natural Disease

Cats (Felis catus, NCBITaxon:9685), where the disease is called feline pyothorax: - In an Australian series of 27 cases, 78% had a "mixed anaerobic bacterial infection of oropharyngeal origin" (PMID 16055006). - Common organisms include Pasteurella multocida (NCBITaxon:747), Fusobacterium, Bacteroides and Actinomyces. - Survival to discharge is 46–78% in reported series, and mortality is highest in the first 48 hours (PMC8406089; PMC10229369).

Dogs (NCBITaxon:9615) also get pyothorax.

Comparative biology: the oral-anaerobic, polymicrobial pattern seen in cats resembles human community-acquired disease. Pyothorax is not zoonotic in itself.

Genetics: no OMIA or breed entries were retrieved.
