Congenital Pulmonary Sequestration

1. Disease Information

2026-11-11
Claude Code MONDO:0017843 Model: claude-fable-5, claude-haiku-4-5-20251001 58 citations Prompt: disease_pathophysiology_research.md f452f0a9c7e3

1. Disease Information

Overview. Congenital pulmonary sequestration (synonyms: bronchopulmonary sequestration, BPS; pulmonary sequestration; sequestered lobe) is a rare congenital lung malformation consisting of a mass of non-functioning lung tissue that (a) lacks normal communication with the tracheobronchial tree and (b) receives its arterial blood supply from the systemic circulation rather than the pulmonary artery. Orphanet defines it as "a cystic or solid mass of nonfunctioning primitive segmental lung tissue that does not communicate with the tracheobronchial tree and has anomalous systemic blood supply" (Orphanet ORPHA:3161). The term "sequestration" was coined by Pryce in 1946.

2. Etiology

Primary cause — developmental, not genetic or infectious (for the congenital lesion). The dominant embryologic model is the accessory lung bud theory: a supernumerary lung bud arises from the ventral primitive foregut caudal to the normal lung bud and migrates caudally with the developing esophagus; splanchnic vessels supplying the aberrant bud persist as the systemic feeding artery. Budding before pleural formation produces a sequestration invested within normal lung (ILS); budding after pleural formation produces a separately encased ELS (ILS review, PMC2876708; Annals of Thoracic Surgery collective review). A complementary and increasingly favored model is the bronchial atresia/obstruction sequence: Riedlinger and colleagues found an atretic bronchus in 100% of extralobar and 82% of intralobar sequestrations examined ("Bronchial atresia is common to extralobar sequestration, intralobar sequestration, congenital cystic adenomatoid malformation, and lobar emphysema," Pediatr Dev Pathol 2006;9:361–373 — PMID to be confirmed on fetch), placing BPS on a shared developmental spectrum with CPAM and congenital lobar emphysema in which the level, degree, and timing of fetal airway obstruction determine the resulting malformation (Bronchial atresia: the hidden pathology, J Pediatr Surg 2006).

For adult-diagnosed ILS specifically, an acquired hypothesis persists: recurrent necrotizing pneumonia with lower-lobe bronchial obstruction and hypertrophy of pre-existing systemic collateral arteries could produce an ILS-like lesion postnatally. Prenatal ultrasound detection of ILS demonstrates that at least a subset is unambiguously congenital; the field treats ILS origin as mixed/contested (Intralobar sequestration review, PMC2876708; AFIP radiologic–pathologic correlation). This congenital-vs-acquired fork should be recorded as a mechanistic hypothesis grouping, not resolved silently.

Risk factors. - Genetic: none established. No susceptibility loci, no GWAS signal, no modifier genes. A single familial recurrence (two male infants of the same parents, both with left lower-lobe echogenic masses with systemic arterial supply on prenatal US) "raises the possibility of a genetic predisposition" (PMID:8677110). - Demographic: male sex is modestly over-represented — male:female 1.58:1 in 2,625 Chinese cases (PMID:21459605); birth-prevalence 0.45 vs 0.38 per 10,000 by infant sex in the Chinese Birth Defects Monitoring Network (BMC Pulm Med 2023, DOI:10.1186/s12890-023-02308-8). ELS shows the stronger male predominance (classically ~3–4:1). The Changsha 11-year surveillance analysis found prevalence significantly correlated with maternal age and education level (PMID:39837960) — associations, not demonstrated causes. - Environmental: no toxin, infection, or exposure has been causally linked to BPS.

Protective factors: none known, genetic or environmental.

Gene–environment interactions: none documented.

Progression/severity: ILS infections are typically chronic, recurrent, and antibiotic-refractory; hemoptysis can escalate to life-threatening. Fetal lesions frequently regress: ~80% show partial regression from ~26 weeks and up to ~18.5–50%+ substantial or complete prenatal regression (series-dependent) (Prenatal growth characteristics, PMC5828905). Quality-of-life data specific to BPS are limited to surgical-recovery series; no EQ-5D/SF-36 disease-specific literature was identified — a genuine knowledge gap.

4. Genetic / Molecular Information

  • Causal genes: none. No OMIM entry, no ClinVar/ClinGen assertions, no recurrent pathogenic variants in the malformation itself.
  • Pathogenic variants / allele frequencies / zygosity: not applicable — the lesion is almost always sporadic and no causal variant class is defined. Nothing is known about somatic (post-zygotic, lesion-confined) variants in BPS; unlike CPAM (where mosaic KRAS variants have been reported), no analogous somatic-driver study of sequestration tissue was identified. This is a flaggable knowledge gap, not evidence of absence.
  • Molecular findings in lesion tissue (IN_VITRO/human tissue evidence):
  • HOXB5: in normal human lung, Hoxb-5 protein is high in the pseudoglandular period, decreased in the canalicular period, negligible at the alveolar stage; "In BPS and CCAM tissue, Hoxb-5 is maintained in a manner typical of an earlier developmental stage and is associated with development of abnormal lung tissue" (Volpe et al., Birth Defects Res A 2003;67:550–556, PMID:14632303).
  • Cell-adhesion molecules: α2-, α3-, β1-integrin and E-cadherin expression are aberrant in BPS and CCAM tissue previously showing abnormal HoxB5; the authors conclude "aberrant cell adhesion molecule and Hox protein regulation are likely part of the mechanism involved in the development of BPS and CCAM" (Volpe et al., Am J Physiol Lung Cell Mol Physiol 2009;297:L143–L152, PMID:19411307).
  • Modifier genes, epigenetics, chromosomal abnormalities: none specifically associated. BPS is usually an isolated anomaly; karyotype is typically normal, and routine microarray is not indicated for isolated lesions (associated-anomaly cases, e.g. with CDH, follow the work-up of the associated anomaly). HGNC binding lead for the one named gene: HOXB5 = hgnc:5115 ⚠ (verify).

5. Environmental Information

No environmental factor, lifestyle exposure, or infectious agent is known to cause congenital pulmonary sequestration. CTD lists no curated chemical–disease association specific to BPS. Infection is a downstream complication (bacterial pneumonia, lung abscess, empyema within the sequestered tissue; case reports of aspergilloma and mycobacterial colonization), never the documented initiating cause of the congenital lesion. The acquired-ILS hypothesis (necrotizing pneumonia as initiating event for some adult ILS) is the one place infection is proposed as etiologic, and it remains contested (see §2).

6. Mechanism / Pathophysiology

Ordered causal chain

Developmental trunk (congenital origin — demonstrated at tissue level, inferred at molecular level): 1. An unknown initiating event during weeks 4–8 of gestation — modeled either as a supernumerary ventral foregut lung bud or as segmental bronchial atresia/obstruction of a developing airway — leads to a focus of lung parenchyma disconnected from the tracheobronchial tree. (The two models branch here and may both be true in different patients; the atresia model is supported by atretic bronchi in 82–100% of resected lesions — Riedlinger et al. 2006.) 2. Persistent/aberrant expression of early-developmental patterning factors (HOXB5 retained "in a manner typical of an earlier developmental stage", PMID:14632303) with dysregulated integrin/E-cadherin adhesion signaling (PMID:19411307) is associated with disorganized branching of the sequestered tissue — inferred as mechanism, demonstrated only as correlation. 3. Failure of the aberrant tissue's primitive splanchnic vascular connections to regress results in a persistent systemic feeding artery — thoracic aorta in ~76.6%, abdominal aorta/celiac axis in ~18.5% (PMID:21459605; PMID:442005), with >1 feeder in ~15% (PMID:442005). 4. Timing relative to pleural formation determines the branch: early bud → ILS (within the lobe, draining to pulmonary veins in ~95%); late bud → ELS (own pleura, draining to systemic veins) (Predictive role of venous drainage, PMC12072680). 5. The sequestered tissue is ventilated only by collateral channels (pores of Kohn, incomplete) or not at all, resulting in a non-functioning, fluid/mucus-filled, often microcystic mass; ~40–50% of ELS carry coexisting CPAM type 2 histology ("hybrid lesions") (Pathology Outlines).

Branch A — fetal mass effect (ELS, large lesions): 6. A large fetal mass causes mediastinal shift, lung compression, and (via lymphatic/venous obstruction or torsion) tension hydrothorax; 7. tension hydrothorax leads to impaired venous return and nonimmune hydrops fetalis (~7% of ELS), which untreated leads to fetal demise (UCSF; PMC8954010); 8. sustained compression can result in pulmonary hypoplasia and neonatal respiratory distress. Conversely, involution of the lesion's blood supply explains the frequent spontaneous prenatal regression (~80% partial) (PMC5828905).

Branch B — recurrent infection (mainly ILS, postnatal): 9. Absent bronchial clearance and poor drainage lead to mucus stasis within the sequestration; 10. stasis plus collateral aeration leads to recurrent, localized, antibiotic-refractory bacterial pneumonia, abscess, and empyema (13/25 adult cases, PMC5393996); 11. chronic inflammation results in fibrosis, bronchiectatic cystic change, and bronchiolization of alveoli seen histologically (AFIP). 12. Chronic inflammation with hypervascular systemic supply predisposes to erosion into airways/vessels, resulting in hemoptysis — occasionally massive (PMID:31886079).

Branch C — hemodynamic shunt: 13. Systemic artery → sequestration → pulmonary/systemic vein flow constitutes a left-to-right shunt; a large shunt results in volume overload, high-output congestive heart failure, and pulmonary hypertension in neonates (preterm ELS with celiac-trunk supply and double shunt — S2213576615001062; sPAP fall from 35–40 to 17 mmHg after neonatal embolization — Children 2026).

Branch D — torsion (ELS): 14. The ELS pedicle can twist, causing hemorrhagic infarction of the mass and pleural effusion, presenting as acute chest/abdominal pain (PMC9241176; PMID:40674965).

Branch E — neoplasia (rare, contested): 15. The chronically inflamed, aberrantly perfused microenvironment may rarely harbor or support malignancy (isolated reports: squamous/adenocarcinoma, carcinoid tumorlet, even melanoma within sequestrations; only ~8 lung-cancer-in-sequestration cases reported since 1963), but "there is no strong evidence supporting the argument that pulmonary sequestration by itself carries a substantial risk of malignant transformation" (Ann Thorac Surg comment; PMID:36617929).

7. Anatomical Structures Affected

  • Primary organ: lung (UBERON:0002048), overwhelmingly the lower lobes — ILS in the posterobasal segment, left > right (~60% left); ELS between left lower lobe and diaphragm in ~80%, with <10% intradiaphragmatic, infradiaphragmatic/intra-abdominal (classically left suprarenal), or intrapericardial (PMID:442005; intrapericardial systematic review, PMC12898276).
  • Vascular structures: descending thoracic aorta (≈76%) and abdominal aorta/celiac/splenic arteries (≈19%) as feeders (PMID:21459605); inferior pulmonary vein (ILS drainage); azygos/hemiazygos/portal veins (ELS drainage). Candidate UBERON ⚠: thoracic aorta UBERON:0001515 ⚠, abdominal aorta UBERON:0001516 ⚠, pulmonary vein UBERON:0002016, azygos vein UBERON:0006615 ⚠.
  • Secondary involvement: heart (volume overload), pleura (effusion, empyema), diaphragm (associated CDH; intradiaphragmatic lesions), mediastinum (shift from mass effect), esophagus/foregut (rare patent communications — "total visceral inflow and outflow" variants, PMID:26969317).
  • Body systems: respiratory (primary), cardiovascular (shunt physiology), rarely gastrointestinal (foregut communication, feeding difficulty).
  • Tissue/cell level: sequestered tissue contains disorganized airway-like structures lined by ciliated (bronchiolar-type) epithelium, dilated bronchioles/alveolar ducts, mucus-filled cysts, chronic inflammatory infiltrate and fibrosis (ILS), thick-walled muscularized systemic-type arteries; ELS may show lymphangiectasia and frequently CPAM-2-like microcystic areas (Pathology Outlines).
  • Subcellular: no specific organelle pathology is described; GO cellular-component annotation is not applicable.
  • Lateralization: unilateral in nearly all cases; left-sided predominance for both types; bilateral sequestration is reported but rare (PMID:37448606).

8. Temporal Development

  • Onset: congenital by definition; the lesion forms in the first trimester (weeks 4–8). Clinical onset is bimodal: prenatal/neonatal (most ELS, large lesions, hydrops) versus childhood–adulthood (most ILS; >50% of the Wei & Li cases diagnosed after age 20, PMID:21459605; case reports extend to the eighth decade, PMC13251046).
  • Progression: fetal lesions commonly plateau around 26 weeks and partially regress (~80%), with complete prenatal regression in ~18.5% of one cohort (PMC5828905; PMC10726340). Postnatally, untreated ILS tends toward recurrent infection with cumulative fibrotic/bronchiectatic damage; asymptomatic lesions can remain stable for decades.
  • Course pattern: episodic (recurrent pneumonia, intermittent hemoptysis) on a stable structural base; not relapsing–remitting in the immunologic sense; chronic lifelong if unresected.
  • Remission: spontaneous prenatal involution is well documented; treatment-induced "remission" is definitive after complete resection, and embolization alone achieves complete regression in a minority (7%) with partial regression in most (83%) (pediatric embolization vs surgery review).
  • Critical periods: mid-gestation surveillance window (hydrops can evolve rapidly; CVR monitoring); the first months of life for elective resection planning (commonly 3 months–2 years, institution-dependent — Surgical management of CLM, PMC13542683).

9. Inheritance and Population

  • Inheritance: none — sporadic. Not AD/AR/X-linked/mitochondrial; no penetrance, expressivity, anticipation, germline mosaicism, founder effect, consanguinity signal, or carrier frequency applies. The single sibling-recurrence report (PMID:8677110) is the entire familial literature.
  • Prevalence/incidence:
  • Birth prevalence (China, national surveillance 2010–2019): rose from 0.10 to 0.75 per 10,000 births, average 0.42/10,000 (≈1 in 24,000 births); isolated PS 0.31/10,000, non-isolated 0.11/10,000 (BMC Pulm Med 2023, DOI:10.1186/s12890-023-02308-8). The rise is attributed to improved prenatal ultrasound detection.
  • Commonly cited Western figures: ~1 in 20,000–60,000 births; BPS comprises 0.15–6.4% of congenital pulmonary malformations and is the second most common congenital lung lesion after CPAM (ESR poster; Lung Sequestration overview).
  • All congenital lung malformations together: ~1 in 2,500–8,000 live births (rising with prenatal detection; EUROCAT ~4/10,000).
  • Demographics: male excess overall (1.58:1, PMID:21459605; 0.45 vs 0.38 per 10,000, BMC 2023), strongest in ELS (~3–4:1). Chinese national data show higher detected prevalence in urban than rural areas (0.51 vs 0.30/10,000) and regional variation — detection artifacts are likely. No ethnicity-specific variant geography exists (no causal variant exists).
  • Orphanet prevalence class: ORPHA:3161 is listed among rare respiratory malformations; copy the exact epidemiology row from the ORPHA:3161 cache when curating rather than from this report.

10. Diagnostics

  • Prenatal: second-trimester ultrasound shows a well-defined, homogeneously echogenic thoracic (or suprarenal) mass; color Doppler demonstration of a systemic feeding artery from the aorta is the pathognomonic finding. Fetal MRI (high, uniform T2 signal; feeder delineation) refines diagnosis and distinguishes subdiaphragmatic ELS from neuroblastoma and adrenal hemorrhage (AJR prenatal imaging). For the suprarenal mass: sequestration "usually is echogenic, is left-sided, and can be identified in the second trimester," whereas neuroblastoma is "most often cystic, right-sided, and identified in the third trimester" (PMID:9166798). CVR (lesion volume ÷ head circumference) <1.6 predicts low hydrops risk (PMC10726340).
  • Postnatal imaging: contrast CT angiography is the current diagnostic standard — >95% accuracy/sensitivity, replacing digital subtraction angiography; it defines the feeding artery, venous drainage, and lesion anatomy for operative planning (clinical value of CTA, PMC3619944; 3D MDCT angiography in children). Chest radiograph typically shows a persistent basal opacity or recurrent same-site consolidation. MRA is an alternative without radiation. Doppler US is useful in neonates and for intra-abdominal ELS (PMC3671666).
  • Laboratory: no specific blood test or biomarker. Normal urinary catecholamines (VMA/HVA) help exclude neuroblastoma for neonatal suprarenal masses (PMC9125789). Infection work-up (cultures, inflammatory markers) during complications.
  • Histopathology (definitive, on resection): nonfunctional lung with dilated bronchioles/cysts, mucus stasis, chronic inflammation and fibrosis (ILS), elastic-walled systemic arteries; ELS resembles immature/normal lung with dilated airspaces ± lymphangiectasia; hybrid CPAM-2 areas in up to ~50% of ELS.
  • Genetic testing: not indicated for isolated BPS (no gene to test). Associated-anomaly presentations (e.g., with CDH or multiple malformations) warrant standard genetics work-up (CMA ± exome) directed at the associated condition, not at the sequestration.
  • Differential diagnosis: CPAM (no systemic feeder), congenital lobar emphysema, bronchogenic cyst, bronchial atresia with mucocele, CDH, teratoma/mediastinal mass, neuroblastoma and adrenal hemorrhage (for suprarenal ELS), necrotizing pneumonia/abscess and malignancy in adults (PMID:36741081; PMC6196208).
  • Screening: detected through routine obstetric anomaly ultrasound; no population newborn/carrier screening applies.
  • Candidate NCIT leads ⚠: CT Angiography NCIT:C20081 ⚠; Doppler Ultrasound ⚠; Fetal MRI ⚠ — verify every code before binding.

11. Outcome / Prognosis

  • Fetal: excellent in the absence of hydrops — in the UCSF series of fetal BPS the non-hydropic fetuses did well ("Fetal pulmonary sequestration: a favorable congenital lung lesion," PMID:10511360); hydrops untreated → ~100% demise; with fetal therapy (shunt or laser), hydrops resolves in essentially all treated series and survival is high (PMID:29207383; PMC8954010).
  • Postnatal/surgical: resection is curative with very low mortality in modern series; thoracoscopic resection carries low morbidity and short stay (Monocentric case series, PMC11476800; surgical management review, PMC13542683). Symptomatic patients have a >2-fold higher post-operative complication risk than asymptomatic ones — an argument used for elective resection before infection (timing database study, PMC12417296).
  • Complications defining morbidity: recurrent pneumonia, lung abscess, empyema, massive hemoptysis, hemothorax, high-output cardiac failure (neonates), ELS torsion/infarction, and rare malignancy-in-lesion (see §6, Branch E).
  • Prognostic factors: fetal — hydrops, CVR ≥1.6, lesion growth trajectory, tension hydrothorax; postnatal — symptomatic status at surgery, extent of chronic suppurative damage, cardiac shunt burden. No molecular prognostic biomarker exists.
  • Life expectancy: normal after uncomplicated resection; asymptomatic unresected adults commonly live decades undiagnosed (diagnoses into the 70s reported, PMC13251046).

12. Treatment

  • Surgical resection — definitive standard. ILS: lobectomy or segmentectomy (lung-sparing segmentectomy increasingly used); ELS: sequestrectomy. Video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery (VATS) is now the preferred approach, with better musculoskeletal/pulmonary outcomes than thoracotomy; elective resection of asymptomatic lesions is commonly performed between ~3 months and 2 years, though observation is a defensible alternative and the asymptomatic-management question remains genuinely controversial (PMC13542683; can we postpone resection?). The feeding artery must be identified and controlled first — intraoperative hemorrhage from an unrecognized (especially infradiaphragmatic) feeder is the classic operative hazard. All symptomatic lesions warrant resection. Candidate NCIT ⚠: Surgical Procedure NCIT:C15329; Lobectomy ⚠; Thoracoscopy ⚠.
  • Endovascular embolization (coils/vascular plugs of the feeding artery): used as (a) definitive minimally invasive therapy in selected patients — 7% complete and 83% partial regression in a pediatric comparative review, with no significant outcome difference vs surgery and fewer complications (coil embolization outcomes; transcatheter series, PMC10378210); (b) rescue therapy for neonatal high-output heart failure (sPAP 35–40→17 mmHg, Children 2026); (c) preoperative adjunct to reduce bleeding (PMC12835465). Residual/recollateralized tissue and incomplete involution are its limitations.
  • Fetal therapy (hydropic lesions): thoracoamniotic shunting of tension hydrothorax resolves hydrops within ~1 week in treated series; ultrasound-guided interstitial laser ablation of the feeding artery (median 26.9 weeks; hydrops resolution at median 7.5 days; hydrothorax at 21 days, PMID:29207383) appears to be the most effective definitive fetal option; sclerosant injection is reported. A French national cohort covers fetal therapy for congenital pulmonary malformations broadly (PMID:39138024).
  • Supportive care: antibiotics for infective episodes (bridge to resection, never curative); bronchial-artery/feeder embolization for acute hemoptysis; drainage of empyema; cardiac failure management in neonates.
  • Pharmacotherapy/advanced therapeutics: no disease-specific drug, gene, cell, RNA, targeted, or immunotherapy exists or is in development — the condition is structural.
  • Experimental/registries: NCT06302985 (nebulized indocyanine green fluorescence guidance for thoracoscopic sequestration surgery, multicenter, China); NCT03044769 (Swiss congenital lung anomaly registry); NCT03913416 (3D-printed models for thoracoscopic planning); NCT04449614 (surgical management review cohort).
  • Treatment algorithm (synthesis): prenatal diagnosis → serial US/CVR; hydrops → fetal intervention (shunt/laser); neonatal symptoms → early resection (± embolization bridge); asymptomatic → postnatal CTA, then elective VATS resection in infancy or structured observation (institutional equipoise); adult symptomatic → resection (± preoperative embolization); adult asymptomatic/elderly → individualized, with no strong malignancy rationale for prophylactic resection (Ann Thorac Surg comment).

13. Prevention

  • Primary prevention: none possible — no modifiable cause is known. No vaccine relevance.
  • Secondary prevention: routine second-trimester anomaly ultrasound is the de facto screening program; early detection enables hydrops surveillance, delivery planning at a fetal-surgery-capable center, and elective resection before infective complications.
  • Tertiary prevention: elective resection of known lesions prevents recurrent pneumonia, hemoptysis, and (in ELS) torsion; prompt treatment of infections pending surgery; feeder embolization to pre-empt hemorrhage in high-risk anatomy.
  • Genetic counseling: recurrence risk is essentially population-level; the single sibling-recurrence report (PMID:8677110) justifies mentioning anecdotal recurrence without quantifiable risk. No carrier testing or PGD applies.
  • Public health/environmental interventions, prophylactic medication: not applicable.

14. Other Species / Natural Disease

Naturally occurring extralobar pulmonary sequestration is described in dogs (first canine report: a 6-month-old male mixed-breed dog with gross and histopathologic characterization, J Vet Med Sci, PMID:23569925; NCBITaxon:9615) and in cattle as "pulmonary choristoma/extralobar sequestration" in newborn calves, with systemic intra-mass arterial supply as a shared macroscopic hallmark (NCBITaxon:9913; Frontiers Vet Sci case). These are sporadic case reports: no OMIA entry exists for pulmonary sequestration, no breed predisposition (hence no VBO term) is established, and no inherited animal form is known. Comparative pathology is concordant with the human disease — nonfunctional pulmonary tissue with its own pleura and systemic arterial supply — supporting a conserved developmental accident of foregut/lung budding rather than a species-specific process. The disease is non-transmissible; zoonotic potential is not applicable.

15. Model Organisms

There is no genetic animal model of pulmonary sequestration, consistent with the absence of a causal gene. Relevant adjacent systems:

  • Mouse developmental biology (NCBITaxon:10090): mouse Hoxb5 expression during lung branching morphogenesis parallels the human pattern that is pathologically prolonged in BPS tissue (PMID:14632303 draws the human–mouse comparison explicitly). Hoxb5-perturbation mice model branching regulation, not sequestration itself — any KB link should be graded as mechanistic-context only, with explicit fidelity limitations.
  • Human lesion tissue (ex vivo) is the de facto "model": Western blot/immunohistochemistry of resected BPS vs normal developing lung underpins the entire molecular literature (PMID:14632303; PMID:19411307) — evidence_source IN_VITRO on human surgical tissue.
  • Spontaneous veterinary cases (dog, calf; §14) recapitulate the anatomy (systemic feeder, separate pleura) with high fidelity but are too rare for systematic study.
  • No zebrafish, Drosophila, C. elegans, organoid, or iPSC model of sequestration exists; no IMPC/MGI phenotype line is annotated to this malformation. This is an honest model-organism gap worth recording as such (KNOWLEDGE_GAP), and lung-bud organoid systems are the plausible future platform for testing the HOXB5/adhesion hypothesis.
