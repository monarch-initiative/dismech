Chronic Pyelonephritis

1. Disease Information

2026-11-27
Claude Code MONDO:0001110 Model: claude-haiku-5-5, claude-opus-5-5 61 citations Prompt: disease_pathophysiology_research.md f452f0a9c7e3

1. Disease Information

Overview. Chronic pyelonephritis is "continuing pyogenic infection of the kidney that occurs almost exclusively in patients with major anatomic abnormalities. Reflux of infected urine into the renal pelvis is the usual mechanism. Causes include obstructive uropathy, struvite calculi, and, most commonly, vesicoureteral reflux (VUR)" (MSD Manual Professional – Chronic Pyelonephritis).

The pathology is described as "patchy interstitial scarring, tubular atrophy, and loss of nephron mass." VUR "ultimately results in chronic interstitial nephritis, tubular atrophy, and renal scarring along with glomerular hypertrophy and secondary FSGS" (MSD – Reflux nephropathy; StatPearls NBK526055).

The concept is still debated. Radiopaedia notes "no definitive consensus as to whether the condition represents an active chronic infection, arises from multiple recurrent infections, or represents stable changes from a remote single infection" (Radiopaedia).

2. Etiology

Causal factors. These are infectious and mechanical: - Infection: ascending bacterial infection, most often E. coli and Proteus mirabilis (MSD). - Structural substrate: VUR (most common), obstruction or obstructive uropathy, and struvite stones (MSD). - Host inflammation: inflammation, not bacterial genotype alone, appears to drive scarring (PMID 20850815; Olson et al. 2017, PMID 28882930).

Risk factors - Clinical: VUR, obstruction, kidney stones, neurogenic bladder and diabetes (BMJ Best Practice). - In children after a first UTI, scarring was more common with VUR: "RR: 2.6 [95% CI: 1.7–3.9]" (Shaikh et al. 2010, PMID 21059720). - Recurrent UTI was a risk factor for new scarring in a RIVUR secondary analysis (PMC4702233). - Bladder and bowel dysfunction is also a risk factor (Swedish reflux trial V, PMID 20488486; PMC3968988). - Demographic: Medscape reports the disease is about twice as common in females, more common in children under 2, and about three times more common in white than African-American children (Medscape). - Genetic susceptibility: - A 2023 systematic review and meta-analysis found pooled associations of pediatric UTI with variation in CXCR1, IL8 (CXCL8), TGF, TLR4 and VDR, but rated the credibility as weak (PMID 38128862). - CXCR1: in Lundstedt et al. 2007, when reflux was excluded, "54% of the patients had CXCR1 sequence variants" (PMID 17786197). The work started from IL-8 receptor (mIL-8Rh) mutant mice that developed acute pyelonephritis with severe tissue damage. - ACE insertion/deletion: the D allele was associated with scarring (OR 1.73, recessive model) in an earlier meta-analysis (Pediatr Nephrol). - VUR/CAKUT genes: in a Chinese pediatric primary-VUR registry, a monogenic cause was found in 7.39% of patients (PAX2 n=4, TNXB n=3, GATA3 n=3, SLIT2 n=3, ROBO2 n=2, TBX18 n=2). "PAX2 mutation carriers (HR 5.1 …) and TNXB mutation carriers (HR 20.3 …)" had a higher risk of ESRD (PMID 34059960). - In European cohorts, a large linkage and association scan found no major VUR locus (PMC4780477; review PMC5468264).

Protective factors - Genetic: common TLR4 promoter variants "may protect against severe urinary tract infection" (PMC2873976). - Uromodulin (Tamm–Horsfall protein, UMOD): the protein is protective in mouse knockouts (Bates et al., Kidney Int 2004;65:791–797; Mo et al., Am J Physiol Renal 2004;286:F795). Urinary uromodulin was studied against UTI risk in the Cardiovascular Health Study (PMC5409878). - Environmental: prompt treatment of febrile UTI and correction of obstruction. Treatment-delay data were not retrieved.

Gene–environment interaction. Disease requires both a host factor (reflux or obstruction, innate-immune variants) and bacterial exposure. In mice, sterile reflux alone did not cause reflux nephropathy; reflux plus sustained kidney infection did (Bowen et al. 2013, PMC3701213). Tlr4-defective C3H/HeJ mice had more severe damage than C3H/HeN mice. Bacterial papG II was associated with acute pyelonephritis, while "scarring was predicted by host factors rather than bacterial virulence genes" (summary of PMID 20850815).

4. Genetic / Molecular Information

  • Causal genes: None. The disease is acquired.
  • Susceptibility genes: CXCR1, TLR4, CXCL8, VDR, TGFB1 and ACE (see Section 2), plus VUR/CAKUT genes PAX2, TNXB, GATA3, SLIT2, ROBO2 and TBX18 (PMID 34059960).
  • ACMG classification: Not applicable to susceptibility alleles. PAX2 and other CAKUT variants are classified in their own monogenic disorders.
  • Variant types: CXCR1 variants were heterozygous polymorphisms and rare mutations predicted to reduce CXCR1 expression (PMID 17786197, as summarized in PMC4286230). In TNXB-associated VUR, all variant carriers tested had joint hypermobility, with no excess high-grade reflux or scarring (PMC4780477).
  • Allele frequencies (gnomAD), epigenetics, chromosomal abnormalities: Not retrieved, and no disease-specific data were found.
  • Germline vs. somatic: All are germline susceptibility variants.

5. Environmental Information

  • Infectious agents: Escherichia coli (uropathogenic) and Proteus mirabilis are the most common causes (MSD).
  • In XGP case series (693 cases), P. mirabilis was found in 18.5% of positive cultures and E. coli in 14.8% (PMID 33827527).
  • Proteus urease makes urine alkaline, which leads to struvite stones and in turn to obstruction.
  • UPEC virulence factors: P fimbriae (PapG adhesin binding Gal(α1-4)Gal) are "strongly associated with pyelonephritis in children" (PMC10341809). Hemolysin is associated with renal damage.
  • Lifestyle and other factors: Bladder and bowel dysfunction and diabetes are risk factors (BMJ Best Practice). Metabolic acidosis worsened pyelonephritis in reflux-prone mice (PMC7543054). No toxin or occupational data were found.

6. Mechanism / Pathophysiology

Causal chain: 1. Structural predisposition: congenital VUR (sometimes from PAX2, TNXB or other CAKUT gene variants), obstruction or stones lead to retrograde flow or stasis of urine in the renal pelvis (MSD; PMID 34059960). 2. Bacterial ascent: UPEC, using P fimbriae/PapG, or Proteus colonize the bladder and then the pelvis, which results in intrarenal reflux of infected urine into collecting ducts, preferentially at the poles (PMC10341809; Radiopaedia). - Branch: with Proteus, urease produces struvite stones, which cause obstruction, which feeds back into step 1. 3. Innate recognition: TLR4 on uroepithelial and tubular cells senses LPS, which induces IL-8/CXCL8 secretion. This recruits neutrophils through CXCR1 (PMID 17786197; PMC4286230). - Branch A (too little response): low CXCR1 means neutrophils get trapped and bacteria are not cleared, which leads to severe acute pyelonephritis and scarring (shown in mice; associated in humans). - Branch B (too much response): an excessive inflammatory response causes collateral tubular injury. In mice "the extent of fibrosis correlates most significantly with inflammation" (Olson 2017, PMID 28882930; related PMC5283888). The steroid rationale rests on the same idea (PMC10393849). 4. Tubulointerstitial injury: tubular rupture releases Tamm–Horsfall protein (uromodulin) into the interstitium. This is associated with peritubular inflammation (a histological association; causation is inferred) (PathologyOutlines). 5. Repair and fibrosis: repeated or persistent injury results in interstitial fibrosis, tubular atrophy with "thyroidization" (dilated tubules with colloid-like casts), and obliterative endarteritis. Together these produce a coarse cortical scar over a blunted calyx (Brown Digital Pathology). 6. Nephron loss: reduced nephron mass leads to compensatory glomerular hyperfiltration and hypertrophy, then secondary FSGS and proteinuria (StatPearls). The hyperfiltration step is inferred from general CKD physiology. 7. Clinical outcome: hypertension (the renin-angiotensin link is inconsistent; PMC3216353) and progressive CKD, which can lead to ESKD (PMID 7761049). - XGP branch: chronic obstruction plus infection leads to lipid-laden macrophage (xanthoma cell) granulomatous destruction of the kidney (PMC8434833).

Mechanism checklist: - Pathways: toll-like receptor 4 signaling, IL-8/CXCR1 chemotaxis, TGF-β-driven fibrosis (TGF variants per PMID 38128862). - Suggested GO process labels: toll-like receptor 4 signaling pathway; neutrophil chemotaxis; inflammatory response; defense response to Gram-negative bacterium; extracellular matrix organization / fibrosis. - Suggested CL labels: neutrophil; macrophage (foam cell in XGP); kidney tubule epithelial cell; urothelial cell; fibroblast / myofibroblast. - Omics: No human transcriptomic, proteomic, metabolomic, single-cell or spatial datasets specific to chronic pyelonephritis were retrieved.

7. Anatomical Structures Affected

UBERON IDs were not retrieved; these are labels only. - Primary: kidney (renal cortex, renal papilla, renal pelvis, calyces) and the tubulointerstitium. - Upper and lower urinary tract: ureter and bladder (the source of reflux). - Secondary: cardiovascular system (hypertension); placenta and pregnancy (pre-eclampsia). - Tissues and cells: tubular epithelium, interstitial connective tissue, arterioles (obliterative endarteritis); neutrophils, macrophages, fibroblasts. - Subcellular (suggested GO cellular component labels): extracellular matrix; tubular lumen casts containing uromodulin. - Localization: scars are most common at the upper pole (MSD reflux nephropathy). Disease can be unilateral or bilateral. Severe bilateral scarring predicts hypertension (PMID 7761049). XGP is usually unilateral.

8. Temporal Development

  • Onset: Insidious, after childhood febrile UTIs, especially under age 2 (Medscape). XGP presents mostly in middle-aged to elderly patients (Medscape).
  • Course: Episodic infections are superimposed on slowly progressive scarring.
  • Scarring appears 5–24 months after UTI on DMSA (MDedge summary of PMID 21059720).
  • Kidney function typically lasts ≥20 years (MSD).
  • About 2% of children with VUR-related disease progress to renal failure (StatPearls).
  • Remission: VUR often resolves on its own, but "resolution of reflux does not appear to correct hypertension" (StatPearls).
  • Critical period: Infancy and early childhood, when the growing kidney is most vulnerable to scarring. Early treatment of febrile UTI is the window for intervention.

9. Inheritance and Population

  • Inheritance: Multifactorial. Primary VUR itself clusters in families, with heterogeneous monogenic causes in about 7% of the Chinese cohort (PMID 34059960). Penetrance, anticipation, founder effects and carrier frequencies are not applicable or not retrieved.
  • Burden of kidney failure: Reflux nephropathy accounted for about 7% of pediatric ESKD in ANZDATA 2006–2011 (ANZDATA 2012). Structural causes as a group accounted for 35.8% (Orr et al., PMID 19373494). Reported global estimates range from 7% to 17% (PMC2478704).
  • Prevalence and incidence: Population-level rates per 100,000 were not retrieved.
  • XGP frequency: 0.6–1% of pyelonephritis cases (Medscape).
  • Sex ratio: About 2:1 female:male (Medscape).

10. Diagnostics

  • Laboratory:
  • Urinalysis and urine culture.
  • Serum creatinine / eGFR.
  • Urine protein (proteinuria is a prognostic marker; PMID 9184449).
  • In XGP, resistance profiling matters: piperacillin/tazobactam resistance was 14.3% and ceftriaxone resistance 16.6% (PMID 33827527).
  • Imaging:
  • DMSA scintigraphy "is the gold standard for the diagnosis of acute pyelonephritis and renal scarring" (StatPearls).
  • Ultrasound and CT show a "large, deep, segmental, coarse cortical scar usually extending to one or more of the renal calyces" (Radiopaedia).
  • A voiding cystourethrogram (VCUG) detects VUR. The AAP 2011 guideline recommends renal-bladder ultrasound after a first febrile UTI at age 2–24 months and does not routinely recommend VCUG (Roberts et al., Pediatrics 2011;128:595; commentary PMID 21873698).
  • Histopathology: patchy interstitial fibrosis with chronic inflammation, thyroidization of tubules, interstitial Tamm–Horsfall protein (PAS-positive), periglomerular fibrosis, obliterative endarteritis, and early preserved glomeruli (PathologyOutlines). XGP shows foamy macrophages.
  • Genetic testing: Not used for diagnosis. A CAKUT/VUR gene panel (PAX2, TNXB, GATA3, SLIT2, ROBO2, TBX18) may be considered in familial or syndromic VUR. No WGS, CMA or omics diagnostics are established.
  • Differential diagnosis:
  • Other chronic tubulointerstitial nephritides (drug-induced, analgesic nephropathy).
  • Renal dysplasia / hypoplasia, which looks similar on imaging but is congenital.
  • Renal infarct, tuberculosis.
  • For XGP: renal cell carcinoma.
  • ADTKD-UMOD.
  • Screening: There is no population screening. Imaging is targeted after febrile UTI.

11. Outcome / Prognosis

  • Course: Usually slow (MSD). In 294 adults with reflux nephropathy, about 38% had hypertension and about 31% had proteinuria (PMID 7761049).
  • Prognostic factors: proteinuria ("the worst prognostic feature," PMID 739156); diastolic hypertension; bilateral severe scarring; reduced GFR; and PAX2 or TNXB variants (PMID 34059960).
  • Pregnancy: outcomes are good when kidney function is near normal and hypertension is absent; risk rises with creatinine at conception (PMID 3330495; PMID 7924016).
  • Complications: ESKD, hypertension, pre-eclampsia, stones, and XGP or abscess. After nephrectomy for XGP, complications occurred in 73% (n=8) in one 2023 series (Ir J Med Sci).
  • Transplant: Long-term kidney transplant outcomes in CAKUT, which includes reflux nephropathy, are reported in PMID 31309282.
  • Mortality and quality-of-life figures: Not retrieved.

12. Treatment

NCIT IDs were not retrieved; these are labels only. - Antibiotics: For acute flares, treat febrile UTI for 7–10 days, orally where feasible (AAP; PMC4173959). For chronic disease, long-term antibiotics (MSD). - Antimicrobial prophylaxis (trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole): - RIVUR (NCT00405704; n=607; PMID 24795142) halved recurrence (RR 0.55) but did not reduce scarring in the primary analysis. 63% of breakthrough E. coli isolates were resistant on prophylaxis vs 19% on placebo. - PRIVENT (Craig et al., NEJM 2009;361:1748; n=576) reduced recurrence modestly. - Swedish reflux trial IV reported renal damage outcomes by treatment arm (PMID 20494369). - Adjuvant corticosteroids: In the DEXCAR RCT, scarring was 22% with dexamethasone vs 21% with placebo (p=0.907), with "no evidence for an adjuvant corticosteroid treatment recommendation" (PMID 35041042). A meta-analysis of 5 RCTs (n=498) found 14.1% vs 27.3% scarring (PMC10393849). This is experimental and unresolved. - CKD and hypertension care: antihypertensives and renoprotection. No reflux nephropathy-specific RCTs were retrieved. - Surgery and interventions: correction of obstruction, stone removal, and VUR correction (endoscopic injection or ureteral reimplantation). Nephrectomy is used for a nonfunctioning infected kidney (MSD) and is the main treatment for XGP; laparoscopic approaches are increasing and partial nephrectomy is an option for focal XGP (PMC8434833). Dialysis and transplantation are used for ESKD. - Advanced therapies: Gene, cell, RNA and targeted therapies are not applicable. - Pharmacogenomics: none specific to this disease. TMP-SMX and G6PD considerations were not retrieved.

13. Prevention

  • Primary: prompt diagnosis and treatment of febrile UTI in infants; treating bladder and bowel dysfunction; adequate hydration (not retrieved as evidence). There is no licensed UTI vaccine; none was retrieved.
  • Secondary: renal ultrasound after a first febrile UTI (AAP 2011), DMSA in selected children, and blood pressure and proteinuria monitoring.
  • Tertiary: prophylaxis in selected high-grade VUR (RIVUR, PRIVENT), surgical correction, blood pressure control, and pre-conception counseling (PMID 3330495).
  • Genetic counseling: relevant only for familial VUR or CAKUT.

14. Other Species / Natural Disease

NCBI Taxon IDs were not retrieved. - Pigs (sows): the porcine cystitis-pyelonephritis complex is "a leading cause of mortality in sows," primarily caused by Actinobaculum suis, often with E. coli. Its incidence fell after artificial insemination was widely adopted (Merck Vet Manual; Isling et al. 2010, Acta Vet Scand). - Cattle: contagious bovine pyelonephritis is caused by the Corynebacterium renale group (C. renale, C. pilosum, C. cystitidis). Herd prevalence is 0.5–1.5%, and up to one-third of affected animals die or are culled (Anipedia). - Dogs and cats: no data retrieved. - Zoonotic potential: none established.

15. Model Organisms

  • Mouse, male C3H/HeN surgical bladder-inoculation model with UPEC: produces ascending pyelonephritis, abscesses and fibrosis, and antibiotic-treated mice develop scars. This models post-treatment scarring and kidney function loss (Olson et al., Dis Model Mech 2017, PMID 28882930; PMC5719254).
  • Mouse, C3H strains with natural VUR (C3H/HeN vs Tlr4-defective C3H/HeJ): kidney infection is necessary for reflux nephropathy, and sterile reflux alone is not enough (PMC3701213). The same background was used to show metabolic acidosis worsens disease (PMC7543054).
  • Genetic mouse models:
  • mIL-8Rh (Cxcr2) knockout mice develop severe acute pyelonephritis and scarring (PMID 17786197).
  • Umod (Tamm–Horsfall protein) knockout mice are more susceptible to UTI (Bates 2004; Mo 2004).
  • Pax2 1Neu+/- mice show VUR (PMID 17881463); Pax2/Emx2 compound heterozygotes also do (PMC3123351).
  • Robo2 mutants have CAKUT (PMC4962383).
  • Pig and minipig: high-pressure sterile reflux causes scarring (PMID 6086232). This supports a "water-hammer" component that conflicts with the mouse data, so the mechanism differs by species.
  • Methods: methods to detect VUR and obstruction in mice are described in PMID 22639276 and PMC3094056.
  • Limitations: mouse models compress time (weeks rather than decades). Most models use males because surgical inoculation is needed to get reflux in males, whereas human disease is female-predominant. Hypertension and pregnancy complications are poorly modeled. Resources: MGI, IMSR.

Evidence types

  • Human clinical: RCTs (RIVUR, PRIVENT, Swedish reflux trial, DEXCAR), meta-analyses, cohorts, registries.
  • Mouse in vivo: Olson 2017, Bowen 2013, Cxcr2/Umod/Pax2/Robo2 mice.
  • Porcine experimental: sterile reflux study.
  • Computational / genetic association: 2023 meta-analysis (PMID 38128862).
