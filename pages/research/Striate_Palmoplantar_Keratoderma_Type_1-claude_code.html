Striate Palmoplantar Keratoderma Type 1 (SPPK1; DSG1): Research Report

Scope and identifiers. This report covers MONDO:0007859 / OMIM 148700. It is caused by heterozygous variants in DSG1 (desmoglein 1; HGNC hgnc:3048) on chromosome 18q12.1. Biallelic DSG1 loss of function causes a different disease, SAM syndrome (MONDO:0014218), which this report treats only as a differential diagnosis. Striate PPK caused by DSP (type 2, MONDO:0013034) and by KRT1 (type 3) are separate diseases.

1. Disease information

SPPK1 is an autosomal dominant, non-syndromic thickening of the skin of the palms and soles (keratoderma). Typical features: - Palms: linear hyperkeratotic bands running along the volar (palm-side) fingers onto the palm. - Soles: focal hyperkeratosis at pressure points, often appearing before the palmar changes. - No other organs involved: no hair, heart or systemic disease.

The pattern varies. The same gene, and sometimes the same allele, gives focal or diffuse keratoderma in some families. That is why OMIM and MONDO name the disease "striate, focal, or diffuse".

2. Etiology

Primary cause. Heterozygous germline loss-of-function variants in DSG1 reduce the amount of desmoglein 1 by half (haploinsufficiency). - "Recently we have identified a mutation in the DSG1 gene coding for desmoglein 1 as the cause of the autosomal dominant skin disease striate palmoplantar keratoderma (SPPK) in which affected individuals have marked hyperkeratotic bands on the palms and soles." PMID:11313759 [cached] (Hunt et al. 2001, Eur J Hum Genet) - "This latter mutation thus results in the mutant allele synthesising only 25 amino acid residues of the prosequence of the protein so that this is effectively a null mutation implying that dominance in the case of this mutation was caused by haploinsufficiency." PMID:11313759 [cached]

Environmental modifier: mechanical stress. This is a modifier of how severe the lesions are, not a cause. - "The most severe consequences of SPPK mutations are in regions of the body where pressure and abrasion are greatest and where desmosome function is most necessary." PMID:11313759 [cached] - Desmoglein 1 loss "has a critical impact on the integrity of tissues experiencing strong mechanical stress." PMID:16484817 [cached]

Other factors: - No GWAS or susceptibility loci have been reported. - No protective genetic or environmental factors have been reported. - One 2024 case had a co-occurring LDLR splice variant. The authors only speculated about a role and did not show one: He & Zhu 2024, Genes & Genomics, doi 10.1007/s13258-024-01587-7 [web, not cached].

Gene-environment interaction. The only one documented is genotype × friction/pressure: lesions concentrate at sites of mechanical load. This comes from clinical observation, not from formal study.

4. Genetic and molecular information

Gene and protein. - Gene: DSG1 (hgnc:3048, OMIM 125670). It encodes a desmosomal cadherin of the stratified epidermis that is expressed most strongly in the suprabasal and granular layers. - Protein structure:* the extracellular domains are EC1–EC4 plus an extracellular anchor. The intracellular part binds plakoglobin.

Variant spectrum. More than 25 alleles have been reported. Almost all introduce a premature termination codon (PTC). - "To date, more than 25 mutations in DSG1 have been identified in patients with SPPK1. These mutations were uniformly found to result in a premature termination codon (PTC)." PMID:29315490 [cached] - First allele described (Rickman et al. 1999): a splice-site variant causing in-frame skipping of exon 3. "This causes aberrant splicing of exon 2 to exon 4, which are in-frame, with the consequent removal of exon 3 encoding part of the prosequence, the mature protein cleavage site and part of the first extracellular domain." PMID:10332028 [cached] - Nonsense: - c.395C>A: "Direct DNA sequencing analysis revealed a heterozygous C-to-A transversion at nt 395 of the DSG1 gene." PMID:12930313 [cached] - Recurrent p.R26X, Pakistani families: "Mutation analysis resulted in the identification of one recurrent mutation (p.R26X) and four novel mutations (c.Ivs4-2A>G, c.515C>T, c.Ivs9-3C>G, and c.1399delA) in the DSG1 gene. Each mutation is predicted to cause haploinsufficiency of DSG1 protein." PMID:19157795 [cached] - p.(Arg117), reported 2024 (Koschitzki et al., Clin Case Rep, DOAJ) [web, not cached] - Frameshift / NMD: - Exon-12 skipping allele: "We predict that this mutation will lead to the skipping of exon 12 which is out of frame (134nt), subsequent degradation of the mutant mRNA by non-sense mediated RNA decay, and haploinsufficiency for DSG1." PMID:17194569 [cached] - UK series: p.Tyr126Hisfs2, p.Ser521Tyrfs2, p.Trp3, p.Asp591Phefs9, p.Met249Ilefs6 PMID:27534273 [cached] - Syrian family: "This frameshift mutation was heterozygous in all affected family members and absent in all normal individuals." PMID:31192455 [cached] - Missense (exceptional): "…the first case of SPPK caused by a missense (rather than PTC-causing) mutation affecting a residue predicted to be of critical importance for DSG1-mediated adhesion." PMID:29315490 [cached] - Somatic vs germline: germline. No somatic or mosaic cases were found.

Functional consequence. Loss of function through haploinsufficiency, mostly via nonsense-mediated decay (GO:0000184 nuclear-transcribed mRNA catabolic process, nonsense-mediated decay). There is no dominant-negative effect. In one patient, sequencing of cDNA from affected skin found no transcript from the mutant allele: "Direct sequencing of cDNA derived from affected skin in one patient failed to reveal a pathogenic mutation, suggesting that SPPK results from haploinsufficiency for DSG1." PMID:19018793 [cached]

Allelic series by dose: - Heterozygous → SPPK1. - Biallelic → SAM syndrome: "Monoallelic desmoglein 1 mutations have been known for many years to cause striate palmoplantar keratoderma, but only recently, biallelic loss-of-function mutations were associated with a new disorder, designated as SAM syndrome…" PMID:25041099 [cached]; original description Samuelov et al. 2013, Nat Genet PMID:23974871 [web; PMID confirmed on PubMed]. - Exception: a 2025 J Dermatol report describes a homozygous splice variant, c.685-3T>A, that gave only mild striate PPK without SAM (PMC12592573) [web, not cached]. A hypomorphic (partially functional) allele would explain this, but that explanation is an inference.

Other genetic data: - Allele frequency: PTC alleles are absent or ultra-rare in gnomAD. DSG1 tolerates loss of function less than most genes, but some heterozygous LoF carriers exist, so penetrance or ascertainment caveats apply. gnomAD was not queried for this report. - ClinVar / ClinGen: neither was queried in this session, so no curated tier is reported here. - Modifier genes, epigenetics, chromosomal abnormalities: none reported. Large 18q deletions spanning the desmosomal cadherin cluster would be expected to cause haploinsufficiency, but no such case was found.

5. Environmental information

  • Main modifier: mechanical friction and pressure (manual labour, walking, footwear), as described in section 2. There is no chemical, toxic, infectious or lifestyle cause.
  • Infection: not applicable as a cause. Secondary fungal or bacterial infection of fissures is a general PPK complication; no SPPK1-specific data were found.

6. Mechanism and pathophysiology

Causal chain

  1. A heterozygous DSG1 PTC variant (nonsense, frameshift or splice) leads to the mutant mRNA being degraded by nonsense-mediated decay (GO:0000184), and therefore to about 50% of normal desmoglein 1 protein. Demonstrated: PMID:17194569, 19018793, 11313759.
  2. Branch: the rare missense allele (PMID:29315490) instead impairs adhesion through the EC interface, so the protein is present but less functional. Inferred from structural prediction.
  3. Reduced Dsg1 in suprabasal keratinocytes (CL:4033013 suprabasal keratinocyte) leads to fewer and smaller suprabasal desmosomes, i.e. disturbed GO:0002934 desmosome organization. Demonstrated in patient skin: "Both Dp and Dsg1 mutations were accompanied by significantly reduced numbers of desmosomes in the suprabasal layers, while decreased desmosome size was evident only in Dsg1-associated SPPK." PMID:15149499 [cached]
  4. Weaker desmosomes lead to reduced keratinocyte cell-cell adhesion (GO:0098609 cell-cell adhesion), which appears as widened intercellular spaces and partial acantholysis in the spinous and granular layers. "varying degrees of widening of the intercellular spaces and partial disadhesion of keratinocytes in the mid and upper epidermal spinous cell layers, often extending to the granular cell layer." PMID:20082890 [cached]
  5. Parallel branch: loss of Dsg1 signalling. Normally Dsg1 binds Erbin, which disrupts SHOC2–Ras–Raf scaffolds and so suppresses EGFR → ERK1/2 signalling (GO:0007173 epidermal growth factor receptor signaling pathway; GO:0070371 ERK1 and ERK2 cascade).
  6. "Instead, Dsg1 was required for suppression of epidermal growth factor receptor-Erk1/2 (extracellular signal-regulated kinase 1/2) signaling, thereby facilitating keratinocyte progression through a terminal differentiation program." PMID:19546243 [cached; in vitro]
  7. In patient epidermis: "analysis of epidermis from DSG1-deficient patients with SPPK demonstrated increased Ras-SHOC2 colocalization and decreased Erbin-SHOC2 colocalization…" PMID:23524970 [cached]
  8. Result: the brake on ERK signalling is weaker.
  9. Higher ERK activity and weakened scaffolding lead to abnormal terminal differentiation (GO:0030216 keratinocyte differentiation): K16 goes up, the keratin filament network is disturbed, and involucrin is abnormal.
  10. "In both types of SPPK upregulation of K16 was pronounced and involucrin labelling was abnormal." PMID:15149499 [cached]
  11. "The expression of keratins K5, K14 and K10 was reduced in Dsg1-associated SPPK skin…" PMID:15149499 [cached]
  12. Steps 3 and 5 combined with mechanical load (friction and pressure on volar skin) lead to compensatory hyperproliferation and hyperkeratosis, which is localised to stressed areas (UBERON:0013776 skin of palmar/plantar part of autopod). The result is linear bands along flexural lines of the fingers and focal plantar plaques. How load is turned into hyperkeratosis is inferred from where lesions occur (PMID:11313759, 16484817).
  13. Thick hyperkeratotic plaques lead to fissuring and pain on walking (HP:6001399), the main clinical burden.

Upstream vs downstream

  • Upstream: NMD → haploinsufficiency.
  • Middle: desmosome loss and EGFR-ERK de-repression, which are parallel and interacting branches.
  • Downstream: differentiation defect → hyperkeratosis → pain.

Other mechanism categories

  • Immune involvement: not part of SPPK1. Allergic/Th2 inflammation emerges only with complete DSG1 loss (SAM: PMID:23974871, PMID:25041099).
  • Metabolic, epigenetic, omics: no metabolic, epigenetic, single-cell, spatial or proteomic studies specific to SPPK1 were found. Dsg1-related transcriptomic studies come from keratinocyte knockdown and SAM work, not from SPPK1 tissue.

7. Anatomical structures affected

  • Organ:
  • Primary: skin of the palms and soles (UBERON:0013776).
  • Occasional: knees, ankles, knuckles, nails.
  • No internal organs are affected. Hair and heart are normal, unlike DSP-related syndromes.
  • Tissue and cell: stratified squamous epidermis; suprabasal (spinous and granular) keratinocytes.
  • Subcellular: the desmosome (the adhesive junction plaque) and the keratin intermediate-filament network.
  • Laterality: bilateral, but often asymmetric, following individual pressure patterns.

8. Temporal development

  • Onset: infancy or childhood, often in the first years once the child walks. The soles are frequently affected before the palms. Example: "since 1 year of age" (PMID:29315490).
  • Course: chronic, lifelong and stable. There are no stages and it does not progress systemically.
  • Fluctuation: severity changes with mechanical load and occupation. There is no spontaneous remission.
  • Possible intervention window: early protective footwear may reduce painful plantar plaques. This is expert opinion only.

10. Diagnostics

Clinical diagnosis. Striate or focal PPK with a dominant family history, without hair, cardiac or other organ involvement.

Histology. Hyperkeratosis and acanthosis with widened intercellular spaces and suprabasal disadhesion. "These findings, which are associated with haploinsufficiency of desmoglein 1, were not observed in any of the other 6 PPK cases." PMID:20082890 [cached] - Electron microscopy: fewer and smaller suprabasal desmosomes (PMID:15149499). - Immunostaining: reduced Dsg1 staining.

Genetic testing: - Recommended first test: a multigene PPK panel (DSG1, DSP, KRT1, KRT9, KRT16, AAGAB, SERPINB7, TRPV3, AQP5, GJB2 and others), or exome sequencing. - Single-gene DSG1 testing: reasonable when the striate pattern and histology fit. - Copy number: deletion/duplication analysis is worth considering, although no SPPK1 CNV has been reported. - Not useful: chromosomal microarray, karyotype, FISH, mtDNA and repeat-expansion testing. - Why a broad panel matters: "Mutations in desmoglein 1 cause diverse inherited palmoplantar keratoderma phenotypes: implications for genetic screening" (PMID:27534273). Because the gene also produces focal and diffuse forms, DSG1 should be on panels for non-striate PPK as well.

Differential diagnosis: - SPPK2 (DSP): may come with woolly hair or cardiomyopathy in recessive and syndromic forms (Carvajal syndrome). - SPPK3 (KRT1). - Focal non-epidermolytic PPK: KRT6C/KRT16. - Punctate PPK: AAGAB. - Other hereditary PPK (e.g. epidermolytic PPK, KRT9): shows epidermolysis on histology. - SAM syndrome (biallelic DSG1): severe dermatitis, allergies, metabolic wasting. - Acquired keratoderma: friction callosities, keratoderma climactericum.

Screening. No newborn or population screening. Cascade testing of relatives is possible once the familial variant is known.

11. Outcome and prognosis

  • Survival: normal life expectancy. There is no disease-specific mortality.
  • Morbidity: pain, fissures, limits on walking and manual work, and cosmetic and psychosocial burden. There are no validated QoL instrument data.
  • Complications: painful fissures and possible secondary infection (general PPK knowledge). No increased cancer risk has been reported.
  • Recovery: none expected. Symptoms can be controlled.
  • Prognostic factors: occupational and mechanical load. No biomarkers exist.

12. Treatment

Treatment is entirely supportive. There are no disease-modifying or approved targeted therapies. Koschitzki 2024 notes that effective treatment options remain limited [web, not cached].

13. Prevention

  • Primary prevention: not applicable beyond genetic counselling. Each child of an affected parent has a 50% risk. Prenatal or preimplantation testing is technically possible but rarely sought for a mild condition.
  • Secondary prevention: early diagnosis to start footwear and skin-care habits.
  • Tertiary prevention: reduce friction (occupational adjustments, padded gloves and footwear) and treat fissures to prevent infection.
  • Immunization, prophylaxis, public health measures: none applicable.

14. Other species and natural disease

  • Naturally occurring disease: no DSG1-associated PPK in animals was found. OMIA was not queried in this session.
  • Dsg1 orthologs: conserved in mammals. Mice have three Dsg1 paralogs (Dsg1a/b/c), which makes modelling harder.
  • Related animal disease: canine pemphigus foliaceus involves autoantibodies to Dsc1 and Dsg1. This is acquired autoimmune disease, not a genetic model.
  • Zoonotic or transmission questions: not applicable.

Limitations of current models: - Mice lack the thick, friction-adapted palmoplantar epidermis in its human form. - Murine footpads differ from human soles. - There is no heterozygous model with a striate phenotype. This is a HUMAN_MODEL_MISMATCH-type gap.

Key gaps

  1. No prevalence data.
  2. No quality-of-life measurement.
  3. No treatment trials.
  4. No haploinsufficient animal model.
  5. No omics data from patient palmoplantar skin.
  6. No clear explanation for why the same allele gives striate, focal or diffuse disease (candidate explanations: modifier genes, mechanical load).
  7. The mild homozygous splice family (2025) conflicts with the simple "biallelic = SAM" rule and needs functional study.
