Cepacia Syndrome

1. Disease Information

2026-11-26
Claude Code MONDO:0958151 Model: claude-haiku-5-5, claude-opus-4-8, claude-opus-5-5 36 citations Prompt: disease_pathophysiology_research.md f452f0a9c7e3

1. Disease Information

Overview. Cepacia syndrome is a rapidly progressive, usually fatal necrotizing pneumonia with bacteremia and septic deterioration occurring in a person (classically with cystic fibrosis) who is infected/colonized with the Burkholderia cepacia complex. The 2015 case report by Hauser & Orsini defines it as "a fatal combination of necrotizing pneumonia, rapid respiratory decline, and bacteremia," classically tied to CF patients (Hauser & Orsini 2015, Case Rep Infect Dis; PMID 26357579, PMCID PMC4555446, doi 10.1155/2015/537627 — PMC4555446). The 2023 systematic review similarly frames it as "the fatal combination of necrotizing pneumonia, worsening respiratory failure, and bacteremia, known as Cepacia syndrome" (Daccò et al. 2023, Pediatric Pulmonology; doi 10.1002/ppul.26359; PMID 36815622 per read.qxmd record 36815622; retrieved via that review and the unimi full-text PDF).

The term originates from the Toronto (Hospital for Sick Children) experience and the foundational observation by Isles et al. 1984, "Pseudomonas cepacia infection in cystic fibrosis: an emerging problem" (J Pediatr 104(2):206–210; PMID 6420530; doi 10.1016/s0022-3476(84)80993-2 — PubMed 6420530), which documented rising "P. cepacia" (now Bcc) colonization in a CF cohort and a subset of patients with fulminant, fatal deterioration.

Key identifiers. - MONDO: MONDO:0958151 - Disease Ontology: DOID:0070528 (synonym "CS") - MedGen: C4025673 - OMIM: Not applicable (no Mendelian entry — infectious syndrome). - Orphanet / ICD-10 / ICD-11 / MeSH: I did not retrieve a dedicated Orphanet, ICD-10, ICD-11, or MeSH code specifically for "cepacia syndrome" in this session. Related MeSH headings that do exist are "Burkholderia Infections" and "Burkholderia cepacia complex"; ICD-10 coding in practice uses the pneumonia/sepsis plus organism codes (B96.-/J15.-) — not independently verified in-session, flag as unconfirmed. - Causative organism taxonomy (NCBI Taxonomy): Burkholderia cepacia complex (txid87882); Burkholderia cenocepacia (txid95486); Burkholderia cepacia (txid292) — species confirmed in the taxonomy literature but exact taxids not individually re-verified in-session.

Synonyms / alternative names. "CS" (Disease Ontology); historically "Pseudomonas cepacia infection"/"fulminant Pseudomonas cepacia pneumonia" before the genus was reclassified to Burkholderia (1992) and before Bcc was split into genomovars/species.

Data derivation. Disease-level, aggregated from individual patient case reports and small case series synthesized in a systematic review — i.e., primarily published clinical cases rather than a structured EHR cohort or registry-level syndrome surveillance.

2. Etiology

Primary cause (infectious). Infection with the Burkholderia cepacia complex, a group of >20 closely related Gram-negative β-proteobacterial species (PMC7057466; Vandamme et al. 1997, PMID 9336927, PubMed 9336927; Mahenthiralingam, Urban & Goldberg 2005, Nat Rev Microbiol 3(2):144–156, doi 10.1038/nrmicro1085). The complex was originally resolved into ≥5 genomic species ("genomovars") by Vandamme et al.; the medically dominant species in CF are B. cenocepacia (genomovar III) and B. multivorans (formerly genomovar II). B. cenocepacia is the species most strongly linked to the worst outcomes (see §9, §11).

The syndrome is a host–pathogen event, not pure infection: it represents transition from chronic airway colonization to invasive, bacteremic, hyperinflammatory disease. The dominant risk substrate is cystic fibrosis (impaired CFTR-dependent mucociliary clearance and airway defense); rare non-CF cases occur in immunocompromised/structurally abnormal lungs (COPD, chronic granulomatous disease, post-pneumonia, nursing-home/nosocomial exposure) (PMC4555446; PMC8208758; PMC3780462; Cureus 2024, PMC10894023).

Risk factors

Host genetic. - CFTR mutations (cystic fibrosis) — the overwhelming predisposing background. (CFTR = HGNC:1884; OMIM gene 602421; disease CF OMIM 219700.) Bcc is a CF-specific opportunist because of the CF airway environment. - CGD genes (e.g., CYBB/NADPH-oxidase genes) — chronic granulomatous disease is a recognized non-CF setting for severe Bcc infection and MAS/HLH-type deterioration ("'cepacia syndrome'" referenced in a CGD MAS/HLH case). Specific gene-level confirmation not retrieved in-session beyond the CGD association.

Host/clinical (non-genetic). - Chronic Bcc airway colonization preceding the fulminant event. - Advanced/declining lung function and poor nutritional status (classic CF prognostic axes). Exact FEV₁ thresholds specific to cepacia-syndrome progression were not retrievable in-session and are flagged as not established. - Prior lung transplantation or transplant candidacy in the context of B. cenocepacia (post-transplant mortality markedly elevated — see §11). - Nosocomial / contaminated-product exposure (Bcc contaminates aqueous pharmaceuticals, disinfectants, nebulizers, mouthwash; CDC lists Bcc as a notable healthcare-associated waterborne opportunist — CDC B. cepacia).

Pathogen/microbial "risk" determinants (acting like virulence-associated loci): - B. cenocepacia species identity, particularly the ET12 epidemic lineage (genomovar IIIA; reference strain J2315), associated with transmissibility and poor outcome. - Cable pili (cblA) and the 22-kDa adhesin — associated with epithelial binding/transmigration and with the epidemic-strain marker, historically correlated with transmissibility and adverse outcome (PMC419935; PMC1231069), though later shown not strictly required for epidemic spread (PHDC clone lacks cblA/marker; PMC1196026).

Protective factors

  • Cohort segregation / strict infection control has reduced patient-to-patient acquisition of epidemic Bcc strains — the principal "protective" intervention at the population level (Speert et al. 2002, Emerg Infect Dis — CDC EID 8(2); Australian segregation data cited therein). Sporadic acquisition still occurs despite segregation.
  • Genetic protective variants: Not applicable / none identified (infectious syndrome). gnomAD-type protective-allele analysis is not meaningful here.

Gene–environment interactions

The core interaction is host CFTR loss-of-function × environmental Bcc exposure: CFTR-deficient airways provide a niche permitting Bcc persistence, and CF macrophages mount an exaggerated, dysregulated inflammatory response to B. cenocepacia (in vitro: amplified IL-1β, IL-10, MCP-1, IFN-γ and increased cell death — see §6; e.g., CF-macrophage studies and the pyrin/TecA axis, PMC8546862). A second interaction axis is CGD (NADPH-oxidase) host defect × Bcc, where failure of the oxidative burst permits Bcc survival.

4. Genetic / Molecular Information

Human causal genes: Not applicable (no human disease gene; infectious syndrome). Host-background genes relevant to susceptibility: CFTR (HGNC:1884; OMIM 602421) and CGD genes (e.g., CYBB). No ACMG/AMP variant classification, gnomAD allele frequencies, somatic/germline distinction, modifier-gene, epigenetic, or chromosomal-abnormality analysis applies to the syndrome itself.

Pathogen (microbial) molecular determinants (the meaningful analogue): - Species/lineage genotype: B. cenocepacia genomovar III, especially ET12 lineage (strain J2315); genome sequenced (Holden et al.; Cardiff/ORCA J2315 2009) — a multireplicon genome (multiple chromosomes + plasmid), a hallmark of the complex (Mahenthiralingam, Urban & Goldberg 2005, doi 10.1038/nrmicro1085). - cblA (cable pilin subunit) + 22-kDa adhesin — adhesion/transmigration (PMC1231069; PMC419935). - B. cepacia epidemic strain marker (BCESM) — epidemiologic marker (not strictly required; PMC1196026). - tecA — Type VI secretion system (T6SS) effector; deamidates Rho GTPases → pyrin inflammasome activation and virulence (see §6; PMC3482472; PMC8546862). - Intrinsic multidrug-resistance determinants (efflux pumps, inducible β-lactamases, LPS modifications) underlie the near-untreatability (see §12).

5. Environmental Information

  • Environmental reservoir/exposure: Bcc is a ubiquitous environmental organism (soil, water, rhizosphere; some strains are agricultural biocontrol/bioremediation agents) and a notorious contaminant of aqueous medical products, disinfectants, nebulizers, and mouthwash, driving healthcare-associated outbreaks (CDC).
  • Lifestyle factors: No established lifestyle risk factors beyond those general to CF lung health; the key modifiable exposure is contact with other Bcc-colonized patients and contaminated equipment (hence segregation).
  • Infectious agent (definitive): Burkholderia cepacia complex — Gram-negative bacilli; NCBI Taxonomy Burkholderia cepacia complex; principal species B. cenocepacia, B. multivorans (Vandamme et al. 1997, PMID 9336927; classification review Vanlaere/Mahenthiralingam, PMID 21257278, PubMed 21257278).

6. Mechanism / Pathophysiology

Ordered causal chain (initiating lesion → clinical manifestation)

  1. Host predisposition — biallelic CFTR loss of function (or NADPH-oxidase defect in CGD) → impaired mucociliary clearance and defective airway/phagocyte killing → permissive niche for Bcc. (Well established for CF susceptibility; direct CFTR-dependence of cepacia-syndrome transition is inferred.)
  2. Acquisition & chronic colonization of the CF airway by Bcc (often B. cenocepacia), aided by cable pili/cblA + 22-kDa adhesin → epithelial adhesion and transmigration across respiratory epithelium (PMC1231069). (Demonstrated in vitro.)
  3. Intracellular survival — Bcc is taken up by macrophages/epithelial cells and survives within membrane-bound vacuoles resembling arrested autophagosomes, delaying phagosome maturation (PMC3405007). (Demonstrated in macrophage/zebrafish models.)
  4. T6SS effector TecA delivery → deamidation/inactivation of Rho GTPases (RhoA Asn41) → cortical actin depolymerization and WASH-mediated actin dysregulation further delaying phagosome maturation (Aubert et al.; PMC3482472). (Demonstrated in vitro/mouse.)
  5. Pyrin inflammasome activation — RhoA inactivation is sensed by pyrin, triggering inflammasome assembly → caspase-1 activation → IL-1β / IL-18 maturation and release (PMC3482472). Branch: in mouse lung infection, TecA worsens weight loss and lethality even when pyrin is dispensable, indicating additional pyrin-independent inflammatory death pathways (PMC8546862; pyrin-independent macrophage death, bioRxiv 2023). (Animal/in vitro.)
  6. Exaggerated CF-macrophage cytokine response — CF macrophages produce excess IL-1β, IL-10, MCP-1, IFN-γ and undergo increased cell death on B. cenocepacia infection (in vitro), amplifying inflammation. (In vitro.)
  7. Breach into bloodstream → bacteremia and systemic inflammatory response → cytokine-storm-like hyperinflammation ("Cepacia syndrome" has been framed as a cytokine storm / inflammasome-mediated event; one IL-18–high patient responded to anakinra + corticosteroids, though cepacia-syndrome labeling of that case is unconfirmed).
  8. Necrotizing pneumonia + diffuse alveolar damage → ARDS → refractory septic shock and multiorgan dysfunction → death. (Clinical, from case reports.)

Upstream vs downstream: Host CFTR defect and bacterial adhesion/intracellular persistence are upstream; Rho-GTPase inactivation → pyrin/inflammasome → IL-1β/IL-18 are midstream drivers; bacteremia, cytokine storm, necrotizing pneumonia, ARDS and shock are downstream manifestations.

Cell types & processes involved: alveolar macrophages (CL:0000235) and monocyte-derived macrophages (CL:0000235), neutrophils (CL:0000775), respiratory epithelial cells (CL:0000082). Processes: inflammasome assembly (GO:0097169 AIM2/ pyrin inflammasome complex; GO:0141163 regulation of inflammasome-mediated signaling), IL-1β production (GO:0032611), defense response to bacterium (GO:0042742), phagocytosis/phagosome maturation (GO:0006909; GO:0090382), Rho protein signal transduction (GO:0007266), actin cytoskeleton organization (GO:0030036), autophagy (GO:0006914).

Molecular pathways: Rho GTPase signaling; pyrin inflammasome → caspase-1 → IL-1β/IL-18; NF-κB-driven cytokine output. Protein dysfunction: bacterial TecA enzymatically inactivates host RhoA (gain-of-toxic-function effector). Immune involvement: both immunodeficiency substrate (CFTR/CGD) and pathological hyperinflammation. Tissue damage: necrosis, diffuse alveolar damage, neutrophil-mediated injury.

Molecular profiling (transcriptomics/proteomics/metabolomics/single-cell/spatial): No cepacia-syndrome-specific multi-omic human datasets were retrievable in-session; mechanistic data are largely targeted in vitro/animal studies. Flagged as not available.

7. Anatomical Structures Affected

  • Primary organ: Lung (UBERON:0002048) — necrotizing pneumonia, cavitation, diffuse alveolar damage; lower respiratory tract (UBERON:0001558).
  • Secondary/systemic: Blood (UBERON:0000178) — bacteremia; multiorgan dysfunction in terminal sepsis (kidney, liver, circulatory system).
  • Body systems: Respiratory (primary); cardiovascular/hematologic/immune (secondary via sepsis).
  • Tissues/cells: respiratory epithelium (UBERON:0000483), alveolar spaces; cell populations: alveolar macrophage (CL:0000583), macrophage (CL:0000235), neutrophil (CL:0000775), respiratory epithelial cell (CL:0000082).
  • Subcellular (GO Cellular Component): phagocytic vesicle / phagosome (GO:0045335), autophagosome (GO:0005776), inflammasome complex (GO:0061702), actin cytoskeleton (GO:0015629).
  • Localization/lateralization: Pulmonary involvement is typically bilateral/diffuse in the fulminant phase; cavitary/necrotic foci may be focal early. (From case radiology; HPO HP:0002108 pulmonary cavitation where applicable.)

8. Temporal Development

  • Onset: Typically a superimposed acute decompensation on chronic CF lung disease in adolescents/young adults; acute-to-fulminant pattern. Non-CF cases: subacute hospital/COPD course tipping into fulminant pneumonia+sepsis.
  • Progression: Rapid — days to a few weeks from onset to respiratory failure/death; "nearly always fatal, with only very few cases reported of successful treatment, all in CF patients" (2023 review discussion, via retrieved summary).
  • Disease course: Monophasic, progressive, usually terminal; no relapsing-remitting phase — it is an end-stage event.
  • Remission: Rare treatment-induced survival reported; no spontaneous remission.
  • Critical period / window of intervention: Early recognition of Bcc bacteremia + clinical decline in a CF patient is the only intervention window; prevention (segregation, avoiding B. cenocepacia acquisition) is more effective than rescue.

9. Inheritance and Population

Inheritance: Not applicable (infectious syndrome — no Mendelian inheritance, penetrance, expressivity, anticipation, mosaicism, founder effect, consanguinity, or carrier frequency). Host CF background is autosomal recessive (CFTR), but that is CF inheritance, not cepacia syndrome.

Epidemiology (of the underlying Bcc infection; syndrome-specific rates are poorly defined): - Bcc prevalence among CF patients is regionally variable — e.g., in Canada 5% (Quebec) to 22% (Ontario) in the Speert et al. survey (CDC EID 8(2), 2002); US CF Foundation registry historically ~2–3% (exact current figure not retrieved in-session; CF Foundation registry is the authoritative source — CFF Bcc page). - Species mix: In a UK CF study, B. multivorans was most prevalent (~56%); B. cenocepacia historically dominant in epidemic contexts. Trend: overall Bcc prevalence/incidence has declined with segregation and improved infection control. - Proportion progressing to cepacia syndrome: reported as roughly ~10–20% of Bcc-infected CF patients in older descriptions (Toronto ~20%; one 2024 case report cites ~10% progression with ~75% mortality) — estimates conflict and the true incidence is "largely unknown" per the 2023 review. - Mortality: Very high; the 2023 review states the outcome "is negative in 50% of the patients" for the collated case set, while describing the syndrome as "nearly always fatal" historically; individual series report up to ~75%. - Sex ratio / age distribution: No reliable syndrome-specific figures retrieved; CF demographics apply (adolescents/young adults). - Geographic distribution of strains: ET12 (B. cenocepacia IIIA) epidemic clone originated in the UK/Canada; PHDC clone in the mid-Atlantic US — strain distribution is geographically structured (PMC5845691; PMC1196026).

10. Diagnostics

Diagnosis is clinical + microbiological, in a compatible host: - Microbiology (core): Sputum/respiratory and blood cultures growing Bcc on selective media (e.g., B. cepacia selective agar / OFPBL / BCSA); species-level identification (recA sequencing / MLST) to distinguish B. cenocepacia (prognostically critical). Repeated positive cultures from blood and sterile sites support invasive disease (e.g., blood/synovial/wound isolates in a non-CF case, PubMed 35811086). - Laboratory abnormalities: leukocytosis (e.g., 21,700/mm³), elevated CRP/inflammatory markers; LOINC codes apply (CBC/differential, CRP, blood culture). - Imaging: chest X-ray/CT showing necrotizing/cavitary pneumonia, progressing to diffuse bilateral infiltrates/ARDS (RadLex "cavitation", "consolidation"). - Functional: deterioration of spirometry (FEV₁) in CF; ABG/respiratory failure markers. - Pathology: necrotizing pneumonia with tissue destruction (histopathology where available). - Clinical criteria: No formal consensus criteria; operationally the triad of necrotizing pneumonia + bacteremia + rapid respiratory decline is treated as jointly required (Daccò et al. 2023; Hauser & Orsini 2015). - Differential diagnosis: CF pulmonary exacerbation from P. aeruginosa/S. aureus; other necrotizing pneumonias (S. aureus/PVL, Klebsiella); ARDS/sepsis of other cause; in CGD, invasive fungal infection and MAS/HLH. - Genetic testing: Relevant only to the host substrate — CFTR testing to establish CF; CGD workup (DHR/neutrophil oxidative burst, CYBB etc.) in non-CF severe Bcc cases. Not applicable to the syndrome itself. - Screening: Routine surveillance respiratory cultures in CF clinics to detect Bcc acquisition; molecular typing for outbreak detection; this is screening for the organism, enabling segregation, not for the syndrome.

11. Outcome / Prognosis

  • Mortality: Among the worst of any CF-associated infection. The 2023 review reports a negative outcome in ~50% of collated cases while noting historical descriptions of "nearly always fatal"; some series report mortality up to ~75%. Survival is reported in only "very few" cases, essentially all in CF patients.
  • Lung-transplant outcomes (prognostically decisive): Pre-transplant B. cenocepacia infection is associated with markedly worse survival — e.g., Duke cohort (Alexander et al. 2008, PMID 18318775, PubMed): 1-year survival 29% vs 92% in uninfected CF transplant recipients; other Bcc species did not significantly reduce 5-year survival. A single-center study (2010, PMID 20810293, PubMed) reported mortality high enough that the center ceased listing B. cenocepacia-infected candidates, while non-cenocepacia Bcc had good long-term survival. Some centers (e.g., a Brazilian series, PMID 29538493, PMC5840825) argue Bcc should not be an absolute contraindication.
  • Prognostic factors: Species (B. cenocepacia = worst); presence of bacteremia; baseline lung function/nutrition; transplant status. Prognostic biomarkers specific to the syndrome: not established in retrievable literature.
  • Morbidity / QoL / recovery: Survivors are rare; recovery potential is low once the fulminant phase begins. No validated QoL instrument data for the syndrome retrieved.

12. Treatment

No proven regimen exists; care is empirical and often unsuccessful ("data are too scanty to identify the most effective therapeutic approach" — 2023 review).

Pharmacotherapy (antibacterial; NCIT antibacterial agent terms apply): - Bcc is intrinsically multidrug-resistant (resistant to polymyxins/colistin, aminoglycosides often, many β-lactams), hampering therapy and compounded by biofilm formation. - Most active agents (variable susceptibility): trimethoprim–sulfamethoxazole (co-trimoxazole) — highest susceptibility (~82% in one 91-isolate CF series; often first-line); meropenem, ceftazidime, temocillin, piperacillin–tazobactam (≥50% susceptible in that series); plus minocycline, levofloxacin, chloramphenicol as options (PMC5702217). - Combination therapy is standard practice (e.g., two or three agents such as ceftazidime + meropenem ± a fluoroquinolone/aminoglycoside; moxifloxacin + ceftazidime ± low-dose colistin proposed) because single agents frequently fail and in-vitro synergy is sought; susceptibility testing poorly predicts clinical outcome in Bcc (PMC5702217). - Newer agents (e.g., ceftazidime–avibactam) proposed for resistant strains.

Immunomodulation (given the cytokine-storm framing): Corticosteroids have been used (in vitro, corticosteroids suppressed the exaggerated CF-macrophage cytokine response; IV dexamethasone implicated in a CF + SARS-CoV-2 + Bcc case). IL-1 blockade (anakinra) + corticosteroids benefited an inflammasome-driven (IL-18-high) patient, but a direct, confirmed cepacia-syndrome case treated with anakinra was not retrievable — the evidence is indirect/analogical. Flag as experimental/unproven.

Advanced/experimental: - Bacteriophage therapy is an area of active interest for Bcc given resistance (the 2023 review's "new perspectives in treatment" framing; specific NCT data not retrieved in-session). - Gene/cell/RNA/targeted/immuno-oncology therapies: Not applicable (infectious syndrome). CFTR modulators (e.g., elexacaftor/tezacaftor/ivacaftor) treat the host CF disease and may reduce infection burden over time, but are not treatments for the acute syndrome and were not studied for it in retrieved sources.

Surgical/supportive: ICU-level organ support (mechanical ventilation, vasopressors for shock); lung transplantation is generally contraindicated/deferred in active B. cenocepacia disease.

Treatment outcomes: Response is poor; most reported cases are fatal despite aggressive multi-drug therapy and intensive care.

13. Prevention

Prevention is the most effective strategy and centers on preventing Bcc acquisition: - Primary prevention (infection control / public health): Strict patient segregation / cohorting, hand hygiene, equipment/nebulizer hygiene, avoidance of CF-patient-to-patient contact, and surveillance culturing — credited with reducing epidemic-strain cross-infection (Speert et al. 2002, CDC EID; CDC Bcc). Control of contaminated medical products/water prevents nosocomial Bcc. - Secondary prevention: Routine surveillance respiratory cultures in CF to detect Bcc early and trigger isolation; molecular typing for outbreak control. - Tertiary prevention: Aggressive management of Bcc pulmonary exacerbations to prevent progression to the syndrome; careful transplant candidate selection (species identification). - Immunization: No vaccine exists (none licensed; a Bcc/Burkholderia vaccine remains experimental). - Genetic screening / counseling: Applies to CF (carrier/newborn screening, genetic counseling) as the host condition, not to the syndrome. - Prophylaxis: No established antimicrobial prophylaxis prevents cepacia syndrome.

14. Other Species / Natural Disease

  • Species affected: Cepacia syndrome as a defined entity is human (CF-associated). The pathogen infects a broad host range as an environmental/opportunistic organism and plant-associated bacterium.
  • Veterinary natural disease: No recognized naturally occurring "cepacia syndrome" analog in companion animals/wildlife was retrieved; Bcc is primarily an environmental and nosocomial human opportunist. (OMIA entry not identified in-session.)
  • Comparative biology / orthologous genes: Not applicable in the Mendelian sense; host comparative work uses Cftr-mutant animals (see §15). Evolutionary conservation relevant to mechanism: pyrin/inflammasome and Rho-GTPase signaling are conserved across mammalian hosts, enabling mouse modeling of TecA-driven inflammation.
  • Zoonotic/cross-species transmission: Bcc is not classically zoonotic; transmission relevant to disease is human-to-human (epidemic CF strains) and environmental/product-borne.

15. Model Organisms

Models are of Bcc infection/virulence, not of a human disease gene (so knockout-of-the-disease-gene framing is N/A). Evidence type: animal/invertebrate/cell models.

  • Rat agar-bead chronic lung infection — classic chronic Bcc respiratory model (PMC2849400; ShvR regulator study PMC6301696).
  • Mouse agar-bead model (C57BL/6) — chronic infection; serial lung passage increased an environmental strain's ability to establish chronic infection (PMID 29135920, PubMed). Cftr-deficient / CF-genotype mice used for host-defect studies; notably, TecA exacerbated weight loss and lethality in C57BL/6 and CF-genotype mice, pyrin-independently (PMC8546862). Limitation: Cftr⁻/⁻ mice do not reproduce the spontaneous chronic airway infection seen in human CF.
  • Zebrafish embryo — intracellular replication and innate-immune response; macrophages identified as a critical replication site for B. cenocepacia K56-2 (PMC2849400).
  • Galleria mellonella (wax moth larva) — robust, quantitative virulence screen (PMC2258804).
  • Caenorhabditis elegans — virulence screening; rankings can differ from Galleria (PMC5626231).
  • Macrophage cell models (human/murine, incl. CF macrophages) — pyrin/TecA inflammasome, phagosome maturation arrest, exaggerated CF cytokine output (PMC3482472; PMC3405007).
  • Plant/alfalfa seedling model — alternative virulence assay compared to the rat model (PMC187319).

Phenotype recapitulation: Animal models capture bacterial persistence, intracellular survival, and inflammatory lethality (incl. TecA-driven), but no single model reproduces the full human cepacia-syndrome triad (necrotizing pneumonia + bacteremia + fulminant decline on a CF background). Resources: MGI (mouse), RGD (rat), ZFIN (zebrafish), WormBase (C. elegans).

The full text of the systematic review (Daccò et al. 2023) has now been extracted, confirming and sharpening several figures I had flagged as pending or conflicting. Below is a precise addendum keyed to the report sections, plus two corrections to things I had inferred.

Addendum — confirmed figures from Daccò et al. 2023 (Pediatr Pulmonol 58:1337–1343; doi 10.1002/ppul.26359)

Citation note: the article text itself prints no PMID; the PMID 36815622 I used comes from the indexing record for this exact article, so it stands, but treat the canonical identifier as the DOI.

§1 / §3 — Definition and clinical features (now exact). The review's clinical definition is: "Cepacia syndrome (CS) is an acute, necrotizing pneumonia with elevated mortality rate, characterized by high fever, bacteremia, and rapidly progressive respiratory failure, occurring in patients with cystic fibrosis (CF) infected with Burkholderia cepacia complex (BCC) bacteria." There are no formal diagnostic criteria. Feature frequencies among the 18 pooled cases: - Fever: 18/18 (100%) — "All patients with CS in our review had fever" (high-grade). → strengthens HP:0001945. - Leukocytosis/neutrophilia: 13/18. → HP:0001974. - Elevated CRP: 4/5 measured (reported values incl. 190, 234, 113, 279 mg/L). - Bacteremia: confirmed in 11/13 cultured (84.6%); present in the preterminal phase in 38% of cases. → HP:0031864. - Lung function: ppFEV₁ measured in 10 patients, range 31–101%; both patients with ppFEV₁ <40% died.

§9 — Epidemiology (now exact). 15 articles / 18 cases (9 female), spanning 1998–2021. Median age at onset 22 years (range 10–60). Median time from first BCC infection to CS = 5 years (range 1–26) — i.e., CS is a late event, not an early post-acquisition one. Mortality 50% (9/18), with a sex skew: case-fatality 66.6% in males vs 33.3% in females. Species triggering CS: B. cenocepacia 11, B. multivorans 3, B. vietnamiensis 1, B. ambifaria 1 (remainder labeled generically B. cepacia). Incidence is "largely unknown" and expected to have fallen with segregation; the review cites CF Foundation Patient Registry data showing a ~3% annual decrease in BCC incidence and prevalence over 2006–2012 (this confirms the registry-trend figure I had flagged as unverified).

§2 — Host-genotype risk factor (new, confirmed). Of the 11 patients with CFTR genotype reported, all carried ≥1 F508del allele (8 homozygous) and all had "severe" genotypes — the review explicitly suggests "a possible relationship between CFTR genotype and occurrence of CS." Add CFTR F508del (the canonical class II/severe allele) as the predominant host background.

§11 — Prognosis (new, confirmed). B. cenocepacia, particularly the ET12 (genomovar IIIA) epidemic strain, is associated with a fivefold higher mortality than other strains — a concrete prognostic multiplier for the species/lineage point I made.

§6 — Mechanism (enriched). The review supplies a specific cytokine step: "BCC lipopolysaccharides are known to induce a remarkable release of pro-inflammatory cytokines (IL-6, IL-8, tumor necrosis factor-α) by the respiratory epithelium, four- to eightfold higher than other Gram-negative pathogens," driving a neutrophil-mediated inflammatory response and a cytokine-storm syndrome; the authors propose CS pathophysiology "could potentially be compared to unrecognized MAS/HLH." So step 7 of my causal chain should name LPS → epithelial IL-6/IL-8/TNF-α (4–8× amplified) → neutrophilic cytokine storm, complementing the macrophage pyrin/IL-1β axis. Suggested additions: GO:0032496 (response to lipopolysaccharide), GO:0071222 (cellular response to LPS), CHEBI:16412 (lipopolysaccharide).

Two corrections to my earlier text

  1. §12 — Immunomodulation evidence is stronger than I stated, and is the review's actual "new perspective." Immunosuppressants (oral/IV steroids, tacrolimus, cyclosporin) were given in 7/18 (38.9%) cases, and half of the survivors had received immunomodulation (with antibiotics). Also, all 5 patients given inhaled/nebulized antibiotics survived (27.8%; the authors caution this may reflect less severe disease). Cotrimoxazole was the most common IV agent; one G551D patient also received ivacaftor.
  2. §12 — Retract the implication that bacteriophage therapy was the review's proposed novelty. The Daccò review does not mention phage/bacteriophage therapy or anakinra/IL-1 blockade at all — its "new perspectives in treatment" refers specifically to immunomodulation (steroids/cyclosporin/tacrolimus, on a MAS/HLH analogy). My anakinra/phage mentions came from separate sources and should be labeled strictly as external, indirect, experimental evidence — not as recommendations from this review.

Everything else in the main report stands. The single most load-bearing, now fully confirmed facts for the knowledge-base entry: MONDO:0958151 = acute necrotizing pneumonia + high fever + bacteremia + rapid respiratory failure in CF; caused by Burkholderia cepacia complex (chiefly B. cenocepacia ET12); ~22-year median onset; ~5 years after BCC acquisition; 50% mortality; no proven therapy.
