Lymph Node Tuberculosis

1. Disease Information

2026-11-27
Claude Code MONDO:0005831 Model: claude-haiku-5-5, claude-opus-5-5 42 citations Prompt: disease_pathophysiology_research.md f452f0a9c7e3

1. Disease Information

Overview. TBL is infection of lymph nodes by the M. tuberculosis complex. It is the most common form of extrapulmonary TB (EPTB), at about 35% of EPTB cases, and systemic symptoms are usually not prominent (Handa et al., J Infect Dev Ctries 2012). It most often affects the cervical nodes and is easy to confuse with other granulomatous diseases (Deveci et al. 2016, PMID 28058405, PMC5206468). An African meta-analysis also calls TBL "the most frequent form of extra-pulmonary TB" (Mekonnen et al., PLoS One 2019).

2. Etiology

Causal factors (infectious) - The main cause is M. tuberculosis (NCBITaxon:1773). - M. bovis (NCBITaxon:1765) causes zoonotic cervical lymphadenitis, mainly through raw milk. It colonizes the tonsil or pharynx and spreads to the neck nodes (FAO review). - In Tunisia, 11 cervical TBL isolates were M. bovis, and most patients reported eating raw dairy (Pasteur Tunis). - In rural Tanzania, 16% of cervical adenitis isolates were M. bovis (UK DFID output). - A 2023 Canadian case involved a teenager who drank unpasteurized milk in Turkey (CMAJ 2023).

Environmental and demographic risk factors - Female sex - In a Liverpool cohort, 74% of LNTB cases were women, compared with 43% of pulmonary TB controls. - In a Houston study of HIV-negative adults, female sex had an OR of 2.6 (P<0.01) (Houston Methodist). - Birthplace - Being born in South or Southeast Asia, India, the Eastern Mediterranean or Africa raised risk in the Houston study. - Another cohort reported higher risk in women, people aged 45–64 and people born in Southern Asia. - Age and migration: In a California series (Polesky et al., Medicine 2005, DOI 10.1097/01.md.0000189090.52626.7a), patients were mostly young foreign-born women, about 5 years after arriving in the US (record). - HIV and immunosuppression - Immunosuppression is a recognized EPTB risk factor (BMJ Best Practice summary). - Findings for TBL specifically are mixed: an Indonesian study of 422 patients found no significant association with HIV, but did find associations with age, sex, nutrition, diabetes and BCG status (WHJ). - An Ethiopian study compared diagnostic yield by HIV status (PMID 18713500). - TNF blockade or CD4 depletion: Animal and mechanistic work shows these can reactivate disease in nodes (PMID 32790739; see §6).

Genetic risk factors (susceptibility only, not causal) - Comparative association study (Bose, Giri & Varma-Basil, Genes 2023; PMID 36672948 per BVS record): 63 SNPs in 23 immunity genes were tested. Variants in LTA4H, P2RX7, CD209 (DC-SIGN) and SP110 "showed susceptibility to LNTB and not PTB". Variants in IFNG, IL12, IL4, TNFB (LTA), IL1RN, TLR2 and TLR4 were associated with pulmonary TB but not LNTB (preprint). - IL12RB1 and MBL2: A Han Chinese study reported association of IL12RB1 rs2305740 G (P=0.040) and MBL2 with TB in general, not TBL specifically (PMID 32349793). - Mendelian susceptibility to mycobacterial disease (MSMD): These rare monogenic inborn errors of IFN-γ immunity predispose to mycobacterial disease, including lymphadenitis after BCG. Genes include IFNGR1, IFNGR2, STAT1, JAK1, IRF8, SPPL2A, IL12B, IL12RB1, IL12RB2, IL23R, ISG15, TYK2, RORC, CYBB and NEMO (IKBKG) (Bustamante 2020, PMC7275902). IL12RB1 and IFNGR1 together account for about 80% of MSMD cases (PMC10975294). See also the overview at PMID 40656276. MSMD is a separate entity and a rare cause of TBL.

Protective factors - BCG vaccination was a significant factor in the Indonesian study (direction not shown in the excerpt). - Pasteurizing milk removes the M. bovis route (inferred from the transmission evidence above). - No genetic variants protective against TBL specifically were retrieved.

Gene–environment interaction: No TBL-specific evidence was retrieved. The general model is that inborn IFN-γ/IL-12 defects combined with mycobacterial exposure produce disease, and that HIV or anti-TNF therapy act as acquired equivalents.

4. Genetic/Molecular Information

  • Causal genes: None. This is an infectious disease.
  • Pathogenic variants and ClinVar classification: Not applicable, except for MSMD genes in rare monogenic predisposition (§2).
  • Modifier/susceptibility genes: LTA4H, P2RX7, CD209, SP110 (PMID 36672948).
  • Epigenetic findings, chromosomal abnormalities, somatic variants: None retrieved; not applicable.
  • Pathogen genetics: Rifampicin resistance arises from rpoB mutations and is detected by Xpert (§10).

5. Environmental Information

  • Infectious agents: M. tuberculosis complex (NCBITaxon:77643), specifically M. tuberculosis and M. bovis.
  • Exposures: Raw or unpasteurized dairy for M. bovis. Pastoral communities in Afar, Ethiopia had little knowledge of this route (PMC3062609).
  • Social exposures: Birth in or migration from high-burden countries (§2).
  • Lifestyle factors (smoking, alcohol, diabetes) are established for TB in general. Diabetes was associated with TBL in the Indonesian study.

6. Mechanism / Pathophysiology

Causal chain 1. Exposure. Inhaled M. tuberculosis reaches the alveoli, or ingested M. bovis reaches the tonsil or pharyngeal mucosa. This leads to infection of resident macrophages and dendritic cells. (FAO review; PMID 32790739.) 2. Transport to nodes. Infected dendritic cells carry bacilli and antigen through afferent lymphatics to the draining nodes (hilar/mediastinal, or cervical after oropharyngeal entry). Priming of T cells is delayed until about 9–11 days after infection. In humans this produces the Ghon complex, a lung lesion plus a granuloma in a thoracic node. (Shown in animal models; PMID 32790739.) 3. Granuloma formation. IL-12/IFN-γ–driven Th1 immunity leads to granulomas of epithelioid macrophages, Langhans giant cells and lymphocytes inside the node. (Inferred from the MSMD genetics, PMC7275902.) 4. Persistence. Granulomas disrupt and can replace normal node architecture, and "lymph nodes are generally not able to eliminate the infection". This makes nodes niches for persistence. (Macaque work: Ganchua et al. 2018, PMC6211753; PMID 32790739.) 5. Caseation. Central caseous necrosis develops, leading to fluctuant nodes, cold abscess, and sinus tracts or scrofuloderma. (Human histopathology; PMID 27144076.) 6. Branch A, local disease. Spread from the primary site via lymphatics or blood leads to peripheral (cervical) TBL, often without lung disease (inferred). 7. Branch B, reactivation and spread. HIV, CD4 depletion or TNF neutralization leads to new non-necrotizing granulomas next to old mineralized node granulomas. Bacilli then exit via lymph and the subclavian vein, seeding the lungs and other organs. In macaques, relapse after SIV infection started from both lung and thoracic nodes (PMID 40548727). Infected inflammatory dendritic cells can also spread granulomatous inflammation (PMC4626772). 8. Paradoxical reaction. Treatment releases antigen and restores immunity, which leads to temporary node enlargement or new nodes during or after therapy. This immunopathogenic mechanism is inferred (PMID 23895938).

7. Anatomical Structures Affected

  • Primary site: Lymph nodes (UBERON:0000029), mainly cervical nodes (UBERON:0002429). Supraclavicular, axillary, mediastinal (UBERON:0002524), hilar and mesenteric nodes can also be involved.
  • Secondary sites: Lung (UBERON:0002048), skin (scrofuloderma) and disseminated disease.
  • Body system: Lymphatic/immune system.
  • Cells: Macrophages, dendritic cells and T cells (§6).
  • Subcellular level: Mtb lives in the phagosome (GO:0045335).
  • Laterality: Usually unilateral cervical. Exact proportions were not retrieved.

8. Temporal Development

  • Onset: Insidious and chronic, over weeks to months. Peak age is young to middle-aged adults, and children are also affected.
  • Course: Untreated disease progresses from discrete enlarged nodes to matted nodes, abscess and sinus formation.
  • Paradoxical upgrading reactions
  • Incidence is about 5.7% (4/70) in one study (Henry Ford pilot).
  • After treatment ends, the incidence is 8.6 per 100 person-years (95% CI 5.8–12.7) in HIV-negative Koreans (PMID 23895938).
  • PR is more common in LNTB than in pulmonary TB (PMC10695755).
  • A Karachi nested case-control study found the incidence varies with the definition used (PMC6943110).
  • Remission: Remission follows treatment. Residual nodes are common at the end of therapy (§12).

9. Inheritance and Population

  • Inheritance: Not applicable. TBL is acquired. MSMD genes follow AR or AD inheritance (PMC7275902).
  • Global burden
  • About 10.7 million people fell ill with TB and 1.23 million died in 2024 (WHO Global TB Report 2025; WHO news).
  • 16% of notified cases were extrapulmonary (WHO 2025, notifications).
  • TBL is about 35% of EPTB (Handa 2012). That gives roughly 5–6% of notified TB as TBL, which is my own estimate, not a reported figure.
  • Geography: India accounts for about a quarter of global cases. Africa is covered by Mekonnen 2019.
  • Sex and age: More women than men (§2); young and middle-aged adults.
  • TBL-specific incidence per 100,000: Not retrieved.

10. Diagnostics

  • Fine-needle aspiration (FNA): First-line. It often yields positive cultures, so surgery is rarely needed first in immunocompetent adults (Polesky 2005). Diagnostic pathway: Sivaratnam et al., Int J Mycobacteriol 2020 (PDF).
  • Histopathology: Caseating granulomas, epithelioid cells and Langhans giant cells (PMID 27144076; PMID 23449832). Ziehl–Neelsen acid-fast bacilli staining and culture are also used.
  • Xpert MTB/RIF and Xpert Ultra: Cochrane review, PMID 33448348.
  • In 1,000 people with 100 LNTB cases, Xpert MTB/RIF would give 118 positives, 37 of them false positives.
  • Ultra would detect 70 cases and miss 30.
  • Specificity against culture was 86.2%. Culture is an imperfect reference standard for nodes.
  • Accuracy is also high in children (PMC9369894). Specimen type does not change accuracy (PMC6545759).
  • Immunologic tests: Tuberculin skin test (94% positive) and IGRA.
  • Imaging: Ultrasound and CT. EBUS-TBNA for mediastinal nodes (not retrieved in detail).
  • Genetic testing: Not used for diagnosis. Consider an MSMD gene panel (IL12RB1, IFNGR1, STAT1 and others) for severe, recurrent or BCG-related disease in children.
  • Differential diagnosis: Nontuberculous mycobacterial lymphadenitis, lymphoma, metastatic carcinoma, sarcoidosis, cat-scratch disease, fungal infection, reactive hyperplasia (PMID 28058405).

11. Outcome/Prognosis

  • Survival: TBL alone carries low mortality. No disease-specific mortality figures were retrieved.
  • Response to treatment: In India, nodes resolved by 6 months in 517/551 patients (93.8%) (operational study).
  • Lower cure rates:
  • M. bovis TBL in Tunisia: 55.5% cure after at least 8 months of treatment.
  • A Philippine series: 63.3% cured at 6 months (Acta Med Philipp).
  • Complications: Paradoxical reactions, abscess or sinus formation, scarring, drug resistance and spread of disease.
  • Prognostic factors: HIV status, M. bovis (which is naturally resistant to pyrazinamide; general knowledge, not retrieved here), and drug resistance.

12. Treatment

  • Standard regimen: 2 months of isoniazid, rifampicin, pyrazinamide and ethambutol, then 4 months of isoniazid and rifampicin (2HRZE/4HR).
  • NCIT terms (suggest): isoniazid C584, rifampicin C789, pyrazinamide C766, ethambutol C478.
  • CHEBI terms: isoniazid CHEBI:6030, rifampicin CHEBI:28077, pyrazinamide CHEBI:45285, ethambutol CHEBI:4877.
  • 6 vs 9 months:
  • A Korean randomized trial (46 vs 46 patients; 2HERZ/4HER vs 2HERZ/7HER) found no difference in residual nodes or recurrence (KNU).
  • In the Indian study, extending to 9 months gave no extra benefit.
  • A 4-month ofloxacin-containing regimen was non-inferior to the 6-month thrice-weekly regimen in South India (BMC Infect Dis 2024).
  • A 6-month regimen worked in children (BMJ 1990, PMC1679938).
  • Surgery: Excision or aspiration for abscesses or diagnosis. It is not routinely first-line (Polesky 2005).
  • Paradoxical reaction: Continue treatment, aspirate the node, and consider corticosteroids. Evidence for this was not retrieved.
  • Pharmacogenomics: NAT2 acetylator status affects isoniazid toxicity (general knowledge; not retrieved here).
  • Advanced or gene-based therapies: None.
  • Trials (NCT numbers): None retrieved.

13. Prevention

  • Primary: BCG vaccination, mainly against severe disease in childhood; pasteurizing milk and controlling bovine TB for M. bovis; treating latent TB infection.
  • Secondary: Contact tracing and screening migrants from high-burden countries.
  • Tertiary: Completing treatment under direct observation and drug-susceptibility testing (Xpert detects rifampicin resistance).
  • Public health: Funding for the WHO End TB Strategy was US$5.9 billion against a $22 billion target, and deaths have fallen 29% since 2015 against a milestone of 75% (WHO 2025).
  • Genetic counseling: Only relevant for MSMD families.

14. Other Species / Natural Disease

  • Cattle (Bos taurus, NCBITaxon:9913) get bovine TB from M. bovis. Lesions typically affect the retropharyngeal, bronchial and mediastinal nodes, and the disease is zoonotic through milk (FAO). I did not retrieve detailed data on lymph node lesions in cattle.
  • Other hosts: Wildlife reservoirs (badger, deer) were not retrieved.
  • OMIA: No relevant entry found.

15. Model Organisms

  • Cynomolgus macaques (Macaca fascicularis, NCBITaxon:9541) show the closest match to human disease: caseous granulomas form in thoracic nodes.
  • Nodes are niches for prolonged persistence, and FDG PET avidity tracks live bacteria (Ganchua 2018, PMC6211753).
  • Cynomolgus macaques control node infection later in infection, while rhesus macaques do not.
  • In the SIV-reactivation model, relapse starts from the nodes (PMID 40548727).
  • Mouse: Used to study DC trafficking and T-cell priming in draining nodes (PMID 32790739). Mice do not form human-like caseous granulomas; this is a known limitation.
  • Zebrafish/M. marinum and mouse granuloma models: Used to study how granulomas spread (PMC4626772).
  • MSMD knockout mice (Il12rb1, Ifngr1, Stat1) model inborn susceptibility. Details were not retrieved.
  • Gap noted by reviewers: Studies of vaccines and drugs should measure effects in thoracic nodes, not just the lungs (PMID 32790739).

Main gaps: - PMIDs were not retrieved for several key sources (Handa 2012, Mekonnen 2019, Polesky 2005, Ganchua 2018). - No TBL-specific incidence per 100,000 or QoL data were retrieved. - No TBL-specific omics data were found. - All ontology IDs need checking.
