Fournier Gangrene (MONDO:0043352): Disease Characteristics Report

How this report was scoped: Fournier gangrene (FG) is an acquired infection. It has no causal gene and no OMIM entry, so the gene and inheritance sections say "not applicable" and do not name a gene. PubMed's API could not be queried directly in this session, so all evidence comes from web searches of PubMed, PMC, MeSH, FDA and journal pages. Some PMIDs were confirmed only through search snippets and are flagged where that is the case. Quotes are taken from the abstract or snippet text the searches returned.

1. Disease Information

Overview. - FG is a necrotizing fasciitis of the perineum, the external genitalia and the area around the anus. - MeSH defines it as "an acute necrotic infection of the scrotum, penis, or perineum… characterized by scrotum pain and redness with rapid progression to gangrene and sloughing of tissue" (MeSH D018934). - A 2015 review describes it as "an obliterative endarteritis of the subcutaneous arteries resulting in gangrene of the overlying skin" (PMC4580094). - Jean-Alfred Fournier first described it in 1883 as a sudden, fulminant scrotal gangrene in previously healthy young men, with no identifiable cause (PMC4359426). - Today a source of infection is found in most cases (colorectal, urogenital or skin).

Causal gene: none. This is an infectious, acquired disease.

Synonyms: Fournier's gangrene; necrotizing fasciitis of the perineum/genitalia; synergistic gangrene of the scrotum. Only the first was confirmed in retrieved sources.

Evidence base: The disease-level data are aggregated mainly from: - single-centre retrospective case series; - administrative and claims databases, such as the US Nationwide Inpatient Sample (PMID 19286224) and Medicare-based NIDDK claims analyses (NIDDK UDA 2025); - pharmacovigilance (FAERS) reports.

2. Etiology

Causal factors (infectious). - FG is a synergistic infection by aerobic and anaerobic bacteria. It is polymicrobial in more than 80% of cases, with E. coli, Klebsiella pneumoniae, Bacteroides fragilis and Staphylococcus aureus as common pathogens (PMC9355918; PMC5798415). - Typical portals of entry: - lower urinary tract, about 40% (urethral stricture, indwelling catheters); - colorectal or anorectal, about 30% (fistulas, perirectal abscess, hemorrhoids); - skin of the penis or scrotum, about 30% (5-Minute Emergency Consult). - In infants, reported triggers include omphalitis, strangulated hernia, prematurity, diaper rash, varicella, circumcision and perineal abscess (tp.amegroups).

Risk factors (environmental and clinical). - Diabetes mellitus: present in 20–70% of cases, and the most common comorbidity (PMC10184784). - Other conditions: alcohol use disorder, obesity, immunosuppression, HIV, renal failure and heart disease (PMID 16927060; PMID 38625013). - Age and sex: - Incidence peaks in men aged 50–79, at 3.3/100,000 (PMID 19286224). - Men are affected far more often (1,641 men versus 39 women in the same cohort). - SGLT2 inhibitors (drug exposure): - In August 2018 the FDA required a class-wide warning, after 12 cases that all needed hospitalization and surgery (FDA). - Bersoff-Matcha et al. (Ann Intern Med 2019; PMID 31060053, PMID not directly verified) reported 55 cases up to January 2019, with onset 5 days to 49 months after starting the drug (PubMed 31060053). - A FAERS analysis concluded that FG "may be a class effect of SGLT2 inhibitors" (PMC7368210).

Genetic risk factors: none identified. No GWAS, ClinVar or ClinGen associations were found.

Protective factors: No genetic protective factors are known. Plausible environmental protective measures were not supported by direct primary data in the retrieved literature: - good glycemic control; - perineal hygiene; - prompt treatment of perianal and urethral disease.

Gene–environment interactions: none described.

Onset and course: onset is usually in adulthood (most often 50–79 years), with rare cases in neonates and infants. Disease is acute and progresses rapidly, over hours to days.

Quality-of-life impact (after the acute illness): - 65% of survivors in a German cohort reported worse sexual function (PMID 24034257, attribution inferred). - A multi-institutional study (Langston 2023) found significant drops in general and sexual functional domains (Can J Urol PDF). - A 2026 systematic review reported fecal incontinence, sexual dysfunction, urinary symptoms and psychosocial impairment (search result).

These items are not applicable: - causal genes; - pathogenic variants (ACMG classification, allele frequencies, somatic versus germline origin); - modifier genes; - epigenetic changes; - chromosomal abnormalities.

5. Environmental Information

  • Infectious agents (NCBI Taxonomy IDs are given from standard taxonomy and should be verified):
  • Escherichia coli (562): 46.6% (PMID 25635243) and 72% (PMID 28257985) in two single-centre series.
  • Bacteroides spp. (B. fragilis 817): 24–43.9%.
  • Streptococcus spp.: 36.8%.
  • Also reported: Klebsiella pneumoniae (573), Staphylococcus aureus (1280), Prevotella, Enterococcus, Clostridium and Candida (Unbound Medicine).
  • In the 2024 systematic review, fungal infection raised the risk of death (PMID 38625013).
  • Drugs: SGLT2 inhibitors, e.g. canagliflozin, dapagliflozin and empagliflozin (FDA class labeling).
  • Lifestyle: alcohol use disorder and obesity (PMID 16927060).
  • Iatrogenic or traumatic: urethral instrumentation, catheters, perianal surgery, circumcision.

6. Mechanism / Pathophysiology

Causal chain 1. A breach of the perineal or genital skin, the anorectal mucosa or the urethral mucosa lets mixed aerobic and anaerobic flora into the superficial fascial planes. - Typical breaches are fistula, abscess, stricture, catheter or minor trauma. 2. Host factors (hyperglycemia, immunosuppression, alcohol use) impair neutrophil defense. This allows bacteria to multiply. - The step is inferred from risk-factor associations. It was not shown mechanistically in the retrieved sources. 3. Aerobes and anaerobes act in synergy: - Anaerobes produce collagenase and heparinase, which promote clotting and break down fascia. - Bacteroides inhibits phagocytosis of the aerobes (PMC4580094). 4. Inflammation and edema lead to obliterative endarteritis and thrombosis of the subcutaneous arteries. 5. Ischemia and hypoxia of tissue result in necrosis of fascia and skin. - Hypoxia favors anaerobic growth and gas production. This feedback loop is inferred. 6. Infection spreads along the fascial planes: - Colles fascia → Buck and Dartos fascia (penis and scrotum); - Colles fascia → Scarpa fascia (anterior abdominal wall). - Spread toward the posterior and lateral sides is limited by attachments to the pubic rami, fascia lata and urogenital diaphragm (PMC4580094). - The testes, corpora and urethra are usually spared because they have a separate blood supply. 7. The process branches into two outcomes: - Local: gangrene and sloughing, which leaves large soft-tissue defects. - Systemic: toxins and bacteremia cause sepsis, septic shock and multi-organ failure, and this leads to death. 8. In SGLT2-inhibitor users, glycosuria may encourage genital colonization and infection. - This is a hypothesis; the retrieved sources did not show it directly.

Ontology suggestions - GO terms: - inflammatory response GO:0006954; - neutrophil chemotaxis GO:0030593; - phagocytosis GO:0006909; - blood coagulation GO:0007596; - necrotic cell death GO:0070265; - response to hypoxia GO:0001666; - collagen catabolic process GO:0030574 (bacterial). - CL terms: - neutrophil CL:0000775; - macrophage CL:0000235; - fibroblast CL:0000057; - endothelial cell CL:0000115; - keratinocyte CL:0000312. - No data found: molecular profiling of FG tissue (transcriptomic, proteomic, metabolomic, single-cell, spatial) or functional screens.

  • Secondary spread: anterior abdominal wall and thighs.
  • Systemic involvement: cardiovascular and renal systems, through sepsis.
  • Tissues: subcutaneous connective tissue (UBERON:0002384), fascia (UBERON:0008982) and skin (UBERON:0002097).
  • Usually spared: testes, corpora cavernosa and urethra (PMC4580094).
  • Subcellular level: not applicable. Extracellular matrix (GO:0031012) is the main compartment degraded.
  • Laterality: the disease is often asymmetric. CT typically shows "asymmetric fascial thickening" (PMID 18349455).

8. Temporal Development

  • Onset: acute, in adults (peak age 50–79).
  • In children, about 66% of pediatric cases occur in infants under 3 months (tp.amegroups).
  • Progression:
  • The disease progresses rapidly, over hours.
  • Its usual course runs from cellulitis to fascial necrosis to gangrene and sepsis.
  • No formal staging system exists. Severity is graded with scores (FGSI, Section 10).
  • Remission: happens only with treatment (debridement plus antibiotics). Spontaneous resolution is not described.
  • Critical window: debridement is recommended within 24 hours (PMC9388173). A delay in treatment was linked to higher mortality (PMC9018989).

9. Inheritance and Population

Epidemiology - US Nationwide Inpatient Sample: - Incidence was 1.6/100,000 males. - It peaked at 3.3/100,000 in men aged 50–79. - The highest regional rate was in the South (1.9/100,000). - Cases made up less than 0.02% of hospital admissions (PMID 19286224). - Medicare claims for people aged 65 and over showed 3–4/100,000 per year from 2016 to 2023 (NIDDK 2025). - Pediatric incidence is about 0.8 per 1 million (tp.amegroups). - Sex ratio: about 42:1 male to female (1,641 men vs 39 women) (PMID 19286224). - Genetic inheritance: not applicable. There is no inheritance pattern, penetrance, anticipation, founder effect or carrier frequency.

10. Diagnostics

  • Clinical diagnosis: FG is a clinical diagnosis, confirmed during surgery by necrotic fascia.
  • Imaging:
  • CT shows "asymmetric fascial thickening, subcutaneous emphysema, fluid collections, and abscesses."
  • CT offers "higher specificity… and superior evaluation of disease extent" than plain radiography or ultrasound (PMID 18349455).
  • Gas is not always present.
  • Mimics include necrotic tumors, gas from trauma or iatrogenic causes, and vaginitis emphysematosa (PMC7047600).
  • Ultrasound can show gas in the scrotal wall. No sensitivity data were retrieved.
  • Laboratory tests and scores:
  • LRINEC score (Wong 2004, Crit Care Med 32:1535):
    • built from CRP, white cell count, hemoglobin, sodium, creatinine and glucose;
    • a score of 6 or more suggests necrotizing fasciitis;
    • a score above 8 had a positive predictive value of 93.4% in the derivation cohort.
    • Later validation was weaker: an AUROC of 0.696 in one prospective study, and false negatives with a score of 0 (PMC12925613).
    • No FG-specific validation was retrieved.
  • FGSI (Laor 1995):
    • nine physiologic and laboratory parameters;
    • in the original series, mortality was about 75% above a score of 9 versus about 22% below (PMC6732100).
    • Corcoran 2008 (68 patients): a cutoff of 9 gave sensitivity 71.4% and specificity 90%, with mortality 46% versus 4% (LVHN).
  • Simplified and Uludag scores: the 2023 meta-analysis found higher admission scores in non-survivors. Mean differences were 5.53 for FGSI, 2.41 for the simplified FGSI and 5.47 for the Uludag FGSI (J Pers Med 2023; PMC10532663).
  • Cultures: wound and blood cultures guide de-escalation of antibiotics (PMID 24973422).
  • Histopathology: fascial necrosis with thrombosis of the subcutaneous vessels (endarteritis obliterans).
  • Genetic, omics-based and screening tests: not applicable.
  • Differential diagnosis:
  • cellulitis or erysipelas;
  • scrotal abscess;
  • epididymo-orchitis;
  • testicular torsion;
  • strangulated hernia;
  • necrotic tumors;
  • pyoderma gangrenosum;
  • non-infectious gas in tissue (trauma or iatrogenic).

11. Outcome / Prognosis

Mortality - US population data: - In-hospital case fatality was 7.5% in the NIS cohort. - Referral-centre series report 20–40% (PMID 19286224). - 2024 systematic review (57 studies, 3,646 patients): - Pooled mortality was 20.41%. - Mortality was highest in HIV-positive patients (54.17%). - The authors concluded that female sex, comorbidities, anatomic extent, sepsis and fungal infection increase mortality (PMID 38625013). - 2020 meta-analysis (38 studies): diabetes, heart disease and renal failure were linked to death (PMID 31962181). - Its abstract gives a risk ratio for diabetes of 0.72, which contradicts the text. Check the full text before using it. - Women: their mortality is reported to be higher, possibly because of atypical signs and delayed presentation (PMC10184784). - Children: one series of 12 neonates and infants had no deaths (PMC4359426). - Long-term: mortality ranged from 5% to 21% over follow-up of 60 days to 11 years (2026 systematic review).

Morbidity and complications - multiple debridements; - large tissue defects needing skin grafts or flaps; - fecal diversion; - sexual and urinary dysfunction, incontinence and psychological effects.

Prognostic factors - FGSI or Uludag score above threshold; - sepsis; - greater extent of disease; - delay to surgery; - older age; - comorbidity.

Stopping the SGLT2 inhibitor — FDA advice

Negative-pressure wound therapy (NPWT) - It is recommended after full necrosectomy by the WSES and SIS-E guidelines (PMC9604683). - In a 36-patient study it meant fewer operations and faster healing (PMID 36684293). - In a 2025 cohort it shortened hospital stay but did not change mortality (PMC12771018).

Hyperbaric oxygen (HBOT) - A 2022 meta-analysis of observational studies linked it to lower mortality but did not confirm shorter stays or fewer debridements (PMC9388173; PMC9388185). - A 23-patient series found no survival benefit (PMC9018989). - Its benefit remains debated.

Not applicable: pharmacogenomics, gene therapy, cell therapy, RNA-based therapy, targeted therapy and immunotherapy.

Clinical trials: no FG-specific NCT trials were retrieved.

Treatment algorithm: 1. Recognize the condition and resuscitate. 2. Start empiric broad-spectrum antibiotics. 3. Debride urgently, and re-look every 24–48 hours. 4. Divert stool or urine as needed. 5. Manage the wound with NPWT, with HBOT as an optional adjunct. 6. Reconstruct.

13. Prevention

  • Primary:
  • control diabetes;
  • limit alcohol;
  • treat perianal and urethral disease early;
  • care for catheters properly;
  • use SGLT2 inhibitors cautiously and counsel patients on genital symptoms (FDA).
  • The effect of these measures on FG rates has not been quantified.
  • Secondary: early recognition (pain out of proportion to findings) and early CT. LRINEC, FGSI and Uludag scores help with triage.
  • Tertiary: prevent recurrence and complications through wound care, rehabilitation and psychosocial support (Urol Int 2025 / bvsalud, attribution uncertain).
  • Not applicable: vaccines, genetic screening and genetic counseling.

14. Other Species / Natural Disease

  • Cattle: necrotizing fasciitis of the scrotum and perineum was reported in a Hereford bull (Bos taurus, NCBITaxon:9913). It was caused by Arcanobacterium haemolyticum, and the bull was euthanized (NZ Vet J 2010; bvsalud mdl-20927177).
  • Dogs (Canis familiaris, NCBITaxon:9615): necrotizing fasciitis occurs, including polymicrobial cases (PMC8024095). Mortality is reported as 100% without timely debridement (Today's Vet Practice). No scrotal-specific canine cases were retrieved.
  • Not applicable: OMIA entry, orthologous genes and breed predisposition.
  • Zoonotic potential: none (the disease arises from the patient's own flora).

15. Model Organisms

  • No FG-specific model: no polymicrobial perineal FG model was found.
  • Related necrotizing soft-tissue infection models:
  • Mouse subcutaneous Streptococcus pyogenes necrotizing skin and fascial infection (JoVE). It shows necrosis along the fascial planes at 24–48 hours.
  • A mouse study found ibuprofen worsens S. pyogenes soft-tissue infection (PMID 21697021).
  • A fecal-slurry wound model in immunosuppressed mice is the closest polymicrobial analogue. It is a chronic wound model rather than necrotizing fasciitis (PMC7359036).
  • A 2025 review covers inoculation, trauma, immunosuppression, genetically engineered and imaging-based models (Dovepress JIR).
  • Limitations of these models:
  • single-organism inoculum;
  • sites other than the perineum;
  • no diabetic or SGLT2-inhibitor background;
  • differences between rodent and human fascial anatomy.
  • Not found: genetic models (MGI, IMPC).
