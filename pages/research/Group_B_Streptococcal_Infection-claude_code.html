Group B Streptococcal Infection

1. Disease Information

2026-11-26

1. Disease Information

Overview. Group B Streptococcal infection is disease caused by Streptococcus agalactiae (Lancefield group B Streptococcus, GBS), a beta-hemolytic, encapsulated, Gram-positive coccus that colonizes the human gastrointestinal and genitourinary tracts as a commensal and can become an invasive pathogen. It is the leading cause of neonatal sepsis and meningitis and an increasingly important cause of invasive disease in non-pregnant adults; it also causes maternal infection, stillbirth, and preterm birth (Seale 2017, Clin Infect Dis; Gonçalves 2022, Lancet Glob Health). S. agalactiae is also a major pathogen of cattle (mastitis) and farmed fish.

Clinical syndromes are conventionally divided into: - Early-onset disease (EOD): day 0–6 of life, acquired vertically around birth; presents as respiratory distress progressing to septicemia/pneumonia, less often meningitis. - Late-onset disease (LOD): day 7–89; presents as sepsis, more often progressing to meningitis (Frontiers in Microbiology 2022 review, PMC-hosted; "Neonatal Group B Streptococcus Disease," PMID 38296778). - Maternal/pregnancy-associated disease: colonization, chorioamnionitis, bacteremia, UTI, endometritis, and associated stillbirth/preterm birth. - Adult non-pregnancy-associated invasive disease: bacteremia, skin/soft-tissue infection, osteomyelitis/septic arthritis, pneumonia, endocarditis — predominantly in older adults and those with diabetes/obesity (Francois Watkins 2019, JAMA Intern Med, PMC6450309).

2. Etiology

Primary cause (infectious). The sole necessary cause is infection with Streptococcus agalactiae. NCBI Taxonomy ID for the organism is txid1311 (S. agalactiae); not re-verified against NCBI Taxonomy this session. Disease arises when the commensal organism breaches mucosal barriers — vertically (ascending from maternal vaginal colonization, or during passage through the birth canal) in neonates, or via mucosal/skin translocation in adults.

Risk factors — host/environmental: - Maternal GBS colonization is the principal upstream risk factor for EOD; worldwide maternal rectovaginal colonization is ~18% (95% CI 17–19%), with regional range 11–35% (Russell 2017, Clin Infect Dis Suppl, PMC5848259). - Neonatal: preterm birth/low birth weight, prolonged rupture of membranes, intrapartum fever/chorioamnionitis, heavy maternal colonization, prior infant with GBS disease, young maternal age, Black race (higher incidence in US surveillance). - Adult: diabetes mellitus and obesity are the dominant comorbidities — among US non-pregnant adult cases, obesity (53.9%) and diabetes (53.4%) were most common; a VA cohort found ~4-fold higher incidence with poor glycemic control vs good control (Francois Watkins 2019, JAMA Intern Med). Advanced age, cirrhosis, malignancy, and immunosuppression also raise risk.

Genetic (host) susceptibility factors. Host genetic susceptibility to GBS is biologically plausible (e.g., maternal antibody levels, innate-immune receptor variation) but I did not retrieve primary GWAS/ClinVar evidence for specific human susceptibility loci this session. This subsection is unverified/not available and should not be populated from memory.

Pathogen genetic factors (the operative "genetics" here): serotype (capsular polysaccharide type), clonal complex/sequence type (notably hypervirulent CC17/ST-17), and specific virulence genes (see Section 4).

Protective factors: - Maternal type-specific anti-capsular IgG transferred transplacentally protects the newborn — the biological basis of maternal vaccination (Madhi 2023, NEJM GBS6 trial, PMID 37467497). - Intrapartum antibiotic prophylaxis (IAP) and universal antenatal screening are the proven preventive interventions for EOD (see Sections 12–13) (CDC 2010 MMWR guidelines). - No validated human genetic protective variant was retrieved.

Gene–environment interactions. A notable host-flora interaction: in a murine model, GBS alone did not establish vaginal colonization/ascending infection, but co-inoculation with Gardnerella vaginalis was required — implicating the vaginal microbiome as a modifier of GBS pathogenesis (PMC8555936). Human GxE data not retrieved.

4. Genetic / Molecular Information

Human causal genes: Not applicable (infectious disease; no human causal gene — consistent with template "not recorded").

Pathogen genetic/molecular determinants (the operative content):

  • Capsular polysaccharide (CPS) serotypes. Ten serotypes (Ia, Ib, II–IX); serotypes I–V account for ~98% of colonizing isolates, and serotype III accounts for ~25% of colonizing isolates and is disproportionately associated with invasive disease (Russell 2017, PMC5848259). In US late-onset disease, serotype III was most common (56.2%) and rising (Nanduri 2019, JAMA Pediatr, PMID 30640366). Six serotypes caused 99.3% of EOD and 99.7% of LOD (ibid.). The CPS is synthesized by the cps gene cluster.

  • Hypervirulent clone CC17/ST-17 carries the surface adhesin HvgA (hypervirulent GBS adhesin), present only in ST-17; ST-17 accounted for >80% of neonatal meningitis isolates in a French collection. HvgA mediates binding to intestinal, choroid plexus, and blood–brain-barrier endothelial cells and is required for gut colonization and meningeal crossing in neonatal mice (Tazi 2010, J Exp Med, PMID 20956545).

  • β-hemolysin/cytolysin = granadaene pigment. The hemolytic/cytolytic activity is due to the ornithine-rhamnolipid pigment granadaene, produced by the cyl operon (cylX–K), not a protein pore-forming toxin; it enables penetration of human placenta (Whidbey 2013, J Exp Med, PMID 23712433).

  • Sialylated capsule / molecular mimicry. The CPS terminal Siaα2-3Galβ1-4GlcNAc mimics host sialoglycans and engages neutrophil Siglec-9 to dampen oxidative burst, protease release, and NET formation (Carlin 2009, Blood, PMC2665898).

  • Other virulence genes retrieved: β protein engages Siglec-5 (Sia-independent) (related 2009 J Exp Med, PMC2722167); hyaluronidase promotes ascending infection/preterm birth; npx is required for invading reproductive tissue and driving PPROM/preterm birth in mice (PMID 36409087, PMC9765263).

  • Antimicrobial-resistance determinants (pathogen): reduced β-lactam susceptibility is associated with PBP2x point mutations (e.g., V405A, Q557E), with additional PBP2b/PBP1a substitutions at higher MICs (US surveillance 1998–2018, PMID 33553474). Macrolide/lincosamide resistance is conferred by erm (MLS_B phenotype, constitutive or inducible) and mef (M phenotype, macrolide-only) (four-state ABCs surveillance; West Virginia 2025 genomic study, PMC12698558).

Human epigenetics / chromosomal abnormalities: Not applicable / not retrieved.

5. Environmental Information

  • Infectious agent (CHEBI/organism): Streptococcus agalactiae, NCBI Taxon txid1311 (provisional, not re-verified). It is the obligatory etiologic agent.
  • Environmental/behavioral factors: Maternal colonization status is the key perinatal "exposure." Adult disease is driven by host metabolic comorbidity (diabetes, obesity) rather than toxins/occupational exposures (Francois Watkins 2019). No toxin/radiation/pollution associations retrieved (not expected for this disease).
  • Vaginal microbiome: Gardnerella vaginalis co-colonization facilitated GBS vaginal colonization and ascension in a mouse model (PMC8555936).

6. Mechanism / Pathophysiology

Causal chain (neonatal early-onset disease)

  1. Maternal rectovaginal colonization by S. agalactiae (commensal state; ~18% of pregnant women) → leads to a reservoir for vertical transmission (Russell 2017).
  2. Ascending infection and/or intrapartum exposure: GBS ascends the genital tract (facilitated by hyaluronidase and the granadaene/β-H/C toxin; npx required for tissue invasion) → results in invasion of amniotic/placental tissue and fetal exposure (Whidbey 2013, PMID 23712433; Randis ascending model, PMID 24474814; npx PMID 36409087).
  3. Granadaene-mediated cytolysis of placental and epithelial barriers → allows bacterial penetration of the human placenta and fetal membranes (branch point: can cause PPROM, preterm birth, stillbirth) (Whidbey 2013).
  4. Neonatal mucosal colonization (aspiration of infected vaginal secretions seeds respiratory/GI mucosa) → leads to mucosal translocation into the bloodstream (Frontiers 2022 review).
  5. Immune evasion in blood: sialylated capsule engages Siglec-9/-5, blunting neutrophil oxidative burst, degranulation, and NETs → permits bacterial survival and bacteremia/sepsis (Carlin 2009, PMC2665898).
  6. Branch — meningeal tropism (esp. CC17/ST-17): HvgA mediates adhesion to blood–brain-barrier endothelium and choroid plexus → enables BBB crossing and meningitis (Tazi 2010, PMID 20956545).
  7. Meningeal/systemic inflammation and tissue injury → results in neuronal injury → neurodevelopmental impairment in survivors (inferred causal link from epidemiologic association; mechanistic detail of neuronal injury not fully retrieved) (Gonçalves 2022).

Checklist coverage

  • Molecular pathways / protein dysfunction: granadaene (ornithine-rhamnolipid, cyl operon) causes membrane lysis; HvgA (surface adhesin) mediates cell adhesion; CPS sialic acid engages host Siglec lectin receptors. Specific KEGG/Reactome pathway IDs not retrieved.
  • Cellular processes: cytolysis, neutrophil suppression (reduced ROS burst, impaired NETosis), epithelial/endothelial barrier breach, inflammation.
  • Immune involvement: innate immune evasion via molecular mimicry (Siglec-9/-5); host protection via type-specific anti-CPS IgG.
  • Tissue damage mechanisms: direct cytolysis (granadaene), BBB disruption, inflammatory injury.

Upstream vs downstream: colonization → ascension/invasion (upstream) → immune evasion/bacteremia → organ seeding (meninges, lung) → inflammation/sequelae (downstream).

8. Temporal Development

  • Onset / age windows:
  • EOD: 0–6 days of life (acute, often within hours of birth) (Nanduri 2019).
  • LOD: 7–89 days.
  • "Very-late-onset": >90 days (mainly in preterm/immunocompromised infants — not quantified here).
  • Adults: median older age; rising with comorbidity (Francois Watkins 2019).
  • Onset pattern: acute to fulminant (sepsis); meningitis subacute-to-acute.
  • Course / duration: acute infection; self-limited with prompt antibiotics in most, but meningitis can leave chronic, lifelong neurodevelopmental sequelae. Fatal in a proportion (see Section 11).
  • Critical period for intervention: intrapartum (IAP during labor) is the key window for EOD prevention (CDC 2010). IAP does not prevent LOD, which has remained stable despite EOD decline (Nanduri 2019).

9. Inheritance and Population

Inheritance: Not applicable (infectious; no Mendelian inheritance, penetrance, anticipation, founder effects, or carrier frequency in the human-genetic sense). "Carriage" here refers to bacterial colonization (~18% of pregnant women), not genetic carrier status.

Epidemiology: - Maternal colonization: ~18% worldwide (95% CI 17–19%; regional 11–35%) (Russell 2017). - Global burden (2015 model): ≥409,000 maternal/fetal/infant cases and 147,000 stillbirths + infant deaths annually; Africa bore 54% of cases and 65% of fetal/infant deaths (Seale 2017, Clin Infect Dis). - Global burden (2020 model): ~19.7 million pregnant women colonized; 231,800 early-onset and 162,200 late-onset infant invasive cases; 46,200 GBS-attributed stillbirths; 40,500 maternal invasive cases; ~37,100 survivors with moderate/severe NDI (Gonçalves 2022, Lancet Glob Health). - US infant incidence (ABCs, 2006–2015): EOD declined from 0.37 → 0.23 per 1,000 live births (P<.001); LOD stable (mean 0.31 per 1,000); in 2015, ~840 EOD and ~1,265 LOD cases nationally; LOD now exceeds EOD (Nanduri 2019, PMID 30640366). - US non-pregnant adults (2008–2016): incidence rose from 8.1 → 10.9 per 100,000 (Francois Watkins 2019). - Demographics: higher incidence historically in Black infants (US); regionally highest burden in Africa. Adult disease skews older with diabetes/obesity.

10. Diagnostics

  • Microbiology (gold standard): culture of S. agalactiae from a normally sterile site (blood, CSF) for invasive disease; CSF analysis for meningitis. For maternal screening, culture of combined vaginal-rectal swab at 36 0/7–37 6/7 weeks (updated from 35–37 weeks in CDC 2010) (CDC 2010 MMWR).
  • Rapid molecular: intrapartum NAAT/PCR for GBS (used when colonization status unknown at labor) — specific assay performance not retrieved this session.
  • Serotyping / genotyping: CPS serotyping and MLST (e.g., ST-17 identification) for epidemiology; HvgA detection proposed as a marker for the hypervirulent clone (Tazi 2010).
  • Susceptibility testing: penicillin remains predictably active, but routine disk diffusion may miss low-level reduced β-lactam susceptibility (PBP2x mutants); erythromycin/clindamycin testing (including D-test for inducible clindamycin resistance) is needed for penicillin-allergic patients (PMID 33553474; macrolide-resistance surveillance above).
  • Omics/liquid biopsy / imaging / EEG etc.: Not disease-defining; not retrieved and generally not applicable to routine GBS diagnosis.
  • Human genetic testing: Not applicable.
  • Differential diagnosis: other neonatal sepsis pathogens (E. coli, Listeria monocytogenes), other causes of neonatal respiratory distress and meningitis; in adults, other streptococcal/staphylococcal invasive infections. Distinguishing-feature detail not specifically retrieved.

11. Outcome / Prognosis

  • Neonatal case fatality: GBS remains a leading cause of neonatal sepsis/meningitis mortality; specific CFR figures were not precisely retrieved this session (the 2015/2020 global models report large absolute death counts — 147,000 stillbirths+infant deaths in 2015 — rather than per-case CFR) (Seale 2017).
  • Morbidity / long-term sequelae: Meningitis survivors are at high risk of moderate-to-severe neurodevelopmental impairment (cerebral palsy, hearing/vision loss, cognitive/motor deficits); ~37,100 survivors developed moderate/severe NDI in the 2020 model (Gonçalves 2022).
  • Prognostic factors: prematurity, meningitis (vs bacteremia-only), delay to effective antibiotics, serotype III/CC17 (meningitis tropism), and in adults, severity of underlying diabetes/comorbidity.
  • Adult outcomes: higher morbidity/mortality with age and comorbidity; quantitative CFR not retrieved.
  • Formal QoL instrument data (EQ-5D/SF-36/PROMIS) specific to GBS: not available from this session's retrieval.

12. Treatment

Antimicrobial therapy (NCIT suggestions in brackets): - Penicillin G is the drug of choice; ampicillin is an acceptable alternative — for both treatment and intrapartum prophylaxis (CDC 2010 MMWR; AAFP summary). (NCIT: Penicillin G; Ampicillin.) - Meningitis: typically ampicillin/penicillin, often combined with an aminoglycoside empirically pending cultures (combination synergy) — specific regimen detail not fully retrieved. - Penicillin-allergic patients (prophylaxis): cefazolin if not at high risk of anaphylaxis; for high-risk allergy, clindamycin only if susceptibility (incl. D-test) confirmed, otherwise vancomycin. Erythromycin is no longer acceptable (CDC 2010). (NCIT: Cefazolin; Clindamycin; Vancomycin.) - Resistance considerations: penicillin resistance is not established clinically, but reduced β-lactam susceptibility (PBP2x mutants) is emerging and may be missed on routine testing (PMID 33553474); macrolide/clindamycin resistance (erm/mef) is substantial and rising (erythromycin rose 15.8%→32.8%, clindamycin 10.5%→15.0% in one surveillance period) (four-state surveillance; West Virginia 2025).

Supportive care: neonatal/adult sepsis and meningitis management — respiratory support, hemodynamic support, fluids; neurorehabilitation for survivors with sequelae. (General; specific GBS protocols not separately retrieved.)

Advanced/experimental therapeutics: No gene/cell/RNA therapy applies. The principal investigational product is a maternal vaccine (see Section 13), not a therapeutic.

Pharmacogenomics / targeted / immunotherapy / surgery: Not applicable beyond source control (e.g., drainage of septic joint/abscess) — surgical detail not retrieved.

13. Prevention

  • Primary prevention (current standard): universal antenatal GBS screening (vaginal-rectal culture at 36 0/7–37 6/7 weeks) + intrapartum antibiotic prophylaxis (IAP) with penicillin G (ampicillin alternative) for colonized women and those with risk factors. This strategy has driven the marked EOD decline but does not prevent LOD (CDC 2010 MMWR RR-59(10); Nanduri 2019). (Note: ACOG later assumed stewardship of the US guideline; the ACOG 2020 document was not retrieved this session.)
  • Immunization (investigational): GBS6, a hexavalent (serotypes Ia, Ib, II, III, IV, V) CPS–conjugate maternal vaccine, elicited anti-CPS IgG in pregnant women that transferred to infants at levels associated with reduced invasive-disease risk, with no vaccine-associated safety signal (phase 2) (Madhi 2023, NEJM 389:215–227, PMID 37467497). A trivalent conjugate also showed protection against colonization, ascending infection, and neonatal transmission in rodent models (PMID 29416049, PMC5803199). No licensed GBS vaccine exists as of the retrieved sources.
  • Secondary/tertiary prevention: early recognition and treatment of neonatal/adult infection; management of comorbidity (glycemic control) to reduce adult risk (Francois Watkins 2019).
  • Genetic counseling / carrier screening / public-health sanitation measures: Not applicable in the usual senses (this is not a heritable or environmentally transmitted disease).

14. Other Species / Natural Disease

S. agalactiae is a genuine multi-host pathogen (2024 host-adaptation study, PMID 39133742):

  • Cattle (bovine mastitis): S. agalactiae is a classic agent of contagious bovine mastitis; clonal complex CC67 is essentially bovine-associated. Genomic adaptation includes acquisition of lactose-metabolism capacity and lateral gene transfer from other mastitis streptococci (S. uberis, S. dysgalactiae) (BMC Genomics 2013, 14:252; transcriptomic/genomic bovine adaptation, PMC3890567).
  • Fish (farmed tilapia): causes streptococcosis/meningoencephalitis in tilapia; Chinese tilapia isolates are genomically close to the human strain A909 but carry a distinct ~10-kb genomic island; serotype Ib fish strains show reductive evolution (10–25% genome loss) (PMID 24215651; host-adaptation review above).
  • Humans: the hypervirulent ST-17 clone is mainly human-associated (2024 host-adaptation study).

Taxonomy: Streptococcus agalactiae (NCBI txid1311, provisional). Zoonotic potential: cross-species transmission and shared lineages exist (notably fish-to-human foodborne invasive disease has been described in the literature); specific zoonosis quantification not retrieved this session. Orthologous host genes / VBO breed IDs / OMIA entries: not retrieved.

15. Model Organisms

Primary models are murine, reproducing distinct stages:

  • Ascending vaginal infection in pregnant mice: models colonization → ascension → fetal infection; used to show roles of β-hemolysin/cytolysin (granadaene), hyaluronidase, and npx in PPROM/preterm birth/fetal demise (Randis, PMID 24474814; npx, PMID 36409087; hyaluronidase, PMC4937215).
  • Neonatal transmission/meningitis model: pups acquire GBS from vaginally colonized dams, reproducing the natural route of infection and neonatal meningitis (Nat Commun 2018, PMC6081475, doi:10.1038/s41467-018-05492-y).
  • Vaccine efficacy models: rodent colonization/ascending-infection models demonstrated maternal-vaccine protection of offspring (PMID 29416049).
  • Host–microbiome interaction model: GBS + Gardnerella vaginalis co-inoculation needed for vaginal colonization/ascension (PMC8555936).

Model characteristics: these systems recapitulate vertical transmission, ascending infection, preterm birth, and neonatal meningitis. Limitations (noted across the literature but not exhaustively retrieved): mouse–human differences in gestation, placentation, and immune/Siglec repertoire limit translation of immune-evasion findings; and GBS alone colonizes mice poorly without microbiome manipulation. Cellular/in-vitro models (e.g., human BBB endothelial cell adhesion assays used in the HvgA work — Tazi 2010) complement the animal models. Specific MGI/IMPC/Cellosaurus resource entries were not retrieved this session.
