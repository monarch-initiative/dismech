Histiocytic Sarcoma (MONDO:0019479) — Comprehensive Disease Characteristics Report

1. Disease Information

Overview. Histiocytic sarcoma is an ultra-rare, highly aggressive haematopoietic neoplasm whose cells show the morphology and immunophenotype of mature tissue histiocytes (macrophages). Orphanet defines it as "a rare histiocytic tumor characterized by a malignant proliferation of cells showing morphological and immunophenotypic features of mature tissue histiocytes" (Orphanet ORPHA:86896). It accounts for <1% of haematological malignancies, arises most often at extranodal sites in adults, and may be de novo (primary) or secondary, i.e. associated with/transdifferentiated from another haematological neoplasm or a mediastinal germ cell tumour (Histiocytic Sarcoma: A Review and Update, Int J Mol Sci 2025;26(17):8554, doi:10.3390/ijms26178554).

Classification. In the WHO 5th-edition classification of haematolymphoid tumours, HS sits in the histiocyte/macrophage neoplasm group of "histiocytic/dendritic cell neoplasms", placed after myeloid neoplasms "in recognition of their derivation from common myeloid progenitors that give rise to cells of the monocytic/histiocytic/dendritic lineages" (quoted via retrieved extraction; WHO 5th edition: Myeloid and Histiocytic/Dendritic Neoplasms, PMC9252913; Pathologic characteristics of histiocytic and dendritic cell neoplasms, PMC11076448). A 2026 Histiocyte Society international consensus proposes the unified designation malignant histiocytic neoplasm (MHN) covering histiocytic, Langerhans cell and interdigitating dendritic cell sarcomas (Goyal et al., Blood 2026;148(3):275–288, PMID 41915766; doi:10.1182/blood.2026033049).

2. Etiology

2.1 Disease causal factors

HS is a clonal neoplasm caused by somatic activating lesions of the RAS–MAPK pathway, arising in a cell of the mononuclear phagocyte lineage or in an upstream haematopoietic progenitor. Egan et al. identified "recurrent mutations involving the RAS-MAPK signaling pathway (MAP2K1, KRAS, NRAS, BRAF, PTPN11, NF1, CBL) in a majority (57%) of histiocytic sarcoma cases" and PI3K-activating mutations in a further 21% (quoted via retrieved extraction; Mod Pathol 2019;32:830–843, PMID 30626916). Across the broader malignant-histiocytosis spectrum, "activating mutations in MAPK-pathway genes were identified in 80% of MH cases; 29% had mutations in the PI3k-AKT-mTOR pathway and 59% had mutations in epigenetic modulating genes" (quoted via retrieved extraction; Mayo/UAB Histiocytosis Working Group, Mod Pathol 2023;36:100268). Summarised ranges across series: RAS/MAPK 57–90%; MAP2K1 15–24%; KRAS 12–50%; BRAF V600E up to 62.5% in selected series; PTPN11 5–19% (Int J Mol Sci 2025;26:8554).

A second, distinct causal route is lineage transdifferentiation from a pre-existing lymphoid neoplasm (see §4.4): the lymphoid clone acquires RAS/MAPK alterations and converts phenotype. "The critical 'transdifferentiation' step to HDT appears to be associated with the acquisition of RAS/MAPK pathway alterations" (quoted via retrieved extraction; Egan et al., Mod Pathol 2021;34:336–347).

2.2 Risk factors

Genetic (constitutional). No germline predisposition syndrome, susceptibility locus or GWAS signal for human HS was retrieved in this session. All recurrent lesions reported are somatic/tumour-restricted. Family-history data are absent from the retrieved human literature. (By contrast, heritable predisposition is established in dogs — §14.)

Acquired/clinical risk factors (strongest evidence). - A prior or concurrent haematological malignancy, especially follicular lymphoma, chronic lymphocytic leukaemia/SLL, mantle cell lymphoma, B-lymphoblastic leukaemia/lymphoma, multiple myeloma and Hodgkin lymphoma (Mod Pathol 2021;34:336–347; clonally related FL + HS, Blood Cancer J 2025; clonally related HDS and CLL/SLL, 7 cases, Mod Pathol; HS with CCND1 rearrangement from mantle cell lymphoma, AJCP 2022; myeloma→HS transdifferentiation, PMC12270463; HS in nodular lymphocyte-predominant Hodgkin lymphoma background, PMC12310312). The 2026 consensus explicitly asks clinicians to document prior haematopoietic malignancy as part of diagnosis (PMID 41915766). - Mediastinal (non-seminomatous) germ cell tumour, where histiocytic proliferations ranging from haemophagocytic syndrome to disseminated HS occur, with evidence interpreted as "hematologic malignancy and MGCTs aris[ing] from a common progenitor cell" (quoted via retrieved extraction; PMID 16086090; PMID 36051154; post-chemotherapy HS after mediastinal GCT, PMID 33786289; PMID 18987551). - Iatrogenic immunosuppression after solid-organ transplantation — rare but documented, including EBV-negative cases (Histiocytic sarcoma in renal transplant patients: a literature review, PMID 37784161 / PMC10546704; kidney-transplant case, PMC5066022; PMC4544561). - Prior cytotoxic therapy is temporally associated in secondary cases (e.g. HS after combination chemotherapy for mediastinal GCT; HS after hyperfractionated cyclophosphamide/vincristine/doxorubicin/dexamethasone for B-ALL), but the retrieved reports describe association in clonally related disease rather than establishing therapy as an independent carcinogenic cause (PMID 33786289; Blood 2016;128:2475). - Demographics: older adult age and male sex (SEER: 99 men vs 60 women; female:male incidence ratio 0.49) (Kommalapati et al., Blood 2018).

Environmental/occupational/lifestyle risk factors. No evidence retrieved. No CTD/TOXNET-type exposure association, no tobacco, alcohol, diet, radiation or occupational signal for human HS was found in this session's searches. This should be recorded in a knowledge base as "no established environmental risk factor", not as "absent".

2.3 Protective factors

None identified. No genetic protective variant, modifier allele, dietary or lifestyle protective factor for HS was retrieved. gnomAD-type allele-frequency reasoning does not apply to somatic drivers.

2.4 Gene–environment interactions

No human gene–environment interaction data retrieved. The only interaction-like observations are therapy–genotype interactions (acquired CBL mutations emerging with MEK-inhibitor exposure and conferring resistance, Leukemia 2023; class III MEK1/2 mutations associating with progression on MEK inhibitors, Cancer Cell 2025, PMID 41135521) — pharmacological rather than environmental.

3. Phenotypes

HS has no single stereotyped presentation; the phenotype is dictated by site. Orphanet's clinical description lists: "a solitary mass, lymphadenopathy, a skin rash or numerous tumors on the trunk and extremities, lytic bone lesions, hepatosplenomegaly with pancytopenia, intestinal obstruction, and/or systemic symptoms" (quoted via retrieved extraction; ORPHA:86896).

Severity and progression. Uniformly aggressive: Orphanet records "The neoplasm is aggressive with typically poor therapy response" (ORPHA:86896); the 2025 review describes an "aggressive clinical course with a limited response to therapy and a high mortality rate" (Int J Mol Sci 2025;26:8554). Severity is bimodal by extent rather than by site: unifocal disease can be indolent after resection, multifocal disease is rapidly progressive (§11).

Quality-of-life impact. No HS-specific QoL instrument data were retrieved. The nearest evidence is from the broader histiocytic-neoplasm population: a prospective registry of 215 patients with histiocytic neoplasms using the Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale found anxiety and depression were "stable over time and correlated with financial burden, symptom severity, and health-related quality of life" (quoted via retrieved extraction; Blood Adv 2025;9(6):1376); survivorship issues in adult histiocytic neoplasms are reviewed in PMC10481619, and a Histiocytic Disorder Survivor Study with parallel HRQoL assessment was launched in September 2022 (Blood Cancer United award page).

4. Genetic / Molecular Information

4.1 "Causal" genes (somatic drivers)

No germline causal gene exists. The recurrent somatic drivers, with gene symbols as retrieved (HGNC IDs could not be verified in this session and are therefore omitted rather than guessed):

  • RAS–MAPK: MAP2K1, KRAS, NRAS, BRAF, PTPN11, NF1, CBL (PMID 30626916)
  • PI3K–AKT–mTOR: mutations activating this pathway in ~21–29% of cases (PMID 30626916; Mod Pathol 2023;36:100268)
  • Tumour suppressors / cell cycle: CDKN2A (39% of HS), TP53 (24% of HS) (Massoth et al., The Oncologist 2021;26:e1263)
  • Chromatin/epigenetic modifiers: SETD2 (a defining feature of one molecular subgroup) (Haematologica 2020;105(4):951–960); epigenetic-modifier mutations in 59% of malignant histiocytoses (Mod Pathol 2023;36:100268)
  • Other reported in individual cases: FLT3, NOTCH2, KMT2A in primary splenic HS with HLH (PMC9124500); NF1 mutation with TUBB3 amplification in gastric HS (Springer 2024, doi:10.1007/s00795-024-00393-0)

4.2 Molecular subgroups

Whole-exome + RNA-sequencing of 21 archival primary HS cases defined two molecular subgroups separating on the presence/absence of NF1/PTPN11 alterations and the prevalence of SETD2 mutations, confirmed by unsupervised clustering of RNA-seq data; the NF1/PTPN11 wild-type cluster "was comprised primarily of cases with prototypic RAS/MAPK pathway activating mutations involving KRAS, NRAS, BRAF and MAP2K1" (quoted via retrieved extraction; Haematologica 2020;105(4):951–960, doi:10.3324/haematol.2019.230375; accompanying editorial PMID 32238464).

4.3 Variant classes and functional consequences

  • Activating missense hotspots: KRAS/NRAS codons 12, 13 and 146; PTPN11 gain-of-function mutations in the SH2 domain; MAP2K1 mutations in the negative-regulatory helix and the core kinase domain; BRAF V600E (PMID 30626916; Int J Mol Sci 2025).
  • RAF-independent ("class III") MEK1/2 mutations, including the in-frame deletion MEK1 E102_I103del, are "common in patients with systemic histiocytic neoplasms, drive disease in a conditional knock-in mouse model, and associate with progression on FDA-approved MEK inhibitors" (quoted via retrieved extraction; Cancer Cell 2025, PMID 41135521; PMC12616613).
  • Loss-of-function: NF1 (loss of RAS-GAP function → RAS-GTP accumulation), CBL (loss of E3-ubiquitin-ligase–mediated receptor downregulation), CDKN2A (mutation/deletion → loss of CDK4/6 restraint), TP53 (PMID 30626916; The Oncologist 2021; Leukemia 2023).
  • Structural/fusion events: a novel MIGA2::BRAF fusion in primary CNS HS, "implicated in possible constitutive activation of the MAP kinase pathway"; the same tumour harboured two BRAF-related fusions (J Neuropathol Exp Neurol 2024;83(10):882, PMC11413427). IGH::BCL2 and CCND1 rearrangements occur in HS transdifferentiated from FL and mantle cell lymphoma respectively (AJCP 2025 abstract 509; AJCP 2022).
  • Somatic vs germline / ACMG classification: all of the above are somatic; ACMG/AMP germline classification is not applicable, and the relevant framework is somatic tiering (e.g. OncoKB/AMP-ASCO-CAP). No HS-specific ClinVar germline curation was retrieved.
  • Population allele frequencies: not applicable to somatic drivers; no HS-relevant gnomAD data retrieved.

4.4 Clonal relationships (the "secondary HS" genetics)

  • Follicular lymphoma → HS: shared IGH::BCL2 and identical IGH rearrangements establish common clonal origin; one case shared canonical FL mutations (CREBBP, PIM1) plus MAP2K1 E203K, "suggesting this mutation preceded transdifferentiation of the FL to HS" (quoted via retrieved extraction; Blood Cancer J 2025; KRAS mutation in secondary malignant histiocytosis from low-grade FL, PMC6190545).
  • B-lymphoblastic leukaemia/lymphoma → HS: identical IGH-MYC and IGK rearrangements (AJCP, doi:10.1093/ajcp/aqz056); shared IGK/TCRB rearrangements and PAX5 alterations, with PTPN11 mutation hypothesised as a transdifferentiation driver (Blood 2016;128:2475; Am J Hematol, doi:10.1002/ajh.26983); a clonally related HS emerging 33 years after B-ALL (PMC13472141).
  • CLL/SLL, mantle cell lymphoma, myeloma, Hodgkin lymphoma: documented clonal relationships (Mod Pathol 7-case CLL/SLL series; AJCP 2022 CCND1; PMC12270463; PMC12310312).
  • Cell of origin: driver mutations traceable to both myeloid and lymphoid lineage cells in patients with histiocytic neoplasms "point[s] toward multipotent progenitors as the cell of origin"; the same KRAS or BRAF mutation was detected in lesions developing up to 25 years apart, implying long-lived mutated progenitors (Blood Neoplasia 2025;2(2):100074, PMC12067900); reviewed in J Pathol 2026 (Kemps et al.) and Nat Rev Dis Primers "Histiocytic disorders".

4.5 Modifier genes

None established for human HS. Candidate modifiers emerge only from secondary/transdifferentiated disease (e.g. PAX5 in B-ALL-derived HS; Am J Hematol) and from canine genetics (§14).

4.6 Epigenetic information

  • Methylation classification works for HS: in primary CNS HS, DNA-methylation data realigned to the NIH HEME classifier gave "a high confidence match (methylation class score 0.99) to HS", while the DKFZ sarcoma classifier gave no definite match (PMC11413427); methylation profiling against the sarcoma classifier was decisive in other CNS histiocytic cases (PMC12279446).
  • Broader classifier context: DNA-methylation classes have been defined across >1,400 haematolymphoid neoplasms with 97% diagnostic concordance in high-confidence cases (Blood Adv, doi:10.1182/bloodadvances.2024015275), and a nanopore-deployed classifier ("Lamprey") spans 86 haematologic entities including histiocytic disorders (medRxiv 2026).
  • Histiocytosis methylome biology (non-HS histiocytoses, mechanistically relevant): 23,322 differentially methylated sites between controls and histiocytoses, with differentially methylated genes in inflammatory response, RAS, TNF and PI3K-mTOR signalling, and hypermethylation of the let-7b–encoding region (PMC11794134; ASH 2023 "Deciphering the Epigenetic Landscape in Histiocytic Neoplasms").
  • Mutations in epigenetic modulating genes in 59% of malignant histiocytoses provide a genetic route to the same phenotype (Mod Pathol 2023;36:100268).

4.7 Chromosomal abnormalities

Per the scope rules of this request, arm-level copy-number changes and chromothripsis are not described. At gene level, recurrent CDKN2A deletions/mutations are the best-documented copy-number lesion (The Oncologist 2021;26:e1263), and gene-level fusions are listed in §4.3. Clonality-defining rearrangements in secondary HS (IGH::BCL2, CCND1, IGH-MYC, IGK) are detectable by FISH/PCR and are diagnostically useful (§10).

5. Environmental Information

  • Environmental factors / toxins / radiation / pollution / occupational exposure: no established associations retrieved. No HS-specific entries in toxicogenomic or exposure-based sources surfaced in this session's searches.
  • Lifestyle factors: no evidence retrieved (no smoking, alcohol, diet, obesity or physical-activity association).
  • Iatrogenic/host-state factors (the only non-genetic contributors with supporting reports): chronic pharmacological immunosuppression after solid-organ transplantation (PMC10546704; PMC5066022) and prior cytotoxic chemotherapy in the setting of an antecedent neoplasm (PMID 33786289).
  • Infectious agents: no pathogen is implicated as a cause. EBV is characteristically not required: in the renal-transplant HS literature review, EBV was positive in 2 cases and negative in another, with one report explicitly "the first report of HS in a solid organ recipient without evidence of EBV infection" (quoted via retrieved extraction; PMC10546704). An in-vitro study models extracellular-vesicle secretion from histiocytic sarcoma cells in the context of bacterial co-infection, i.e. infection as a modifier of tumour-cell behaviour rather than a cause (Int J Mol Sci, doi:10.3390/ijms27114949).

6. Mechanism / Pathophysiology

6.1 Ordered causal chain (initiating lesion → clinical manifestation)

  1. A somatic activating lesion arises in the RAS–MAPK pathway — MAP2K1, KRAS, NRAS, BRAF (including V600E and BRAF fusions such as MIGA2::BRAF), PTPN11 (SH2-domain gain of function), or loss-of-function in the negative regulators NF1 and CBL — in a haematopoietic cell of the mononuclear-phagocyte lineage or in an upstream multipotent progenitor (PMID 30626916; PMC11413427; Blood Neoplasia 2025, PMC12067900).
  2. This lesion leads to constitutive, ligand-independent flux through the MAPK cascade (MAP3K→MEK1/2→ERK1/2; GO:0000165 "MAPK cascade", AmiGO). The functional proof is pharmacological and in vivo: histiocytic neoplasms show "a notable dependence on MAPK signalling and are consequently responsive to MEK inhibition" (quoted via retrieved extraction; Nature 2019, PMID 30867592), and conditional knock-in of MEK1 E102_I103del alone drives a lethal histiocytic-like neoplasm in mice (Cancer Cell 2025, PMID 41135521).
  3. Constitutive ERK signalling results in a self-sustaining programme of proliferation and survival in the histiocyte/macrophage compartment, together with blocked terminal quiescence — inferred for human HS from genotype–response concordance and demonstrated directly in the murine MEK1-mutant model (PMID 41135521).
  4. Branch 3a — co-operating PI3K–AKT–mTOR activation in ~21–29% of cases adds growth/metabolic signalling and a second survival input (PMID 30626916; Mod Pathol 2023). Canine whole-exome/transcriptome work independently shows concurrent ERK and Akt pathway activation in histiocytic sarcoma (Sci Rep 2023, PMC10212919).
  5. Branch 3b — loss of cell-cycle and genome-integrity brakes: CDKN2A (39%) and TP53 (24%) lesions remove G1 restraint and p53-dependent apoptosis/senescence, permitting the high-grade cytology and mitotic rate that define the tumour (The Oncologist 2021). Canine transcriptomes show the downstream consequence as co-ordinated cell-cycle activation (FOXM1, AURKB, PLK1, E2F) (Cancers 2025, PMC11940154).
  6. Branch 3c — epigenetic reprogramming: mutations in epigenetic modulators (59% of malignant histiocytoses) and disease-associated methylome remodelling (including hypermethylation at the let-7b locus, with downstream BCL2L1/BCL2 effects) stabilise the malignant macrophage state; HS has a methylation class distinct enough to be recognised by a haematologic classifier (Mod Pathol 2023; PMC11794134; PMC11413427).
  7. Branch 3d — transdifferentiation route: when the mutation is acquired within an existing lymphoid clone (FL, CLL/SLL, MCL, B-ALL, myeloma), the RAS/MAPK lesion drives lineage conversion to a histiocytic phenotype, producing a secondary HS that carries the lymphoid clone's rearrangements. "The critical 'transdifferentiation' step to HDT appears to be associated with the acquisition of RAS/MAPK pathway alterations"; an epigenetic contribution has been hypothesised but not proven (quoted via retrieved extraction; Mod Pathol 2021;34:336–347; Blood Cancer J 2025).
  8. Clonal expansion of malignant histiocytes produces a mass of large, non-cohesive, atypical cells with abundant eosinophilic cytoplasm, retaining macrophage differentiation markers (CD163, CD68, lysozyme, CD4, CD11c, HLA-DR, PU.1) — the diagnostic phenotype (Int J Mol Sci 2025; PMC11076448; Mod Pathol CD163 series).
  9. Local infiltration destroys the host tissue, with the clinical consequence determined by site: bowel wall infiltration leads to obstruction, bleeding or (rarely) tumour-embolic necrosis (PMC11452572); bone infiltration results in lytic lesions; CNS infiltration results in focal deficits and leptomeningeal dissemination (Diagnostics 2026;16:2623).
  10. Dissemination to lymph nodes, spleen, liver, marrow and other organs follows, giving lymphadenopathy, hepatosplenomegaly and marrow infiltration (Orphanet; PMC9271260; PMC6170302).
  11. Marrow replacement plus macrophage-driven cytokine activation leads to cytopenias and, in a subset, a haemophagocytic (HLH-like) syndrome with fever, hepatosplenomegaly and multilineage cytopenias — the tumour cells being competent phagocytes (PMC9124500; PMC7481156). The cytokine step is inferred from HLH pathophysiology plus the macrophage phenotype, not demonstrated for HS specifically.
  12. High glycolytic flux in the tumour cells makes the lesions intensely FDG-avid (e.g. SUVmax 19.4), which is how extent and response are measured clinically (J Nucl Med Technol 2021;49:358, PMID 34330800).
  13. Untreated or multifocal disease leads to progressive organ compromise and death, with a median overall survival of roughly 6 months in aggregate series (Int J Mol Sci 2025).
  14. Therapeutic interruption of step 2 reverses the phenotype — MEK inhibition yields complete and partial responses, and in MEK-inhibitor-resistant class III MEK1 mutants, ERK inhibition restores control; acquired CBL mutation is one route to MEK-inhibitor resistance, bypassable by CSF1R inhibition (Nature 2019, PMID 30867592; Cancer Cell 2025, PMID 41135521; Leukemia 2023, PMC10400417). This pharmacological reversal is the strongest evidence that step 2 is the mechanistic bottleneck.

6.2 Category detail

  • Molecular pathways: RAS–MAPK (dominant, upstream), PI3K–AKT–mTOR (co-operating), CDKN2A–CDK4/6–RB and p53 (downstream brakes removed), CSF1R-receptor signalling (ligand-receptor axis exploitable therapeutically) (PMID 30626916; PMC10400417).
  • Cellular processes: proliferation and cell-cycle dysregulation (FOXM1/AURKB/PLK1/E2F programme in the canine orthologous disease), evasion of apoptosis, tissue-infiltrative behaviour, phagocytosis/haemophagocytosis (PMC11940154; PMC9124500).
  • Protein dysfunction: gain of function (MEK1/2 kinase, mutant RAS, BRAF V600E and BRAF-fusion kinases, SHP2/PTPN11 phosphatase) and loss of function (neurofibromin's RAS-GAP activity; CBL ubiquitin-ligase activity; p16/CDKN2A; p53) (PMID 30626916; PMID 41135521).
  • Metabolic changes: the only HS-specific metabolic read-out retrieved is high FDG avidity, i.e. elevated glucose uptake (PMID 34330800). No metabolomics or lipidomics studies of HS were retrieved.
  • Immune system involvement: the neoplastic cell is itself an immune (macrophage-lineage) cell; PD-L1 is expressed in ~50% of HS cases, and PD-L1 level correlates with checkpoint-inhibitor response (responders median 88% vs non-responders 30%), with loss of PD-L1 at a resistant site in one patient (Leuk Lymphoma 2025, doi:10.1080/10428194.2025.2561114; PMID 38098600). Secondary immune dysregulation manifests as HLH-like syndrome.
  • Tissue damage mechanisms: direct infiltrative destruction and mass effect; obstruction; rarely vascular occlusion with infarction (PMC11452572). Fibrosis/oxidative-stress mechanisms specific to HS were not retrieved.
  • Biochemical abnormalities: no enzyme deficiency, receptor-trafficking defect or channelopathy applies; the biochemistry is kinase hyperactivity (above).
  • Molecular profiling: transcriptomics in human HS is limited to the RNA-seq clustering that defined the two molecular subgroups (Haematologica 2020); the deepest transcriptomic resource is canine (PMC11940154; PMC10212919). No HS proteomics, metabolomics, lipidomics, single-cell or spatial-transcriptomics datasets were retrieved, and no HS-specific CRISPR/RNAi dependency screen was found — these are genuine gaps.
  • Suggested ontology terms: processes — GO:0000165 MAPK cascade (verified); additional candidate labels (PI3K signalling, positive regulation of cell proliferation, macrophage differentiation) are listed by label only, IDs unverified this session. Cell types — CL:0000235 macrophage and CL:0000576 monocyte (OLS4 CL:0000235; CellGuide).

7. Anatomical Structures Affected

Organ level — primary. HS is predominantly extranodal. Orphanet: "Most cases occur in extranodal sites, most commonly the intestinal tract, skin, and soft tissue" (ORPHA:86896). A recent review reports the site distribution as skin and connective tissue 41%, lymph nodes 14%, gastrointestinal tract 12%, haematopoietic system 9% (Front Oncol 2025); the same review notes primary disease confined strictly to lymph nodes is "exceptionally unusual, accounting for less than 1% of documented cases". In the 27-patient tertiary-centre cohort, involvement was lymph node 50%, soft tissue 40%, bone 36%, bone marrow 22% (PMC9271260). Documented primary sites in the retrieved case literature include stomach (PMC13478121), oral cavity/jaws (PMID 39048487), head and neck (Diagn Pathol 2007;2:7), breast (PMC11149727), lung (PMC10918721; PMC12920180), spleen (PMC9124500), liver (PMID 42405293), bladder (PMC7888511), skin (PMC12400779), vertebra (PMID 35884764) and CNS, including intramedullary spinal cord (Diagnostics 2026;16:2623; PMC11413427).

Organ level — secondary. Multifocal spread to liver, spleen, marrow, lung and CNS (including CNS metastasis from a pulmonary primary, PMC12920180); marrow replacement causing cytopenias (PMC6170302).

Body systems involved. Haematopoietic/lymphoreticular (primary system of origin, reflected in the ICD-10 C96 placement), integumentary, digestive, musculoskeletal, nervous, respiratory, and genitourinary systems depending on site (ICD-10-CM C96.A; site citations above).

Tissue and cell level. The neoplastic population is the mature macrophage/histiocyte (CL:0000235, verified), developmentally downstream of the monocyte (CL:0000576) and, per lineage-tracing of driver mutations, of a multipotent haematopoietic progenitor (PMC12067900). Affected host tissues are connective/soft tissue, mucosa and submucosa of the gut, dermis, lymphoid tissue and marrow stroma; spindle-cell morphology can dominate in some tumours (Diagn Pathol 2007;2:7).

Subcellular level. Diagnostic localisations rather than mechanistic ones: CD163 and CD68 are membrane/lysosomal-granule antigens, lysozyme is a granule/secreted protein and PU.1 (SPI1) is nuclear (Int J Mol Sci 2025). Signalling occurs at the plasma membrane–cytosol interface (RAS/RAF/MEK/ERK) with nuclear effector output. GO cellular-component IDs were not verified in this session and are therefore not asserted.

Localisation / lateralisation. Lesions are typically unifocal and unilateral at presentation when localised, or multifocal/disseminated and asymmetric when advanced; the unifocal/multifocal split is the main prognostic axis (PMC9271260). Verified UBERON terms for the principal sites: UBERON:0000029 lymph node (BioPortal/BCGO entry), UBERON:0002106 spleen, UBERON:0002371 bone marrow (both retrieved via ontology listings this session). Skin, intestine, brain and liver terms exist in UBERON but their exact IDs could not be confirmed in this session and are intentionally omitted.

8. Temporal Development

Onset. Adult-onset; Orphanet records age of onset simply as "Adult" (ORPHA:86896). Median age at diagnosis differs by cohort and ascertainment: 63 years (range 18–96) in SEER (Kommalapati et al., Blood 2018); 59 years (range 3–83) in a 27-patient referral cohort (PMC9271260); 55 years (range 15–89) in the 14-case extranodal series (Hornick, Jaffe & Fletcher, Am J Surg Pathol 2004;28:1133–44). One recent pediatric case review reports a median age of 46 years with a bimodal pattern (0–29 and 50–69 years) and notes HS is "more commonly reported in adults, typically in the sixth decade of life, [while] pediatric cases are distinctly uncommon" (Uncommon but intriguing: a pediatric primary histiocytic sarcoma case, PMID 40486162 / PMC12144458). Onset pattern: subacute, often with a painless mass or nonspecific systemic symptoms, then rapid progression; diagnostic delay from misdiagnosis is common (PMID 41049224; PMID 40260333).

Staging / stages. No HS-specific staging system exists; MONDO-linked descriptions note it "usually present[s] as stage III/IV disease" (MONDO/NORD). Practically, disease is stratified as unifocal/localised vs multifocal/disseminated, with marrow involvement as an additional axis (PMC9271260; Blood 2026 consensus, PMID 41915766).

Progression rate and course. Typically rapid and progressive: median OS ≈6 months overall (Int J Mol Sci 2025), and "the rate of survival does not improve over time" in population data (PMC9297408). However, the course is genuinely heterogeneous — the French national series of 141 malignant histiocytoses concluded that behaviour is heterogeneous and warrants tailored therapy (Blood Adv 2025;9(10):2530), and localised disease can be chronic/curable (12 years disease-free after resection of oesophageal HS; PMC9271260).

Remission patterns. Remissions are treatment-induced, not spontaneous: complete responses occur with chemotherapy (but with frequent relapse), with targeted MAPK-pathway inhibition (including durable CR >2 years on trametinib), with checkpoint blockade, and after allogeneic transplantation (3 of 4 allo-SCT patients in one review achieving CR, 2 with long-term remission) (PMC9271260; JNCCN 2022, doi:10.6004/jnccn.2022.7001; PMID 38098600; TCT 2025 systematic review).

Critical periods / windows of opportunity. (i) At first presentation of a localised lesion — complete resection ± radiotherapy is when cure is realistically achievable (PMC9271260; Blood 2026 consensus). (ii) Before marrow involvement develops — marrow involvement collapses median OS from 43 to 3 months in one cohort (PMC9271260). (iii) At molecular profiling — identifying an actionable MAPK lesion or high PD-L1 early opens the targeted/immunotherapeutic options that produce the most durable responses (PMID 41915766).

9. Inheritance and Population

Epidemiology. - Incidence (US, SEER): 0.17 per 1,000,000 per year (159 cases captured); described as "ultra-rare" (Kommalapati et al., Blood 2018;131:265–268; Int J Mol Sci 2025). - Incidence (Europe, Orphanet): annual incidence <1/1,000,000 (ORPHA:86896). - Prevalence: no point-prevalence estimate was retrieved; given median survival of months, prevalence approximates incidence. - Share of haematological malignancies: <1% (Int J Mol Sci 2025).

Genetic-etiology items (all N/A for this disease, stated explicitly for the knowledge base). Inheritance pattern: not applicable — somatic, non-heritable. Penetrance, expressivity, genetic anticipation, germline mosaicism, founder effects, consanguinity, carrier frequency: not applicable / no data; no germline predisposition gene for human HS was retrieved in this session. (Heritability is established only in the canine disease — §14.)

Population demographics. - Sex: male predominance — 99 men vs 60 women in SEER, female:male incidence ratio 0.49 (Blood 2018); 63% male in the 27-patient cohort (PMC9271260). The extranodal 14-case series was female-weighted (8F/6M), illustrating small-series variance (Hornick 2004). - Age distribution: adults, peaking in the sixth–seventh decades, with rare pediatric cases (Blood 2018; PMC12144458). - Ethnic/geographic distribution: the SEER analysis was framed around "demographic disparities", but specific race/ethnicity effect sizes were not recoverable from the retrieved extraction; no endemic area, geographic cluster, or population-specific variant distribution was retrieved. Cases are reported worldwide (US, Europe, China, Japan, India, Brazil, Middle East across the case literature cited above).

10. Diagnostics

10.1 Biopsy, histopathology and immunohistochemistry (the diagnostic core)

Histology: diffuse growth of large, non-cohesive, polygonal/epithelioid cells with abundant eosinophilic cytoplasm; cytological atypia and prominent (including atypical) mitoses are treated as critical criteria for calling histiocytes malignant; spindle-cell morphology occurs (Int J Mol Sci 2025; PMC11076448; Diagn Pathol 2007;2:7).

Positive immunophenotype: CD163, CD68 (KP1/PGM1), lysozyme, PU.1/SPI1, plus CD4, CD11c, CD45/LCA and HLA-DR (Int J Mol Sci 2025; Mod Pathol five-case CD163 study). CD163 is preferred over CD68 for specificity, and "the combination of CD163 and PU.1 (SPI1) is highly specific for histiocytic sarcoma" (quoted via retrieved extraction; ScienceDirect topic page; Arch Pathol Lab Med review).

Required negatives (to exclude mimics): CD1a and Langerin (Langerhans cell neoplasms), CD21/CD35 (follicular dendritic cell sarcoma), strong S100 (interdigitating dendritic cell sarcoma), MPO/CD13 (myeloid sarcoma), CD20/CD30 (B-cell and other lymphomas), HMB-45/SOX10/S100 (melanoma), cytokeratins/EMA (carcinoma) (Int J Mol Sci 2025; JCDR 2024 case).

Consensus minimum panel (2026): the panel "should adequately confirm that the malignant cells show immunophenotypic differentiation toward mature cells of the mononuclear phagocytic lineage ... with ≥2 lineage-specific markers expressed in the malignant cells"; and "among patients with abnormal peripheral blood counts, a bone marrow aspirate/biopsy is warranted to rule out a concomitant hematopoietic malignancy" (quoted via retrieved extraction; Blood 2026;148(3):275–288, PMID 41915766).

Cytology/FNA can suggest the diagnosis but is not definitive alone (PMC4349012; PMID 29676378).

10.2 Laboratory tests and biomarkers

No diagnostic serum biomarker exists. Relevant labs are supportive: full blood count (cytopenias), marrow examination when counts are abnormal, and the HLH work-up (ferritin, triglycerides, fibrinogen, soluble markers) when a haemophagocytic syndrome is suspected (PMID 41915766; PMC9124500). PD-L1 immunohistochemistry functions as a predictive biomarker and routine testing has been recommended, particularly in advanced disease (Leuk Lymphoma 2025; PMC10918721).

10.3 Imaging

¹⁸F-FDG PET/CT is the imaging backbone: lesions show intense uptake (example SUVmax 19.4), and PET/CT "is a valuable tool in staging this rare entity and in assessing the response to therapy", also guiding biopsy site selection (PMID 34330800; J Nucl Med Technol 49:358). NCCN recommends ¹⁸F-FDG-PET supplemented by organ-specific imaging for initial diagnosis, response assessment and follow-up (Histiocytic Neoplasms, NCCN v2.2021, JNCCN 19(11), PMID 34781268). MRI is used for CNS/spinal disease, where HS can mimic glioblastoma radiologically in older adults (PMC12279446; Diagnostics 2026;16:2623).

Functional tests, electrophysiology: not part of HS diagnosis; no data retrieved.

10.4 Genetic and molecular testing

  • Recommended approach: broad molecular profiling (NGS panel covering MAPK/PI3K pathway genes and fusions) on tumour tissue is a consensus recommendation, because results are directly actionable (PMID 41915766). Published diagnostic series used targeted panels, whole-exome and RNA sequencing (Haematologica 2020; The Oncologist 2021).
  • WES/WGS: research-grade utility demonstrated (WES+RNA-seq defined the two molecular subgroups) rather than routine clinical requirement (Haematologica 2020).
  • RNA sequencing / fusion testing: needed to detect BRAF fusions such as MIGA2::BRAF that panel DNA sequencing may miss (PMC11413427).
  • Single-gene testing: BRAF V600E assessment (IHC/PCR) is the pragmatic minimum where broad panels are unavailable, given BRAF-inhibitor availability (Int J Mol Sci 2025).
  • FISH / clonality assays: essential when a lymphoid neoplasm is present or suspected — t(14;18)/IGH::BCL2 FISH and identical IGH/IGK rearrangement analysis establish clonal relatedness and the diagnosis of secondary/transdifferentiated HS (Blood Cancer J 2025; AJCP 2025 abstract 509; AJCP, doi:10.1093/ajcp/aqz056).
  • Chromosomal microarray / karyotype / mtDNA / repeat-expansion testing: no established role; no HS-specific data retrieved. Germline genetic testing is not indicated (no germline predisposition gene known).
  • Epigenomic diagnostics: DNA-methylation classification is an emerging adjunct and was decisive in difficult CNS cases, with a methylation class score of 0.99 against a haematologic classifier (PMC11413427; PMC12279446; classifier context: Blood Adv).
  • Liquid biopsy: droplet-digital-PCR detection of BRAF V600E in plasma/urine cell-free DNA is validated in LCH/ECD as a non-invasive mutation and response biomarker (PMID 25324352; PMC5705028), but in a large cohort of adults with diverse histiocytic neoplasms there was "poor correlation between tumor and plasma cfDNA molecular findings ... possibly related to low tumor shedding especially among non-BRAF V600E cases" (quoted via retrieved extraction; ASH 2023 abstract 6332). Tissue remains the reference specimen.
  • Proteomics / metabolomics diagnostics: none retrieved.

10.5 Clinical criteria and differential diagnosis

There are no DSM/ICD-style criteria; diagnosis is integrated histopathologic + immunophenotypic + molecular, now codified by the WHO 5th edition and the 2026 consensus minimum criteria (PMC9252913; PMID 41915766). Differential diagnosis includes diffuse large B-cell lymphoma, other large-cell lymphomas, myeloid sarcoma, Langerhans cell sarcoma, interdigitating dendritic cell sarcoma, follicular dendritic cell sarcoma, melanoma, poorly differentiated carcinoma, pleomorphic sarcomas, and reactive/benign histiocytic proliferations and other histiocytoses; ALK-positive histiocytosis is a separate disease entity and is excluded from this report per scope (Arch Pathol Lab Med 2018;142:1322; Arch Pathol Lab Med 2020;144:650; SEER heme/lymph tool). A practical pitfall: tumours whose stroma is heavily enriched in reactive histiocytes were specifically excluded on central review in the French national series — i.e. histiocyte-rich ≠ histiocytic sarcoma (Blood Adv 2025;9(10):2530).

10.6 Screening

No screening is applicable or recommended: there is no asymptomatic screening test, no newborn/carrier screening (no germline gene), and no cascade testing. The only surveillance context retrieved is clinical vigilance for secondary HS in patients with a known lymphoid malignancy or mediastinal germ cell tumour (Mod Pathol 2021; PMID 41915766).

11. Outcome / Prognosis

Survival and mortality. - Median overall survival from diagnosis ≈6 months in aggregate/population-level reporting, and survival has not improved over time (Int J Mol Sci 2025; PMC9297408). - Referral-centre cohorts report longer medians, reflecting selection: median OS 12 months in a 27-patient cohort; a six-patient series reported median OS 52 months (range 0–153) (PMC9271260). - 5-/10-year survival figures specific to HS were not retrievable in this session (the SEER letter's long-term outcome numbers could not be extracted); this is a gap to fill from the primary SEER publication. - Mortality is overwhelmingly disease-specific: in the renal-transplant HS review, all 4 cases died despite chemotherapy in 3 (PMC10546704).

Morbidity, disability and function. No ICF-coded disability data or HS-specific disability registry data were retrieved. Morbidity is driven by site (bowel obstruction, neurological deficit, pathological bone lesions), by cytopenias, and by treatment toxicity. The histiocytic-neoplasm QoL evidence summarised in §3 (anxiety/depression correlating with symptom severity and financial burden) is the closest available surrogate (Blood Adv 2025;9(6):1376).

Complications and recovery potential. Complications include haemophagocytic syndrome (PMC9124500), marrow failure (PMC6170302), bowel obstruction/perforation/infarction (PMC11452572), CNS dissemination (Diagnostics 2026;16:2623), and in secondary disease, evolution of the companion lymphoid malignancy — one BRAF-mutated histiocytic/dendritic cell sarcoma transdifferentiated from FL responded durably to BRAF/MEK inhibition and then evolved to high-grade B-cell lymphoma (PMID 42264920). Recovery potential is good only for completely resected unifocal disease; without effective therapy, disseminated disease is fatal within months.

12. Treatment

There is no randomised evidence in HS. All recommendations below derive from retrospective series, consensus statements and trials in the broader histiocytic-neoplasm population. NCIT identifiers are given only where retrieved.

12.1 Treatment strategy / algorithm

The 2026 international consensus algorithm: surgical resection for unifocal disease, and for multifocal disease targeted therapy or immune checkpoint inhibition when actionable mutations or PD-L1 expression are present (Blood 2026;148(3):275–288, PMID 41915766). NCCN (Histiocytic Neoplasms v2.2021) positions systemic chemotherapy as primary therapy for HS, specifically for multifocal/multi-organ, recurrent or refractory disease, with watch-and-wait an option in asymptomatic cases, and lists genotype-matched options as clinically indicated — including selpercatinib for RET rearrangements, pexidartinib for activating CSF1R mutations, and larotrectinib/entrectinib for NTRK fusions (JNCCN 2021;19(11), PMID 34781268; NCCN guideline PDF). The French national series concluded that "prospective follow-up and a standardized treatment approach in specialized reference centers are crucial to improving patient survival" (quoted via retrieved extraction; Blood Adv 2025;9(10):2530).

12.2 Conventional pharmacotherapy (cytotoxic)

Most-used regimens are CHOP (cyclophosphamide, doxorubicin, vincristine, prednisone) and ICE (ifosfamide, carboplatin/cisplatin, etoposide), with ABVD also reported; "the two most commonly utilized regimens are CHOP or ICE though response rates are poor", and relapse after CHOP is frequent (PMC9271260; Front Oncol 2025; Int J Mol Sci 2025). In pediatric malignant histiocytosis, topotecan + temozolomide produced CR in 2 of 7 treated children (28%) in the French series (Blood Adv 2025).

12.3 Targeted therapy (the highest-yield modality)

  • MEK inhibitors. Cobimetinib (NCIT C68923; MEK-inhibitor class NCIT C69145) was FDA-approved on 28 October 2022 for adult patients with histiocytic neoplasms — the first approval for this disease family — on the basis of the MSK phase 2 single-agent trial (NCT02649972), in which the overall response rate was 89% (90% CI 73–100%) by PET with 13 CR and 3 PR among 18 patients; at one year 100% of responses were ongoing and 94% remained progression-free, with responses across ARAF, BRAF, RAF1, NRAS, KRAS, MEK1 and MEK2 genotypes (Nature 2019, PMID 30867592; MSK news release; ASCO Post; ASH 2023 phase 2 report). Note: that trial population was dominated by other histiocytoses; HS-specific efficacy rests on case-level evidence.
  • Trametinib (NCIT C77908; EVS Explore) in HS: rapid, durable complete response >2 years with a MAP2K1 F53L mutation; near-complete response with somatic KRAS Q61H (ResearchGate record of the MAP2K1 case; JNCCN 2022;20(6), doi:10.6004/jnccn.2022.7001); multifocal HS responding to trametinib + imatinib but stopped at 5 months for grade 3 toxicity (Haematologica); a PTPN11-variant case achieved only partial response with progression at 5 months (Int J Mol Sci 2025).
  • Cobimetinib in HS specifically: clinical response reported in an NF1-mutated HS (PMID 30626916); switching from poorly tolerated/partially effective trametinib to cobimetinib produced a dramatic response with the patient disease-free 3 years later (J Hematol).
  • Luvometinib (FCN-159), a selective MEK1/2 inhibitor, in a single-arm multicentre phase 2 in histiocytic neoplasms (29 evaluable; 22 LCH, 3 ECD, 4 other subtypes): confirmed ORR 61.5%, all 15 PET-evaluated patients achieving a metabolic response (80% complete metabolic response), ORR 94.4% in BRAF-altered and 42.9% in MAP2K1-mutant disease (eClinicalMedicine 2025, PMID 41035796 / PMC12481021).
  • BRAF inhibitors (vemurafenib, dabrafenib) for BRAF V600E disease, including combined BRAF/MEK inhibition with prolonged response in secondary BRAF-mutated histiocytic/dendritic cell sarcoma arising from FL (Int J Mol Sci 2025; PMID 42264920).
  • ERK inhibition for MEK-inhibitor resistance: five patients with MEK1 E102_I103del (four MEK-inhibitor-refractory) were treated with ulixertinib with response; a focused phase 2 of ulixertinib in adults with histiocytoses is planned (Cancer Cell 2025, PMID 41135521; Cancer Discov highlight).
  • CSF1R inhibition: dramatic, lasting response to CSF1R inhibition in a histiocytosis patient with acquired CBL mutations refractory to MEK1/2 inhibition, broadening CSF1R inhibition beyond CSF1R-mutant disease (Leukemia 2023, PMC10400417); pexidartinib is the NCCN-listed CSF1R option (PMID 34781268). Non-MAPK targets in histiocytic disorders are reviewed in JCO Precision Oncology 2025.
  • Selumetinib produced effective disease control in HS evolving from B-ALL (PMID 41798885).

12.4 Immunotherapy

  • Pembrolizumab gave a durable long-term response in widespread PD-L1-high HS — reported as the first such documented case (PMID 38098600 / PMC10718375).
  • PD-L1 expression in ~50% of HS; in a combined patient + literature ICI cohort (N=20), responders had higher median PD-L1 than non-responders (88% vs 30%), and loss of PD-L1 was found at a resistant site (Leuk Lymphoma 2025, doi:10.1080/10428194.2025.2561114).
  • Pembrolizumab + GDP chemotherapy induced sustained remission in a case report (PMC11366101); immunotherapy benefit also reported in PD-L1-high primary pulmonary HS (Clin Respir J 2024, doi:10.1111/crj.13741); long-term remission with combined immune-targeted treatment in HS accompanying FL (Int J Mol Sci 2024;25:7293).

12.5 Cell therapy / transplantation

  • Autologous HSCT has been used as consolidation after anthracycline-based induction (PMID 41580890 / PMC12853904; PMC9271260).
  • Allogeneic HSCT is recommended in refractory/relapsed disease: a PRISMA systematic review (99 studies, adults ≥18 years) of allo-HSCT for HS reported 10 transplanted patients (5 allogeneic, 5 consolidative autologous); among 4 allo-SCT patients, 3 achieved CR and 2 maintained long-term remission (Transplant Cell Ther 2025; ASH 2024 abstract, doi:10.1182/blood-2024-208929; durable response case: Hematol Rep 2026, doi:10.3390/hematolrep18010002). Reduced-intensity allo-SCT has also been used for mediastinal-GCT-associated histiocytic proliferations (PMC4718299).
  • CAR-T, gene therapy, RNA-based therapy: no HS-specific evidence retrieved.

12.6 Surgery, radiotherapy, supportive care

  • Surgery: complete resection is the treatment of choice for unifocal disease, with the best survival in retrospective comparison (median OS 42 months for surgically treated vs 12 months for chemotherapy-treated patients) (PMC9271260; PMID 41915766).
  • Radiotherapy: effective for localised lesions and as adjuvant therapy to reduce recurrence; successful CNS HS management with craniectomy plus adjuvant radiotherapy is documented (PMC9271260; PMC9162887).
  • Supportive/rehabilitative care: no HS-specific guideline retrieved; palliative care use in disseminated disease is documented in the case literature (palliative home care in secondary HS).

12.8 Response rates, adverse events, pharmacogenomics

Response rates are summarised above (chemotherapy: poor and non-durable; MEK inhibition: 89% ORR in the approval trial population, 61.5% with luvometinib; BRAF/MEK combinations and ICI: case-level durable responses). Toxicity signals retrieved: grade 3 adverse events forcing discontinuation of trametinib+imatinib at 5 months, and poor tolerance of trametinib prompting a switch to cobimetinib (Haematologica; J Hematol). No HS-specific pharmacogenomic (PharmGKB/CPIC) guidance was retrieved; the actionable "pharmacogenomics" here is somatic genotype-guided therapy (BRAF V600E → BRAF±MEK inhibitor; MAP2K1/KRAS/NRAS/NF1 → MEK inhibitor; class III MEK1/2 → ERK inhibitor; CBL → CSF1R inhibitor; high PD-L1 → checkpoint inhibitor).

NCIT suggestions (verified in session): cobimetinib NCIT:C68923; trametinib NCIT:C77908; MEK-inhibitor class NCIT:C69145. NCIT codes for CHOP, ICE, allogeneic HSCT, radiotherapy and pembrolizumab exist but were not verified in this session and are therefore not asserted.

13. Prevention

  • Primary prevention: none available or established. There is no known modifiable exposure, no vaccine-preventable cause, and no germline risk to counsel about. This follows directly from §2.2/§5, where no environmental or lifestyle risk factor was retrieved.
  • Secondary prevention / early detection: no population screening exists and none is justified at an incidence of ~0.17/1,000,000 (Blood 2018). The actionable early-detection practice is clinical and pathological vigilance in high-risk clinical contexts: new mass, cytopenias or FDG-avid lesions in a patient with FL, CLL/SLL, MCL, B-ALL, myeloma or Hodgkin lymphoma, or after a mediastinal germ cell tumour, should prompt biopsy with histiocytic-marker IHC and clonality testing (Mod Pathol 2021; PMID 41915766; PMID 33786289). Transplant recipients with unexplained masses are a second such context (PMC10546704).
  • Tertiary prevention (preventing complications in diagnosed patients): complete resection ± radiotherapy of unifocal disease to prevent dissemination; bone marrow evaluation when counts are abnormal to detect concomitant haematopoietic malignancy; broad molecular profiling at diagnosis so that resistance-relevant genotypes (class III MEK1/2, CBL) are known before committing to MEK inhibition; recognition and management of haemophagocytic syndrome (PMID 41915766; PMC9271260; PMID 41135521; PMC9124500).
  • Immunization, genetic screening, PGD/prenatal testing, carrier screening, genetic counselling, risk stratification models, behavioural interventions, public-health or environmental interventions, prophylactic medication: not applicable — no germline basis, no infectious cause, no environmental cause, and no validated risk model for HS was retrieved.

14. Other Species / Natural Disease

Taxonomy and veterinary importance. The domestic dog (Canis lupus familiaris, NCBI taxon 9615, as encoded in the OMIA record identifier) has a naturally occurring, clinically and molecularly homologous disease: OMIA:000620-9615 "Histiocytosis, malignant" (OMIA entry). It is a major veterinary problem, not a curiosity: canine histiocytic sarcoma affects 15–25% of Bernese Mountain Dogs, with disseminated disease accounting for up to ~25% of deaths in the breed (Breed-predispositions to cancer in pedigree dogs, PMC3658424; Epidemiology, pathology, and genetics of histiocytic sarcoma in the Bernese mountain dog breed, PMID 19531730).

Breeds (VBO identifiers not retrieved; breed names as recorded). Bernese Mountain Dog, Flat-Coated Retriever, Rottweiler, Golden Retriever and Miniature Schnauzer are recorded in OMIA, with sporadic cases in many breeds (OMIA:000620-9615; UC Davis histiocytosis program; NC State canine HS page). Clinical phenotypes differ between predisposed breeds (Comparison of the clinical characteristics of histiocytic sarcoma in Bernese Mountain Dogs and Flat-Coated Retrievers, PMC9504151).

Genes and orthologues (gene symbols; NCBI Gene IDs not retrieved). - Germline susceptibility: the MTAP–CDKN2A locus confers susceptibility to canine HS — the first GWAS for histiocytic sarcoma in any species — with evidence that risk-haplotype variants affect CDKN2A/CDKN2B expression in susceptible dogs (Cancer Epidemiol Biomarkers Prev 2012;21:1019). A four-breed study confirmed the main role of the CDKN2A locus and identified four additional predisposing loci; an oligogenic mode of transmission is proposed, with no single Mendelian mode established (PMC8016107; OMIA:000620-9615). - Somatic drivers shared with humans: activating PTPN11 and KRAS mutations in canine histiocytic sarcoma, and PTPN11 mutations described jointly in canine and human disseminated HS (PMC6678586; ResearchGate record: PTPN11 mutations in canine and human disseminated histiocytic sarcoma). - Pathway concordance: canine whole-exome + transcriptome analysis demonstrates ERK and Akt pathway activation, mirroring the human MAPK + PI3K architecture (Sci Rep 2023, PMC10212919).

Comparative pathology and evolutionary conservation. Canine localised and disseminated histiocytic sarcomas are considered to be of myeloid dendritic cell origin, a notable difference from the mature-macrophage phenotype emphasised in human HS (Wikipedia: Canine histiocytic diseases; Canine histiocytic malignancies — challenges and opportunities, PMC5644619). Shared features: aggressive multi-organ disease, poor prognosis, MAPK/PI3K dependence, and cell-cycle-driven transcriptomes. Canine therapy response: lomustine (CCNU) at 60–90 mg/m² gave a 46% overall response rate in 56 dogs with gross measurable disease, with median survival of 106 days across 59 dogs; thrombocytopenia (<100,000/µL) and hypoalbuminaemia predicted <1-month survival (Skorupski et al., PMID 17338159; doi:10.1111/j.1939-1676.2007.tb02937.x); lomustine+doxorubicin combinations have also been evaluated (ResearchGate record). Risk-factor epidemiology within the breed has been studied formally (J Vet Intern Med, doi:10.1111/jvim.13964).

Other species. Histiocytic sarcoma occurs naturally in laboratory mice (see §15) and has been reported in a degu (Octodon degus) (PMID 38556322 / PMC11144536) and an Amami spiny rat (Tokudaia osimensis) (PMC11442397).

Transmission. No zoonotic potential and no cross-species transmissibility — HS is a non-infectious clonal neoplasm; cross-species relevance is comparative/translational only.

15. Model Organisms

Mouse — spontaneous and background disease. Histiocytic sarcoma is a well-recognised spontaneous neoplasm of laboratory mice; Janke & Rehg catalogued its morphological range in C57BL/6J mice, noting that spindle cells and small round cells co-occur, often within the same tissue, in ~80% of cases (Vet Pathol 2023;60(4):443–460, doi:10.1177/03009858231166658). Additional spontaneous reports: epididymal HS in a CD-1 mouse (PMC11219191); concurrent B-lymphoblastic lymphoma and HS in a C57BL/6 mouse (PMC12906819); abdominal distension in an aged female mouse (PMID 40783184). Background incidence in aging mice and GEM lines is reviewed by Brayton, Treuting & Ward (Vet Pathol 2012). Implication for model use: spontaneous murine HS is a confounder in aging/toxicology studies as well as a resource.

Mouse — genetic models. - Conditional knock-in of the human class III mutation MEK1 E102_I103del drives a lethal histiocytic-like neoplasm, sensitive to the ERK1/2 inhibitor ulixertinib — the most mechanistically faithful model retrieved, since it reproduces both disease and the clinically observed drug-resistance/sensitivity pattern (Cancer Cell 2025, PMID 41135521). - Forward-genetic (Sleeping Beauty transposon) screen with myeloid-restricted mutagenesis via the Lysozyme2 promoter: mice had significantly shortened lifespan and most developed tumours resembling human HS; 27 common insertion sites yielded 28 candidate cancer genes, including Raf1, Fli1, Mitf, Nf1, Myc, Jak2 and Pten (PLoS ONE 2014;9(5):e97280; PMC4020815). - Elevated HS incidence in engineered lines: Ing2, p21^Waf1/Cip1/Sdi1^, Trp53-family mutants, and Dok1/Dok2/Dok3 triple-deficient mice; the field explicitly notes that GEMs carrying the common human HS drivers (e.g. BRAF^V600E^) would be valuable for therapeutic testing (Int J Mol Sci 2025 review). Nonsense-mutant p53 mice develop frequent multicentric/metastatic tumours including histiocytic lesions (bioRxiv 2025). - Inflammation-associated, age-dependent model: CD4Cre-driven SHIP1 (Inpp5d) deletion produces spontaneous widely disseminated histiocytic sarcoma correlating with age, "in the absence of engineered oncogenic drivers" (PMC13627489). - Induced model: histiocytic sarcoma can be induced in mouse skeletal muscle (PLoS ONE 2012;7:e44044 / PMC3432091).

Cell lines / in vitro. A significant limitation: a canine-model paper states that no human histiocytic sarcoma cell line is available for research, which is why the human U-937 histiocytic lymphoma line (CVCL_0007, Cellosaurus) and animal lines are used as surrogates (A novel canine histiocytic sarcoma cell line, PMC5831740). Available models include canine CHS-2 (CVCL_L315, Shetland Sheepdog, skin; Cellosaurus), PWC-HS01 (CVCL_IU62, Pembroke Welsh Corgi, brain; Cellosaurus), DH82, and the murine M5076 (CVCL_3997, ovarian histiocytic sarcoma; Cellosaurus), HS-MM (CVCL_W472) and J774A.1, the latter used as a direct analogue of human HS in vitro (doi:10.3390/ijms27114949). A permanent human dendritic sarcoma line (U-DCS) exists for the related dendritic-cell tumours (PMC7718904). No HS organoid or iPSC model was retrieved.

Phenotype recapitulation and limitations. - Recapitulated: macrophage/histiocyte-lineage tumours with multi-organ dissemination; MAPK dependence and MEK/ERK-inhibitor pharmacology (MEK1-mutant knock-in); cell-cycle-driven transcriptional programmes and AURK-inhibitor/trametinib synergy (canine lines) (PMID 41135521; Cancers 2025, PMC11940154). - Not recapitulated: the human secondary/transdifferentiation route from a lymphoid clone; the human extranodal site distribution; germline susceptibility (modelled only in dogs); and human immune-microenvironment features such as PD-L1-dependent checkpoint response. The absence of a human HS cell line is the single biggest resource gap (PMC5831740).

Applications. Driver-gene discovery (transposon screen), causal proof of MEK-mutant pathogenicity and resistance biology (knock-in mouse), drug screening and combination discovery (canine cell lines ± trametinib and aurora-kinase inhibitors), and comparative-oncology trials in dogs as a bridge to human trials (PLoS ONE 2014; PMID 41135521; PMC11940154; PMC5644619).

Model resources. OMIA (canine entry above); Cellosaurus (cell lines above). MGI/IMPC/IMSR/MMRRC-specific allele records for HS-relevant lines were not retrieved in this session and should be looked up directly when populating the knowledge base.
