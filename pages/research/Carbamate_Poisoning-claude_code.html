Carbamate Poisoning

1. Disease Information

2026-10-28
Claude Code Model: claude-haiku-5-5, claude-opus-4-8, claude-opus-5-5 22 citations Prompt: disease_pathophysiology_research.md f452f0a9c7e3

1. Disease Information

Overview. Carbamate poisoning is an acute cholinergic toxidrome caused by exposure (ingestion, inhalation, or dermal) to N-methyl carbamate insecticides. These compounds reversibly carbamylate acetylcholinesterase (AChE) at cholinergic synapses, causing acetylcholine to accumulate at muscarinic receptors, nicotinic receptors (neuromuscular junction and autonomic ganglia), and in the CNS. The clinical picture is essentially indistinguishable at the bedside from organophosphate (OP) poisoning, but the enzyme–inhibitor bond is spontaneously hydrolyzed (decarbamylation) and, critically, does not "age," so recovery of enzyme function is generally faster and the classic delayed syndromes are much less prominent (AJEM review; WikEM: Carbamates; StatPearls).

Key identifiers. - ICD-10-CM: T60.0 — Toxic effect of organophosphate and carbamate insecticides (carbamates share the code with OPs) (AAPC T60.0; gesund.bund.de T60). - ICD-11: NE61 Harmful effects of or exposure to noxious substances, chemical category / 9D00-range for organophosphate & carbamate — verify against the live ICD-11 browser; carbamates are coded with OPs there as well. - MeSH: No single descriptor for "carbamate poisoning." Relevant descriptors are Carbamates (class) and specific agents, e.g., Carbofuran D002235 (CAS 1563-66-2; UNII SKF77S6Y67; UMLS C0006995) (NLM MeSH D002235; CISMeF). The umbrella descriptor Cholinesterase Inhibitors (D002800) is also relevant. - MONDO: No dedicated MONDO term for carbamate insecticide poisoning exists at the time of writing — an OLS search of MONDO returns only unrelated carbamoyl/carbamazepine entries ([OLS MONDO query]). This is an OUT_OF_SCOPE-vs-curate consideration for the queue; if curated, it would likely anchor on a broad chemical-poisoning MONDO parent with skos:narrowMatch, or motivate a new-term request. - ECTO: ECTO:9000282 exposure to carbamate — the directly usable exposure term for the environmental: block. - Orphanet: No specific ORPHA code (acute toxic exposure, not a rare disease).

Data provenance. Information is from aggregated disease-level clinical toxicology literature (prospective cohorts, reviews, poison-center references), not individual EHR records.

Synonyms: carbamate insecticide poisoning; carbamate intoxication; carbamate toxicosis; anticholinesterase carbamate poisoning; (agent-specific) aldicarb/carbofuran/carbaryl/methomyl/propoxur poisoning.

2. Etiology

Primary cause — environmental/toxicological (not genetic). Exposure to an N-methyl carbamate insecticide. Common agents and their approximate human relevance: - Aldicarb (Temik; highly toxic, WHO Class Ia) — notorious in malicious/deliberate poisonings ("Tres Pasitos"), CHEBI:38511. - Carbofuran (Furadan; WHO Ib) — a leading agent in South Asian self-poisoning; CHEBI:34611, CAS 1563-66-2. - Carbosulfan (a carbofuran pro-insecticide, WHO II) — high human case fatality despite lower hazard class. - Fenobucarb (BPMC) — liquid formulation, South/Southeast Asia. - Methomyl (Lannate; WHO Ib) — CHEBI:34848. - Carbaryl (Sevin; WHO II) — CHEBI:3390, the prototypical lower-toxicity carbamate. - Propoxur (Baygon) — household insecticide; frequent in accidental/pediatric and some deliberate exposures (Baygon case overview). - Methiocarb, bendiocarb, oxamyl, pirimicarb — other agrochemical carbamates.

(Note: carbaryl CHEBI, methomyl CHEBI etc. should be re-verified in CHEBI before binding.)

Routes of exposure / risk contexts (environmental risk factors). - Deliberate self-poisoning (suicide) — the dominant cause of severe cases and deaths in low- and middle-income agricultural regions (South Asia, Southeast Asia) (Sri Lanka carbamate cohort, PMC4950420). - Occupational — agricultural applicators, formulators, re-entry workers (dermal/inhalational). - Accidental — children ingesting household products (e.g., propoxur baits); contaminated food. - Homicidal/malicious — aldicarb in particular.

Risk factors: rural agricultural residence; ready access to concentrated liquid formulations; absence of regulatory bans; psychiatric illness/acute distress (for self-poisoning); occupational handling without PPE.

Protective factors (population/policy level): national pesticide regulation and bans on the most lethal agents — a well-documented structural intervention. Sri Lankan pesticide regulation markedly reduced acute poisoning deaths (Influence of pesticide regulation on poisoning deaths, archive.cmb.ac.lk; Lancet Glob Health 2021). PPE, safe storage, and dilution of formulations are secondary protective measures.

Gene–environment interaction. The pharmacologically relevant host-variation axis is not a disease-causing variant but detoxification enzyme polymorphism: paraoxonase-1 (PON1) and butyrylcholinesterase (BCHE) variants modulate response to anticholinesterase pesticides. PON1 is classically tied to OP (oxon) hydrolysis more than carbamates; BCHE activity affects scavenging capacity. These are modifiers of susceptibility/severity, not causal. (Evidence base is stronger for OPs than carbamates — flag as INDIRECT/uncertain for carbamate-specific claims.)

3. Phenotypes

The toxidrome is acute-onset (minutes to ~2 hours post-exposure), generally transient (hours to a few days as AChE spontaneously reactivates), and dominated by cholinergic excess. Mnemonics: muscarinic DUMBELS (Defecation, Urination, Miosis, Bronchorrhea/Bronchospasm/Bradycardia, Emesis, Lacrimation, Salivation) and SLUDGE/BBB (Medscape; StatPearls).

Muscarinic (clinical signs/autonomic): - Miosis — HP:0000545 (Myopia is different; pinpoint pupils → HP:0030810 Miosis) - Hypersalivation — HP:0002307 (Drooling) / sialorrhea - Bronchorrhea & bronchospasm → respiratory distress — HP:0002094 Dyspnea, HP:0002104 Apnea; excessive airway secretions (no precise HP; coarse) - Lacrimation — HP:0009926 (Increased lacrimation / Epiphora) - Diarrhea — HP:0002014; vomiting — HP:0002013 - Urinary incontinence — HP:0000020 - Bradycardia — HP:0001662; hypotension — HP:0002615 - Sweating/diaphoresis — HP:0000975 (Hyperhidrosis)

Nicotinic (neuromuscular / ganglionic): - Muscle fasciculations — HP:0002380 (Fasciculations) - Muscle weakness — HP:0001324; progressing to flaccid paralysis and respiratory muscle failure (the main proximate cause of death) - Tachycardia/hypertension (ganglionic, early) — HP:0001649 / HP:0000822

CNS: - Altered consciousness, drowsiness, coma — HP:0001259 (Coma), HP:0001250 (Seizure) - Confusion/agitation — HP:0001289 - Seizures (especially severe/pediatric) - Note: Carbamates cross the blood–brain barrier less readily than many OPs (e.g., carbaryl), so CNS effects may be relatively less prominent for some agents — but highly lipophilic/potent carbamates (aldicarb, carbofuran) produce profound CNS depression and seizures.

Laboratory abnormalities: - Depressed red-cell (true/AChE) and plasma (butyryl-/pseudo-) cholinesterase activity — the key biomarker, but spontaneous in-vitro reactivation of carbamylated enzyme can cause falsely normal or rapidly recovering levels, making ChE a less reliable confirmatory test than in OP poisoning. LOINC: RBC acetylcholinesterase e.g. LOINC:32460-1 (verify); serum/plasma cholinesterase LOINC:2098-2 (Cholinesterase, verify exact code and Long Common Name before binding). - Hyperglycemia, leukocytosis, metabolic/respiratory acidosis, hypokalemia (variable).

Onset/severity/progression/frequency. Onset acute (minutes–2 h). Severity ranges mild→fatal; dose- and agent-dependent. Course episodic/self-limited — most survivors recover within 24–48 h as enzyme reactivates, shorter than OP poisoning; carbamates "do not lead to chronic toxicity or delayed syndromes" in the classic teaching because the bond hydrolyzes spontaneously (StatPearls). Frequency of individual signs is not well-quantified per-agent in the literature; bronchorrhea/respiratory failure drives mortality.

Quality-of-life impact. For survivors, usually full recovery with no chronic sequelae (contrast OP, which can leave intermediate syndrome / OPIDN / chronic neuropsychiatric effects). The major burden is acute mortality in deliberate poisoning rather than chronic disability.

6. Mechanism / Pathophysiology

Ordered causal chain

  1. Exposure (ingestion / dermal / inhalation) of an N-methyl carbamate leads to systemic absorption and distribution of the parent carbamate (and, for pro-insecticides like carbosulfan, bioactivation to the active carbamate).
  2. Circulating carbamate reaches cholinergic synapses and the neuromuscular junction and results in carbamylation of the catalytic serine hydroxyl of acetylcholinesterase (and butyrylcholinesterase), forming a carbamyl–enzyme conjugate — a reversible covalent modification. (Demonstrated biochemically.)
  3. Carbamylated AChE cannot hydrolyze acetylcholine, which leads to accumulation of acetylcholine in the synaptic cleft at muscarinic receptors, nicotinic receptors, and CNS synapses.
  4. Excess ACh at muscarinic receptors leads to the parasympathetic/exocrine signs (miosis, bronchorrhea, bronchospasm, bradycardia, salivation, lacrimation, GI hypermotility, urinary signs).
  5. Excess ACh at nicotinic receptors (NMJ/ganglia) leads to fasciculations → depolarizing blockade → muscle weakness → flaccid paralysis of respiratory muscles.
  6. Excess ACh in the CNS leads to agitation, seizures, and central respiratory depression.
  7. Branch point — divergence from OP poisoning: the carbamyl–enzyme bond undergoes spontaneous hydrolysis (decarbamylation) and, crucially, does not undergo "aging" (irreversible dealkylation). This leads to spontaneous reactivation of AChE over hours and results in a shorter, usually self-limited course, with the classic OP delayed syndromes (intermediate syndrome, OPIDN) being largely absent or rare. (Aging is OP-specific: USPTO patent background; toxicology study guide.) Caveat/inferred: the speed of spontaneous reactivation is disputed — lay sources say "rapid," but primary data (e.g., Reiner's reactivation-rate tabulations, PMC2428035; and a rivastigmine–enzyme study showing <10% Torpedo-enzyme reactivation at 48 h, Proteopedia 1gqs) show it is enzyme- and compound-dependent and can be slow. This nuance underlies the oxime controversy (§12).
  8. Proximate cause of death: respiratory failure (bronchorrhea + bronchospasm + respiratory-muscle paralysis + central depression), compounded by aspiration and, less commonly, early cardiac events (Eddleston treatment protocol, PMC1065055).

7. Anatomical Structures Affected

  • Body systems: autonomic nervous system; respiratory; neuromuscular; cardiovascular; GI; CNS.
  • Primary organs: nervous system / neuromuscular junction (functional target); lungs/airways (UBERON:0002048 lung, bronchi UBERON:0002185) — bronchorrhea/bronchospasm; eye (pupil, UBERON:0001771 pupil → miosis); exocrine glands (salivary UBERON:0001044, lacrimal UBERON:0001817).
  • Secondary: heart (UBERON:0000948) — brady/tachyarrhythmia; brain (UBERON:0000955) — seizures, central respiratory depression; skeletal muscle (UBERON:0001134), diaphragm (UBERON:0001103) — paralysis.
  • Tissue/cell level: neuromuscular junction (UBERON:0002375 muscle tissue / GO:0031594 neuromuscular junction); cholinergic synapses.
  • Subcellular: synaptic cleft; AChE is anchored at the synaptic basal lamina / cell membrane (GO:0045202 synapse, GO:0005605 basal lamina).
  • Localization: systemic/bilateral (miosis bilateral; weakness generalized).

8. Temporal Development

  • Onset: acute, minutes to ~2 h after exposure (faster with ingestion of concentrated liquids).
  • Pattern: acute toxidrome; self-limited in survivors.
  • Progression: rapid peak; recovery typically within 24 h and usually complete within 24–48 h as AChE reactivates — notably shorter than OP poisoning.
  • Critical period / intervention window: the first hours — airway protection and atropine titration are life-saving in this window; death is early (respiratory, occasionally cardiac).
  • Delayed syndromes: classically absent — carbamates generally do not cause the OP intermediate syndrome (24–96 h muscle paralysis) or OP-induced delayed polyneuropathy (OPIDN, 2–3 weeks) (CDC ATSDR intermediate syndrome material). Exceptions/uncertainty: rare suspected intermediate-syndrome-like presentations are reported, chiefly in veterinary cases (methiocarb and aldicarb in dogs — Tinson 2017 Aust Vet J; Klainbart et al. anticholinesterase dog review) and isolated human reports; curate these as a HUMAN_MODEL_MISMATCH/KNOWLEDGE_GAP discussion rather than an established feature.

9. Inheritance and Population

Inheritance: not applicable (acquired intoxication — no inheritance pattern, penetrance, expressivity, anticipation, mosaicism, founder effect, consanguinity, or carrier frequency).

Epidemiology. - Carbamates are a major component of the global pesticide self-poisoning burden, concentrated in rural Asia. WHO-era estimates put pesticide self-poisoning case fatality in the developing world at 10–20%, up to 50–70% for the most toxic agents (Eddleston protocol, PMC1065055). - Prospective carbamate cohort (Sri Lanka, 6 hospitals; Clinical Toxicology 2016;54:624–631, DOI 10.1080/15563650.2016.1187735; PMC4950420): n = 1,288 carbamate self-poisonings (carbofuran powder n=719; carbosulfan EC25 n=389; fenobucarb EC50 n=127). 183 (14.2%) required intubation/ventilation; 71 (5.5%) died. Case fatality by agent: carbofuran ≈2.2%, fenobucarb ≈6.3% (OR ~3.0 vs carbofuran), carbosulfan ≈11.1% (OR ~5.5) — higher mortality with the high-strength liquid formulations despite lower WHO hazard class (PMC4950420). Reduced GCS at presentation predicted worse outcome. - Atropine safety cohort (Konickx, Eddleston et al.; OP + carbamate, n=1,957): early atropine given before oxygen — primary cardiac death within 3 h of atropine in only 4/1,957 (0.2%); most deaths were later respiratory ([search summary; confirm PMID before citing]). - Lancet Global Health 2021: since 2013 the five deadliest agents in Sri Lanka (including fenobucarb and carbosulfan) had case fatality 7.2–8.6% (DOAJ).

Demographics: predominantly working-age adults in deliberate self-poisoning (regional sex ratios vary); children in accidental household exposures. Geographic: South/Southeast Asia, sub-Saharan Africa, agricultural LMICs.

Prevalence/incidence as a formal disease rate is not well defined; occurrence tracks pesticide availability and regulation. For a Prevalence block, use measure_type: CASES_IN_LITERATURE or cohort-derived figures with explicit denominators rather than a population prevalence.

10. Diagnostics

  • Primarily clinical — recognition of the cholinergic toxidrome (DUMBELS/SLUDGE + miosis + bronchorrhea + fasciculations/weakness) plus exposure history. Treatment must not wait for labs (StatPearls).
  • Cholinesterase assays: depressed RBC acetylcholinesterase and plasma/pseudocholinesterase. Important caveat: carbamylated enzyme spontaneously reactivates (including in the sample tube), so ChE levels may be only transiently low or falsely normal — less reliable than for OPs, and a normal level does not exclude carbamate poisoning. (LOINC codes to verify: Cholinesterase in serum/plasma; Acetylcholinesterase in RBC.)
  • Differentiation from OP: clinically similar; the distinction matters mainly for oxime decision-making and prognosis (shorter course, no aging). History of the specific product is key.
  • Supportive labs/tests: ABG (respiratory failure/acidosis), glucose, electrolytes (hypokalemia), ECG (brady/tachyarrhythmia, QT), chest imaging (aspiration). Specific agent confirmation by GC-MS/LC-MS on blood/urine/gastric contents is forensic/research, not routine.
  • Genetic testing, newborn/carrier screening, CMA, karyotype, repeat-expansion, mitochondrial testing: all N/A.
  • Differential diagnosis: OP poisoning (primary), nicotine/other cholinergic agents, nerve-agent exposure, other causes of coma with miosis (opioids — but opioids lack fasciculations/bronchorrhea), status epilepticus, severe pneumonia.

11. Outcome / Prognosis

  • Generally good with prompt supportive care — most survivors recover fully within 24–48 h with no chronic sequelae (contrast OP). Overall cohort mortality ~5.5% but strongly agent/formulation-dependent (carbofuran ~2% vs carbosulfan ~11%) (PMC4950420).
  • Proximate cause of death: respiratory failure (secretions, bronchospasm, muscle paralysis, central depression), aspiration; occasionally early cardiac events.
  • Prognostic factors: specific agent and formulation strength; GCS at presentation (lower = worse); dose; time to atropine/airway support; development of respiratory failure requiring ventilation (14.2% in the cohort).
  • Morbidity/disability: low long-term disability in survivors; the burden is acute mortality. QoL instruments not disease-specific.
  • Chronic effects: the chronic neuropsychiatric/neuropathic sequelae described for OPs are largely not features of carbamate poisoning (uncertainty noted in §8 for rare intermediate-syndrome-like reports).

12. Treatment

Mainstay = airway + atropine; decontamination; oximes controversial.

  • Resuscitation/airway (NCIT:C15247 / supportive): "aggressive airway management is of utmost importance" (WikEM); oxygen, suction of secretions, intubation/ventilation for respiratory failure (14.2% of the cohort).
  • Atropine (antimuscarinic; CHEBI:16684; NCIT:C47485 Atropine): the specific antidote — titrated to drying of secretions / clear chest / adequate oxygenation, not to pupils or heart rate alone; large cumulative doses (tens–hundreds of mg) may be needed (Eddleston protocol, PMC1065055). Treatment term: NCIT:C15986 Pharmacotherapy + therapeutic_agent atropine.
  • Oximes (pralidoxime, CHEBI:8352; obidoxime): controversial and generally not recommended for pure carbamate poisoning. Rationale: carbamylated AChE reactivates spontaneously and does not age, so oximes add little; and "unconfirmed reports suggest increased toxicity of carbamates when oximes are administered" (notably historical carbaryl animal data) (AJEM review; Baygon case review). However, when OP co-exposure cannot be excluded, pralidoxime is still given (WikEM); meta-analyses show no clear mortality benefit of adding oxime to atropine. Curate the oxime question as a mechanistic_hypotheses/discussion with conflicting evidence.
  • Benzodiazepines (NCIT:C205 Benzodiazepine): for seizures/agitation (diazepam/lorazepam).
  • Decontamination: remove and bag clothing, skin wash; staff PPE (gloves/gowns) (WikEM). Activated charcoal if early and airway protected (evidence limited).
  • Pharmacogenomics / targeted / gene / cell / RNA / immunotherapy: N/A (no genotype-guided therapy; no advanced therapeutics).
  • Experimental: none established; general pesticide-poisoning trials (e.g., magnesium, lipid emulsion, alkalinization) are investigational and mostly OP-focused.
  • Adverse events: atropine toxicity (anticholinergic delirium, hyperthermia, tachycardia) from over-dosing — a real risk, with warnings against reflexive repeat atropine without indication (Uganda audit: 38.8% of atropine given without a clear indication).

13. Prevention

Primary prevention (the highest-impact, best-evidenced lever): - Regulatory bans / restriction of the most lethal carbamates (aldicarb, carbofuran; carbosulfan bans tracked internationally) — population-level mortality reductions demonstrated in Sri Lanka (Lancet Glob Health 2021; carbosulfan ban tracker). - Safer formulations/packaging, dilution, secure storage, means-restriction for suicide prevention. - Occupational controls: PPE, training, re-entry intervals. - Public health / environmental: central/community pesticide storage; mental-health and suicide-prevention services in agricultural communities.

Secondary/tertiary: rapid access to care and atropine; poison-center protocols. Immunization, genetic/carrier screening, prenatal testing, genetic counseling: N/A.

14. Other Species / Natural Disease

  • Veterinary relevance is high — carbamate toxicosis is common in companion animals (malicious baiting with aldicarb/carbofuran; accidental ingestion) and wildlife (secondary poisoning of raptors/scavengers from carbofuran-laced carcasses — a major conservation issue).
  • Species (NCBITaxon): dog (NCBITaxon:9615), cat (NCBITaxon:9685), and many wild birds/mammals; the molecular target AChE is conserved across vertebrates and insects (the intended target).
  • Natural/veterinary disease: confirmed canine carbofuran/chlorpyrifos poisonings (Czech Vet Med 2003); suspected intermediate syndrome after methiocarb (dog, Tinson 2017) and aldicarb (dogs/cats, São Paulo thesis). These veterinary data are MODEL_ORGANISM evidence and are the main source of the "carbamate intermediate syndrome" signal — treat human extrapolation as a HUMAN_MODEL_MISMATCH.
  • Comparative biology: mechanism (AChE carbamylation) is identical across species; the insecticidal action on insect AChE is the same mechanism exploited therapeutically.
  • Zoonotic/transmission: N/A (toxicological, not infectious).

15. Model Organisms

  • In vivo mammalian: rats and mice are standard for carbamate AChE-inhibition pharmacology/toxicology, antidote (atropine/oxime) studies, and the historical carbaryl+oxime interaction data. Guinea pigs are used for anticholinesterase/antidote work (AChE profile closer to human).
  • Ex vivo / in vitro: purified AChE (human, Torpedo, electric eel) for carbamylation/decarbamylation kinetics (Reiner's reactivation studies, PMC2428035; rivastigmine–AChE crystallography, Proteopedia 1gqs); erythrocyte/neuronal cell assays.
  • Invertebrate / computational: insect AChE (target of action); molecular docking/QSAR of carbamate–AChE binding (COMPUTATIONAL evidence).
  • Model value/limitations: animal models reproduce the acute cholinergic crisis and antidote response well; species differences in spontaneous reactivation rate and BBB penetration limit direct quantitative translation, and the human oxime-harm question is incompletely resolved by models.
  • Resources: MGI (mouse AChE), CTD (carbofuran/aldicarb/carbaryl toxicogenomics), ESTHER database (cholinesterase/carbamate enzymology).
