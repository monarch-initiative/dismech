Lowry-Wood Syndrome (MONDO:0009191): A Comprehensive Disease Characteristics Report

MONDO: MONDO:0009191 · Causal gene: RNU4ATAC (RNA, U4atac small nuclear; HGNC:34140; gene OMIM 601428; chr 2q14.2) · Orphanet: ORPHA:2648 · Category: Mendelian, autosomal recessive

Summary

Lowry-Wood syndrome (LWS; MONDO:0009191) is an ultra-rare, autosomal-recessive skeletal dysplasia caused by biallelic loss-of-function variants in RNU4ATAC, a non-coding, single-exon gene on chromosome 2q14.2 (HGNC:34140; OMIM 601428) that is transcribed into the U4atac small nuclear RNA (snRNA) — a core component of the minor (U12-dependent) spliceosome. Although the disorder's descriptive synonym ("Multiple epiphyseal dysplasia with microcephaly and nystagmus") emphasizes the skeletal and ophthalmologic triad, the causal lesion is not in a cartilage or eye gene but in a housekeeping RNA required for excision of ~700–800 "minor" (U12-type) introns scattered across the genome. LWS represents the mildest end of a clinical spectrum of RNU4ATAC-opathies that also includes Roifman syndrome (RS) and the severe, often perinatally lethal microcephalic osteodysplastic primordial dwarfism type 1 (MOPD1 / Taybi-Linder syndrome).

The defining clinical features of LWS are multiple epiphyseal dysplasia (causing short stature and joint/limb anomalies), microcephaly, developmental delay / intellectual disability (typically mild), and eye involvement (nystagmus and progressive retinal dystrophy). Across the broader RNU4ATAC-opathy spectrum, additional management-relevant features include immune deficiency (antibody deficiency, low B-cell subsets), cerebral anomalies, and chilblain-like skin lesions. Genotype–phenotype analyses show that severity is governed largely by variant position within the U4atac RNA secondary structure: the most severe MOPD1-causing variants cluster in the functionally constrained 5′ stem-loop critical for U4/U6.U5 tri-snRNP assembly, whereas milder LWS and RS variants are more dispersed.

The unifying molecular mechanism is transcriptome-wide retention and mis-splicing of U12-type minor introns, demonstrated by RNA-seq in patient cells and consistent across the spectrum. This minor-intron splicing defect depletes self-amplifying neural progenitors (producing microcephaly), disrupts chondro-osseous development (producing epiphyseal dysplasia and short stature), and secondarily perturbs ciliary function, JAK/STAT signaling, and type I interferon responses (contributing to immune and skin phenotypes). Diagnosis hinges on sequencing the non-coding RNU4ATAC exon — frequently missed by standard exome pipelines — with RNA-seq minor-intron-retention as a functional biomarker that enables variant reclassification. No disease-modifying therapy exists; management is supportive and multidisciplinary, with genetic counseling for the 25% recurrence risk.

Key Findings

Finding 1 — LWS is caused by biallelic variants in the non-coding minor-spliceosome gene RNU4ATAC

Exome sequencing across multiple families established that LWS results from biallelic (homozygous or compound heterozygous) pathogenic variants in RNU4ATAC, which transcribes the U4atac snRNA of the minor (U12-dependent) spliceosome. Farach et al. (2018) first established the genetic cause, describing "three patients with Lowry Wood syndrome with biallelic RNU4ATAC pathogenic variants" and stating that their report "is the first to establish the genetic cause for Lowry Wood syndrome" (PMID: 29265708). Shelihan et al. (2018) confirmed the association, noting that "Pathogenic variants in RNU4ATAC, an RNA of the minor spliceosome important for the excision of U12-dependent introns, have been recently associated with LWS" (PMID: 30368667). RNU4ATAC has a single non-coding exon on chromosome 2q14.2. This is a human clinical/genetic finding.

Finding 2 — RNU4ATAC disorders form a severity spectrum governed by variant location, with LWS the mildest

The RNU4ATAC-opathies constitute a continuum of severity from MOPD1 (most severe, frequently lethal) through Roifman syndrome to Lowry-Wood syndrome (mildest). Shelihan et al. (2018) analyzed all reported variants and found that "FitCons scores for nucleotides mutated in the more severe MOPD are higher than RS or LWS and that they were more frequently located in the 5' Stem-Loop of the RNA critical for the formation of the U4/U6.U5 tri-snRNP complex, whereas the variants are more dispersed in the other conditions" (PMID: 30368667). A recent integrated reevaluation confirms the spectrum "whose severity ranges from the microcephalic osteodysplastic primordial dwarfism type 1 (MOPD1) to the milder Roifman syndrome" (PMID: 41808109). Clinically the categories overlap substantially; one reported individual had "a novel genotype and phenotype spanning all three disorders" (PMID: 36622817), supporting the view of a single phenotypic continuum rather than discrete diseases.

Finding 3 — Core clinical phenotype: multiple epiphyseal dysplasia, microcephaly, short stature, developmental delay, and eye involvement

LWS is defined as "a skeletal dysplasia characterized by multiple epiphyseal dysplasia associated with microcephaly, developmental delay and intellectual disability, and eye involvement" (PMID: 30368667). In the largest RNU4ATAC-opathy cohort reported to date (n = 60), "Core features in this cohort—present in most individuals assessed and varying in severity—include microcephaly, short stature, skeletal anomalies, developmental delay, cerebral anomalies, skin conditions, and immune deficiency" (PMID: 42322193). Eye involvement in LWS prominently includes nystagmus (reflected in the descriptive synonym) and progressive retinal dystrophy.

Finding 4 — Mechanism: defective U4atac impairs the minor spliceosome, causing transcriptome-wide minor (U12-type) intron retention

The U4atac snRNA is "a minor spliceosome component that is essential for minor intron splicing" (PMID: 26522830). The minor spliceosome excises ~700–800 U12-dependent introns (~0.5–1% of all human introns) found in a functionally diverse set of genes. Pathogenic RNU4ATAC variants impair this machinery: RNA-seq of patient cells "confirms extensive and specific defects of minor intron splicing" (PMID: 26522830), and across the large cohort "all individuals who underwent RNA-seq showed a consistent pattern of minor intron retention" (PMID: 42322193). This minor-intron retention is the shared, upstream molecular consequence across the entire RNU4ATAC-opathy spectrum and provides a functional readout usable for variant reclassification.

Finding 5 — Secondary mechanisms: ciliary dysfunction, dysregulated JAK/STAT signaling, and type I interferon activation

Beyond the primary splicing defect, several downstream pathways are perturbed. (1) Ciliary dysfunction: U4atac depletion secondarily produces ciliary defects — "Deficiency of the minor spliceosome component U4atac snRNA secondarily results in ciliary defects in human and zebrafish" (PMID: 36802443) — linking minor-spliceosome deficiency to ciliopathy-like features. (2) JAK/STAT signaling: A Drosophila U4atac mutant shows reduced splicing of the JAK homolog hopscotch; "our work identifies alterations of Jak signaling as part of the pathogenesis of RNU4atac-opathy" (PMID: 42463491), implicating JAK/STAT in hematopoietic/immune pathogenesis. (3) Type I interferon: RNU4ATAC patients commonly develop painful chilblain-like skin lesions; "painful chilblain-like lesions are common in RNU4ATAC patients and are linked to dysregulated type I interferon signalling" (PMID: 40415209).

Finding 6 — Immunodeficiency and progressive retinal dystrophy are recurrent, management-relevant features

Immune deficiency — antibody deficiency, low lymphocyte and B-cell subsets, impaired B-cell maturation — is characteristic of Roifman syndrome and is now recognized even in the severe MOPD1 end of the spectrum. A report of twin sisters emphasized B-cell anomalies in MOPD1 and underscored "the importance of considering immunodeficiency in MOPDI management to reduce the risk of serious infectious episodes" (PMID: 38837402). Retinal dystrophy in RNU4ATAC-Roifman syndrome is a generalized rod-cone dystrophy: in a deeply phenotyped cohort of 10 patients, all had abnormal ERG and "nine showed generalized rod-cone dystrophy" (PMID: 37225827), with nyctalopia common and progressive visual loss on follow-up. These findings justify immunologic and ophthalmologic surveillance in LWS.

Finding 7 — LWS is an autosomal recessive disorder, first described by Lowry and Wood (1975), and is ultra-rare

Lowry and Wood (1975) first described two boys with epiphyseal dysplasia, short stature, microcephaly, and nystagmus. Nevin et al. (1986) reported a second family, noting "the second example of the syndrome first described by Lowry and Wood [1975] in two boys who had epiphyseal dysplasia, short stature, microcephaly, and nystagmus; one of these patients was mildly mentally retarded" and concluding that "The Lowry-Wood syndrome probably is an autosomal recessive trait" (PMID: 3706411). Only a few dozen molecularly confirmed RNU4ATAC-opathy patients exist worldwide (60 in the largest cohort), making LWS ultra-rare with no formal prevalence estimate.

Finding 8 — Animal models recapitulate key features: minor-spliceosome loss causes microcephaly and limb micromelia

Model-organism work supports causality between minor-spliceosome dysfunction and the cardinal LWS phenotypes. Conditional inactivation of the minor spliceosome (Rnu11/U11 ablation) in developing mouse cortex demonstrated that "Minor spliceosome inactivation causes microcephaly, owing to cell cycle defects and death of self-amplifying radial glial cells" (PMID: 30093551). In the limb, "Loss of U11 small nuclear RNA in the developing mouse limb results in micromelia" (PMID: 32665241), linking minor-spliceosome loss to skeletal dysplasia. A CRISPR Drosophila U4atac mutant reproduced growth/neurodevelopmental defects, immunodeficiency, and GI symptoms via mis-splicing of innate-immunity and hematopoiesis transcripts (PMID: 42463491), and zebrafish U4atac knockdown produced ciliary defects (PMID: 36802443).

Finding 9 — Diagnosis requires sequencing the non-coding RNU4ATAC exon; variants are frequently missed, and RNA-seq aids classification

Because RNU4ATAC is a single-exon non-coding gene, "the bioinformatic prediction algorithms assessing the effect of sequence variants on splicing or protein function are irrelevant" (PMID: 32628740), and variants "are often overlooked in analysis because of their noncoding nature" (PMID: 42322193). Some exome capture kits do not even target the gene — "RNU4ATAC is not targeted by many commercially available exome capture kits" (PMID: 37666272) — so whole-genome sequencing or targeted RNU4ATAC Sanger sequencing may be required; in one Chinese family, variants were identified only after WES was negative (PMID: 29370840). A cellular minor-intron splicing reporter assay (PMID: 32628740) and RNA-seq demonstrating minor intron retention provide functional evidence: "In 6 individuals, RNA-seq enabled the reclassification of variants of uncertain significance as likely pathogenic" (PMID: 42322193).

Finding 10 — RNU4ATAC disorders are molecularly and clinically distinct from PCNT-related MOPD type II

A critical differential-diagnosis point: the RNU4ATAC-opathies (MOPD1, Roifman, LWS) must be distinguished from the superficially similarly named microcephalic osteodysplastic primordial dwarfism type II (MOPD II), caused not by a spliceosome RNA but by loss-of-function variants in PCNT (pericentrin), a centrosomal protein. "Loss-of-function variants of this gene are responsible for microcephalic osteodysplastic primordial dwarfism type II (MOPDII), a rare inherited autosomal recessive disorder" (PMID: 37234811). MOPD II is distinguished by a high risk of cerebral aneurysms and Moyamoya vasculopathy; in one report "The boy died at 8 years of age as a result of an intracranial hemorrhage due to a cerebral aneurism associated with the Moyamoya malformation" (PMID: 37234811). This cerebrovascular risk belongs to PCNT-MOPD II, not to RNU4ATAC disorders — a distinction that affects surveillance and prognosis.

Detailed Report by Section

1. Disease Information

Overview. Lowry-Wood syndrome is an ultra-rare autosomal-recessive skeletal dysplasia (a "minor spliceosomopathy") characterized by multiple epiphyseal dysplasia, microcephaly, short stature, developmental delay/intellectual disability, and ophthalmologic abnormalities (nystagmus, retinal dystrophy). It is the mildest recognized presentation of the RNU4ATAC-opathy spectrum.

2. Etiology

Causal factor: Purely genetic — biallelic (homozygous or compound heterozygous) loss-of-function variants in RNU4ATAC (PMID: 29265708). No environmental or infectious cause.

Genetic risk factors / modifiers: The principal determinant of phenotype severity is variant position within the U4atac RNA secondary structure — 5′ stem-loop variants (high FitCons constraint) produce severe MOPD1, while more dispersed variants produce milder LWS/RS (PMID: 30368667). Compound heterozygosity combining a "severe" and a "mild" allele can yield intermediate phenotypes. No separate susceptibility loci or distinct modifier genes have been established.

Environmental / protective factors and gene–environment interactions: None identified; not applicable for a fully penetrant Mendelian recessive disorder. Consanguinity increases the chance of homozygosity in affected families (relevant to recessive inheritance generally).

4. Genetic / Molecular Information

Causal gene: RNU4ATAC — non-coding, single-exon, transcribed into the ~130-nt U4atac snRNA; chr 2q14.2; HGNC:34140; OMIM 601428.

Pathogenic variants: All reported disease variants are single-nucleotide changes or small indels in the non-coding RNA; because there is no protein product, variant classes are described by location in the RNA structure rather than missense/nonsense terminology. Recurrent/illustrative variants include n.51G>A (the most frequent MOPD1-causative 5′ stem-loop variant), n.50G>A / n.55G>A (compound het in MOPD1-phenotype patients), n.29T>A (MOPD1 hotspot), and more dispersed variants in milder LWS/RS. Functional consequence is loss of function of U4atac, i.e., impaired minor spliceosome assembly. Pathogenic alleles are very rare/absent in gnomAD; carrier frequency is not well established (PMID: 32628740).

Variant origin: Germline. No somatic involvement.

Modifier genes / epigenetics / chromosomal abnormalities: No established modifier genes, epigenetic signatures, or large chromosomal rearrangements; CMA and karyotype are characteristically normal (the point variants are not detected by those methods) (PMID: 29370840).

5. Environmental Information

Not applicable. LWS is a monogenic recessive disorder with no known environmental, lifestyle, or infectious contributors. (Infections may be consequences of the associated immune deficiency — e.g., HHV-6–associated encephalopathy reported in a MOPD1 patient — but infection is not a cause of the disease.)

6. Mechanism / Pathophysiology

Ordered causal chain (initiating lesion → clinical manifestation):

  1. Biallelic loss-of-function variants in RNU4ATAC lead to a structurally/functionally defective U4atac snRNA.
  2. Defective U4atac impairs assembly of the U4atac/U6atac.U5 tri-snRNP, the catalytic core of the minor (U12-dependent) spliceosome.
  3. Impaired minor spliceosome results in failure to excise ~700–800 U12-type minor introns → transcriptome-wide minor-intron retention and mis-splicing (demonstrated by patient RNA-seq).
  4. Minor-intron retention in affected transcripts reduces functional expression of the diverse minor-intron-containing genes (enriched in cell-cycle, DNA-replication, and signaling functions). 5a. In neural progenitors, this causes cell-cycle defects and death of self-amplifying radial glial cells → reduced neuron output → microcephaly and developmental delay (demonstrated in mouse Rnu11 models; inferred for humans). 5b. In the developing limb/skeleton, minor-spliceosome deficiency disrupts chondro-osseous development → epiphyseal dysplasia, short stature, micromelia (demonstrated in mouse U11 limb model; inferred for humans). 5c. (Branch) Secondary ciliary dysfunction contributes to skeletal/developmental and possibly retinal phenotypes (human + zebrafish). 5d. (Branch) Mis-splicing of JAK/STAT pathway transcripts attenuates cytokine signaling → impaired hematopoiesis/B-cell maturation → immune deficiency (Drosophila + patient data). 5e. (Branch) Dysregulated type I interferon signaling produces painful chilblain-like skin lesions.
  5. The combined developmental and signaling defects manifest as the LWS clinical phenotype; variant position on the severity gradient determines whether the outcome is mild (LWS), intermediate (RS), or severe/lethal (MOPD1).

Upstream vs downstream: Steps 1–3 (variant → U4atac → minor-spliceosome failure → minor-intron retention) are upstream and shared across the entire spectrum. Steps 5a–5e are downstream, tissue-specific consequences.

7. Anatomical Structures Affected

  • Skeletal system (UBERON:0001434): epiphyses (UBERON:0002515), long bones, joints — multiple epiphyseal dysplasia, short stature.
  • Nervous system / brain (UBERON:0000955): microcephaly, cerebral anomalies; cerebral cortex (UBERON:0000956) and radial glia affected.
  • Eye (UBERON:0000970): retina (UBERON:0000966) — rod-cone dystrophy; oculomotor system — nystagmus.
  • Immune/hematopoietic system (UBERON:0002405): B-lymphocyte compartment.
  • Skin (UBERON:0002097): chilblain-like lesions, often acral/bilateral.
  • Lateralization: skeletal and retinal involvement are characteristically bilateral/symmetric.

8. Temporal Development

  • Onset: Congenital/prenatal for growth and microcephaly (intrauterine growth restriction in severe cases); skeletal dysplasia and nystagmus apparent in infancy/childhood.
  • Course: Largely static for structural/developmental features, but progressive for the retinal dystrophy (progressive visual loss on follow-up) and episodic for chilblain-like skin lesions.
  • Duration: Chronic/lifelong. In LWS (mild end), lifespan is not markedly curtailed; by contrast MOPD1 is frequently lethal in infancy/early childhood.
  • Critical period: Fetal/early-postnatal neurodevelopment (radial glial proliferation window) is the key vulnerability period; no established intervention window exists.

9. Inheritance and Population

  • Inheritance: Autosomal recessive (PMID: 3706411); recurrence risk 25% per pregnancy for carrier couples.
  • Penetrance/expressivity: High penetrance for biallelic pathogenic genotypes, but highly variable expressivity governed by variant position and allele combination.
  • Epidemiology: Ultra-rare; only a few dozen molecularly confirmed RNU4ATAC-opathy patients worldwide (60 in the largest cohort). No formal prevalence/incidence estimate.
  • Sex ratio: No sex predilection (autosomal). Consanguinity elevates risk via homozygosity. No well-defined founder effects, though recurrent hotspot variants (e.g., n.51G>A) exist. Carrier frequency not reliably established.

10. Diagnostics

  • Genetic testing is definitive. Because RNU4ATAC is non-coding and single-exon, protein/splice predictors do not apply and the gene is missed by many exome kits. Recommended approach: whole-genome sequencing or targeted RNU4ATAC Sanger sequencing, especially when WES is negative in a clinically suggestive patient (PMID: 37666272; PMID: 29370840).
  • Functional biomarker: RNA-seq demonstrating minor-intron retention and a cellular minor-intron splicing reporter assay provide functional pathogenicity evidence and enable VUS reclassification (PMID: 42322193; PMID: 32628740).
  • Imaging: Skeletal survey (epiphyseal dysplasia), brain MRI (microcephaly/cerebral anomalies); ERG for retinal dystrophy (uniformly abnormal) (PMID: 37225827).
  • Immunology: Immunoglobulin levels and lymphocyte/B-cell subset quantification (PMID: 38837402).
  • CMA/karyotype: Characteristically normal (do not detect RNU4ATAC point variants).
  • Differential diagnosis: Other multiple epiphyseal dysplasias; other primordial dwarfisms, importantly PCNT-related MOPD II (distinguished by aneurysm/Moyamoya risk, PMID: 37234811); and within-gene RS/MOPD1.

11. Outcome / Prognosis

  • Survival: In LWS (mild end) life expectancy is relatively preserved; the severe MOPD1 end is frequently lethal in infancy/early childhood. No LWS-specific mortality statistics exist.
  • Morbidity: Driven by short stature/skeletal limitation, progressive visual impairment, cognitive impairment, and infection susceptibility.
  • Prognostic factors: Variant position and allele combination (severity gradient); degree of immune deficiency (infection risk) and retinal dystrophy (visual prognosis).
  • Complications: Recurrent infections (immune deficiency), progressive visual loss; non-ischemic cardiomyopathy reported at the RS end (PMID: 37666272).

12. Treatment

No disease-modifying or curative therapy exists; management is supportive and multidisciplinary: - Immunoglobulin replacement / infection prophylaxis for antibody deficiency (NCIT: Immunoglobulin Therapy). - Ophthalmologic surveillance and low-vision support for retinal dystrophy/nystagmus. - Orthopedic/skeletal management and physical therapy for epiphyseal dysplasia/short stature. - Developmental/educational support for delay/intellectual disability. - Dermatologic management of chilblain-like lesions (type I interferon involvement suggests a theoretical rationale for immunomodulation, but this is investigational). - Experimental: No approved gene/RNA therapies; the minor-intron-retention readout could in principle serve as a pharmacodynamic biomarker for future splice-modulating approaches. No registered disease-specific clinical trials identified.

13. Prevention

  • Genetic counseling for 25% recurrence risk; carrier testing of at-risk relatives (cascade testing).
  • Prenatal diagnosis / preimplantation genetic testing feasible once familial variants are known — prenatal diagnosis has been reported for a subsequent pregnancy in an affected family (PMID: 29370840).
  • Tertiary prevention: infection prophylaxis, vision monitoring, developmental intervention.
  • No primary prevention (not environmentally modifiable); not vaccine-preventable.

14. Other Species / Natural Disease

  • No naturally occurring companion-animal/wildlife disease ortholog is catalogued (no OMIA entry for LWS specifically).
  • RNU4ATAC/minor-spliceosome components are deeply evolutionarily conserved (present from Drosophila to mammals), enabling cross-species modeling. Orthologous minor-spliceosome snRNA function is conserved in mouse and zebrafish.
  • No zoonotic potential (non-infectious genetic disorder).

The model's central logic is that a single upstream lesion (minor-intron retention) produces a pleiotropic, multisystem phenotype because minor introns reside in functionally diverse genes. The severity gradient (LWS ↔ RS ↔ MOPD1) is explained quantitatively by how much a given variant degrades tri-snRNP assembly (FitCons-weighted, position-dependent). Tissue selectivity (why brain, skeleton, retina, and immune cells are preferentially affected) likely reflects the high proliferative/biosynthetic demand of neural and chondro-osseous progenitors and the splicing dependence of key cell-cycle and signaling transcripts.

Limitations and Knowledge Gaps

  1. No LWS-specific epidemiology. Prevalence, incidence, carrier frequency, and sex/age distributions are unquantified given extreme rarity; most data are pooled across the RNU4ATAC-opathy spectrum.
  2. Genotype–phenotype resolution is incomplete. The FitCons/position model explains broad severity trends but cannot yet reliably predict LWS vs RS vs MOPD1 for a novel allele combination.
  3. Model limitations. Most animal models ablate U11/Rnu11 rather than RNU4ATAC, and use complete knockouts rather than hypomorphic alleles; no model specifically phenocopies the mild LWS end.
  4. Tissue-selectivity unexplained. Why specific tissues (brain, skeleton, retina, B cells) are preferentially vulnerable despite a housekeeping defect remains mechanistically unresolved.
  5. Secondary pathways (cilia, JAK/STAT, IFN) are derived partly from allelic disorders/model organisms; their contribution specifically in mild LWS is inferred, not directly demonstrated.
  6. No natural-history or QoL data, and no therapeutic trials — management recommendations are extrapolated from case experience.

Proposed Follow-up Experiments / Actions

  1. Establish a prospective RNU4ATAC-opathy registry capturing genotype, minor-intron-retention burden (RNA-seq), and longitudinal phenotype (growth, vision/ERG, immune function, neurodevelopment) to quantify natural history and refine genotype–phenotype prediction.
  2. Build hypomorphic, allele-specific models (knock-in of human LWS vs MOPD1 variants in RNU4ATAC itself; mouse and/or patient iPSC-derived cerebral and chondro-osseous organoids) to directly test the position-severity model and tissue selectivity.
  3. Standardize a quantitative minor-intron-retention assay as a clinical functional test to resolve VUS and correlate retention burden with severity (candidate severity biomarker).
  4. Targeted-sequencing / WGS reflex testing in any microcephalic-primordial-dwarfism or multiple-epiphyseal-dysplasia patient with negative exome — raise diagnostic yield by ensuring RNU4ATAC coverage.
  5. Pathway-directed therapeutic exploration: test whether modulating type I interferon or JAK/STAT signaling ameliorates immune/skin phenotypes, and evaluate splice-modulating small molecules/ASOs using minor-intron-retention as a pharmacodynamic endpoint.
  6. Systematic immunologic and ophthalmologic surveillance protocols for diagnosed patients, given the demonstrated antibody deficiency and progressive rod-cone dystrophy.
