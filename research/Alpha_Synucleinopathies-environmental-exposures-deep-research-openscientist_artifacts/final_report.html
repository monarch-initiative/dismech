Environmental Exposures Contributing to the Alpha-Synucleinopathies

Summary

Environmental, occupational, lifestyle and iatrogenic exposures are robustly and reproducibly linked to Parkinson disease (PD), but are sparsely studied in the other synucleinopathies — dementia with Lewy bodies (DLB), multiple system atrophy (MSA) and isolated REM sleep behaviour disorder (iRBD) — and, critically, their directions of effect do not transfer between diseases. The single most important curatorial conclusion of this investigation is therefore a negative one: a risk factor established for PD cannot be assumed to apply to DLB, MSA or iRBD, and in several cases the measured direction is opposite. Smoking, the archetypal inverse ("protective") factor for PD (RR ≈ 0.59), is a risk factor in prodromal iRBD (OR 1.43) and is null in DLB. Pesticide exposure, the strongest occupational signal in PD, was null in the one MSA case-control study that applied a job-exposure matrix. These dissociations are not noise; they are a central result that should guide curation of each disease's environmental section independently.

For Parkinson disease specifically, the evidence supports a coherent toxicological model. Risk is raised by mitochondrial complex-I/pro-oxidant pesticides (paraquat OR 1.64; rotenone OR 1.57; organochlorines OR 1.54; chlorpyrifos >2.5-fold residential), chlorinated solvents (trichloroethylene, via the Camp Lejeune cohort), cumulative bone lead, manganese welding fume (as a distinct parkinsonism), ambient air pollution (PM2.5 HR 1.04 per IQR), dairy consumption (OR ≈ 1.26, male-predominant), and specific infections (Helicobacter pylori OR 1.65; hepatitis C OR 1.20). Risk is lowered by cigarette/smokeless tobacco (RR 0.59), caffeine (HR 0.80), physical activity (HR 0.66), high urate/gout (IRR 0.80), ibuprofen (RR 0.73), calcium channel blockers (RR 0.76–0.81), statins (RR 0.79–0.86), and full truncal — but not superselective — vagotomy (HR 0.53 at >20 years). MPTP provides the mechanistic proof-of-principle that a complex-I toxicant can cause human parkinsonism.

The mechanistic through-line for PD is convergence on mitochondrial dysfunction, oxidative stress, α-synuclein aggregation, autophagy-lysosome failure, neuroinflammation, and a gut–vagal propagation axis. The inverse ("protective") associations, however, are persistently vulnerable to reverse causation (prodromal PD reduces smoking and coffee intake and raises fall/head-injury risk) and survival/selection bias, and much of the pesticide and metals literature rests on post-diagnosis biomonitoring and recall-based case-control designs. Where evidence is weak, conflicting or confounded, this report states so explicitly.

Key Findings by Disease

Parkinson Disease

Pesticides and herbicides

Pesticides are the best-established occupational/residential risk class for PD, and the subclasses converge mechanistically on mitochondrial complex-I inhibition and oxidative stress. Paraquat, a redox-cycling herbicide, carries a pooled odds ratio of 1.64 (95% CI 1.27–2.13) across 13 case-control studies PMID: 30474499, with strong animal face-validity: a PRISMA meta-analysis of 54 rodent studies found paraquat produces substantial substantia nigra pars compacta neuronal loss (SMD 3.07, 95% CI 2.48–3.65) PMID: 41871812. Rotenone, a botanical complex-I inhibitor, was associated with PD at OR 1.57 in the 181,842-participant AGRICAN cohort, strongest in farmers with the longest exposure PMID: 29136149, and remained associated "regardless of glove use" in the FAME nested case-control study PMID: 25461423 — an argument for causality rather than recall artefact. Dietary fat modifies the effect: rotenone OR rose to 5.8 (2.3–15) in those with high saturated-fat intake PMID: 24120951.

Organochlorines (dieldrin, DDT/DDE) increase PD risk, with a blood-measured pooled OR 1.54 (1.32–1.79) across 7 studies and OR 1.19 (1.04–1.35) by indirect assessment PMID: 39520734; the signal was positive in Asian studies and non-significant in North America/Europe. Animal work shows dieldrin impairs aldehyde dehydrogenase and dopamine homeostasis causing nigral oxidative damage PMID: 42808099, and perinatal dieldrin produces persistent sex-specific DNA-methylation changes in dopaminergic neurodevelopment genes PMID: 38746441. Chlorpyrifos, an organophosphate, is uniquely informative: in the UCLA PEG cohort long-term residential exposure conferred >2.5-fold increased risk, and the accompanying mouse/zebrafish work showed the dopaminergic loss was "at least partially due to autophagy dysfunction and synuclein accumulation" — restoring autophagic flux or eliminating synuclein was protective PMID: 41382278. Chlorpyrifos thus maps distinctively onto the Autophagy-Lysosome Pathway Dysfunction node.

Composite rural proxies reinforce these findings: a classic meta-analysis reported rural living OR 1.56 (1.18–2.07), farming OR 1.42 (1.05–1.91), pesticide exposure OR 1.85 (1.31–2.60), and well-water use OR 1.26 (0.97–1.64, borderline) PMID: 11437458. A Bradford-Hill weight-of-evidence assessment, however, cautioned that these rural associations are weaker and less consistent than the smoking "positive control," concluding that an unidentified factor correlated with rural living/farming — rather than any single measured pesticide — may be causal PMID: 27055126.

Solvents

Chlorinated solvent exposure increases PD risk. The strongest evidence is the Camp Lejeune population-based cohort: Marines stationed at the trichloroethylene (TCE)-contaminated base (monthly median TCE >70× the permissible level) had elevated PD risk (reported ~70% higher) and more frequent prodromal features versus the uncontaminated Camp Pendleton base PMID: 37184848. A follow-up found VOC-exposed PD patients progressed faster to psychosis, fracture and fall (adjusted HRs exceeding 2) PMID: 38988230. The companion mortality study reported an elevated PD hazard that persisted after quantitative bias analysis for smoking and alcohol PMID: 38961410.

Metals

A systematic review/meta-analysis of 88 studies found that cumulative lead in bone was associated with increased PD risk, while serum copper, iron and zinc were lower in cases (likely reflecting disease rather than cause), and — importantly — no association for other metals, including manganese PMID: 37022311. Cumulative bone lead also correlated with worse cognition in PD PMID: 23143985 and reduced olfactory recognition in elderly men PMID: 26121922. Manganese deserves separate treatment: Mn-containing welding fume causes a dose-dependent parkinsonism (prevalence 15.6% in welders vs 0% in referents) with a clinical phenotype overlapping but distinct from Lewy-body PD (less rest tremor/asymmetry) PMID: 22975422; longitudinal follow-up showed dose-dependent progression (annual UPDRS3 +0.24 per mg Mn/m³·year) PMID: 28031394. Manganism is therefore best curated as a distinct toxicant parkinsonism, not idiopathic synucleinopathy.

Air pollution

The Ontario cohort (~2.2M adults, 38,745 PD cases) found each IQR (3.8 µg/m³) increase in PM2.5 associated with a 4% higher incident PD risk (HR 1.04, 1.01–1.08), with comparable small effects for NO2 and ozone PMID: 30124852. A Korean cohort found NO2 associated with incident PD (HR 1.41 highest vs lowest quartile) PMID: 33999109. An earlier US nested case-control found no overall association but positive subgroup effects in women and never-smokers (PM10 OR 1.65 in women) PMID: 27285422. The effect is real but small and somewhat inconsistent.

Head injury

The evidence is genuinely conflicting. Balabandian et al. pooled 15 studies to a TBI–PD risk ratio of 1.48 (1.22–1.74) PMID: 36781627, whereas Huang et al. (18 studies, 3.26M patients) found TBI associated with dementia but not PD PMID: 30396335. This association is especially vulnerable to reverse causation — prodromal PD gait/balance impairment increases falls and head injuries — and to recall bias.

MPTP — mechanistic proof-of-principle

MPTP is not an epidemiological exposure but the historical demonstration that a synthetic complex-I toxicant causes irreversible human parkinsonism. It crosses the blood-brain barrier, is converted by astrocytic MAO-B to MPP+, selectively enters dopaminergic neurons via the dopamine transporter, and inhibits mitochondrial complex I, generating reactive oxygen species and causing nigrostriatal degeneration PMID: 38773545; PMID: 27665784. It is the mechanistic keystone linking the pesticide class to Mitochondrial Dysfunction, Oxidative Stress and Dopaminergic Neuron Loss.

Gut-related exposures

The gut–brain (Braak) axis is strongly supported. Specific GI syndromes precede PD diagnosis in a PD-specific manner (vs AD and cerebrovascular disease): gastroparesis OR 4.64, dysphagia OR 3.58, IBS-without-diarrhoea OR 3.53, constipation OR 3.32 PMID: 37620120. The vagal route is implicated by the vagotomy natural experiment: full truncal vagotomy reduced PD risk (HR 0.53 at >20 years) while superselective vagotomy had no effect (HR 1.09) PMID: 26031848. Appendectomy, by contrast, was null in a large Korean cohort (adjusted HR 1.42, 0.88–2.30) PMID: 38696328, with inconsistent Western results; it should be curated as unresolved. PPI use (which alters gut flora) modestly raised PD risk (OR 1.14, 1.07–1.22) even after adjustment for H. pylori PMID: 41984194.

Infections

A meta-analysis of 23 studies found positive associations for Helicobacter pylori (OR 1.65, 1.43–1.92), hepatitis C (OR 1.20, 1.01–1.41), Malassezia and Pneumoniae, and — importantly — explicit null results for influenza, herpesviruses, HBV, scarlet fever, mumps, varicella, pertussis, rubella and measles PMID: 32017453. Antiviral treatment of HCV reduced PD risk (OR 0.67), supporting causality for the HCV signal.

Protective / inverse associations

  • Smoking: meta-analysis of 69 studies, ever-smokers RR 0.59 (0.56–0.62), with dose-response and a current-smoking RR of 0.35; smokeless tobacco (snus) in never-smokers HR 0.41 implicates nicotine rather than combustion PMID: 26272284; PMID: 27940486. The consistency across prospective designs argues against pure reverse causation, though it cannot be excluded.
  • Caffeine/coffee: pooled HR 0.80 (0.75–0.85), dose-dependent PMID: 32580456.
  • Physical activity: pooled HR 0.66 (0.57–0.78), stronger/clearer in men PMID: 25410713; PMID: 20660864.
  • Urate/gout: gout inversely associated with PD (IRR 0.80), and within gout patients higher urate was further protective PMID: 41050064.
  • Ibuprofen (but not aspirin or other NSAIDs): pooled RR 0.73 (0.63–0.85) PMID: 21368281.

A critical caveat applies to smoking and coffee: reverse causation (prodromal hypodopaminergic personality change reduces addictive behaviours) and survival bias remain plausible alternative explanations, and an umbrella review of 46 systematic reviews attributed many conflicting non-genetic associations to study-design differences (cohort vs case-control), rating 84.7% of reviews as critically low methodological quality PMID: 33814465.

Medications (iatrogenic)

  • Calcium channel blockers (dihydropyridine): pooled RR 0.76–0.81 PMID: 26464872; PMID: 25845582.
  • Statins: pooled RR 0.79–0.86, duration-dependent (long-duration RR 0.49; short-term null), but with no disease-modifying effect on UPDRS-III PMID: 39097909; PMID: 35629248.
  • β2-adrenoreceptor drugs: mechanistically, the β2AR regulates the SNCA (α-synuclein) gene; agonists (salbutamol) lower SNCA and were associated with reduced PD risk (RR ≈ 0.66), while antagonists (propranolol) raise SNCA and PD risk (RR ≈ 2.2) PMID: 28860381. This is the clearest exposure→gene→risk mechanistic chain in the dataset.

Diet

High dairy consumption modestly raises PD risk (meta-analysis of 29 cohorts, 1.3M participants: OR 1.26, 1.12–1.41 for high dairy; milk OR 1.23; low-fat dairy OR 1.30), with a male-predominant effect (males HR 1.28 vs females HR 1.02) and ~13% increased risk per cup of milk/day; legumes/nuts were protective PMID: 41252180; PMID: 41792943; PMID: 28596209.

Multiple System Atrophy

The MSA environmental evidence base is small and conflicting (MSA prevalence ~4–5/100,000). The ESGAP European case-control study (73 MSA cases) reported increased risk with occupational exposure to organic solvents, plastic monomers/additives, pesticides and metals, and noted smoking was less frequent in MSA cases than controls PMID: 11487219. However, the Aquitaine case-control study, using a formal job-exposure matrix, found no significant relationship between occupational pesticide exposure and MSA PMID: 15272223 — directly cautioning against transferring the PD pesticide finding. The smoking direction (inverse) is the same as PD but rests on tiny samples. No robust meta-analytic effect size exists for any MSA exposure.

REM Sleep Behaviour Disorder (prodromal synucleinopathy)

The pivotal multicenter case-control study (347 iRBD cases vs 347 matched controls) found that iRBD patients were more likely to smoke (ever-smoking 64.0% vs 55.5%, OR 1.43, p=0.028) — the opposite direction to PD — and also more likely to report previous head injury (OR 1.59, p=0.037), farming occupation (OR 1.67, p=0.022), borderline welding (OR 1.53), and fewer years of schooling; caffeine and alcohol did not differ between cases and controls PMID: 22744670. The reversed smoking direction is the clearest single demonstration of non-transferability in the entire dataset. On phenoconversion, autonomic rather than classical environmental markers predict progression: in a prospective iRBD cohort, neurogenic orthostatic hypotension (HR 5.05) and abnormal baroreflex function predicted conversion to overt synucleinopathy PMID: 40112275; environmental exposures have not been shown to predict phenoconversion.

Dementia with Lewy Bodies

The single dedicated case-control study (147 DLB cases) found caffeine use less likely in DLB (OR 0.29, 0.14–0.57) — consistent with PD — plus positive associations with anxiety (OR 7.4), depression (OR 6.0), stroke (OR 2.8), family history of PD (OR 4.6), and APOE ε4 (OR 2.2) PMID: 23892702. Critically, smoking and education — which have opposing effects in AD and PD — were NOT risk factors for DLB, and the authors concluded "DLB risk factors are an amalgam of those for AD and PD." Pesticide and head-injury data specific to DLB are essentially absent.

Pure Autonomic Failure

No citable environmental epidemiology was found for pure autonomic failure. This is a genuine gap, and the disease's environmental section should be curated as "no exposure studies identified" rather than populated by transfer from PD.

Mechanistic Model and Interpretation

The PD exposures converge on a small set of interacting mechanism nodes. Toxicant exposures (pesticides, solvents, metals, air pollution, MPTP) act predominantly at the mitochondria/oxidative-stress hub, while gut-related and infectious exposures act via the gut–vagal α-synuclein propagation axis; a subset (chlorpyrifos) additionally hits the autophagy-lysosome node, and β2-adrenergic drugs act "upstream" by transcriptionally regulating SNCA itself.

Evidence Base

The strongest, highest-tier evidence underpinning this report:

  • Smoking: PMID: 26272284 (69-study meta-analysis) and PMID: 27940486 (snus, isolating nicotine) jointly establish a robust, dose-responsive inverse association.
  • Paraquat / rotenone / organochlorines / chlorpyrifos: PMID: 30474499, PMID: 29136149, PMID: 39520734, PMID: 41382278 provide convergent human effect sizes plus animal mechanism; the glove-use analysis PMID: 25461423 strengthens causal inference.
  • TCE / Camp Lejeune: PMID: 37184848 and PMID: 38988230 — a quasi-experimental population comparison that is hard to confound.
  • Metals: PMID: 37022311 is the definitive meta-analysis isolating lead and nulling manganese for idiopathic PD; PMID: 22975422 / PMID: 28031394 separately establish Mn welding parkinsonism.
  • Gut–vagal axis: PMID: 26031848 (vagotomy) and PMID: 37620120 (prodromal GI syndromes) are the core support for the Braak hypothesis; PMID: 38696328 provides the null appendectomy counterpoint.
  • Non-transferability pillars: PMID: 22744670 (iRBD smoking POSITIVE), PMID: 23892702 (DLB smoking NULL), PMID: 15272223 (MSA pesticide NULL) are the three pillars of the central result.
  • Consolidation / caveats: PMID: 33814465 (umbrella review) and PMID: 27055126 (Bradford-Hill) temper the pesticide and inverse-association literature with design and consistency critiques.

Challenging/qualifying evidence is deliberately retained: the TBI meta-analyses conflict (PMID: 36781627 positive vs PMID: 30396335 null); air-pollution effects are small and subgroup-dependent (PMID: 27285422); and the organochlorine signal is geographically heterogeneous (PMID: 39520734).

Limitations and Knowledge Gaps

  1. PD-centricity. The overwhelming majority of effect sizes derive from PD. DLB rests essentially on a single case-control study, MSA on two small conflicting ones, and iRBD on one multicenter case-control plus phenoconversion cohorts. Effect sizes for non-PD synucleinopathies should be treated as provisional.
  2. Pure autonomic failure is unstudied. No citable environmental epidemiology was found; this gap should be stated explicitly in curation.
  3. Reverse causation and survival bias persistently threaten the inverse associations (smoking, coffee) and the TBI association. Prodromal PD can change behaviour decades before diagnosis.
  4. Recall and selection bias dominate the case-control pesticide/solvent literature; much metals evidence uses post-diagnosis biomonitoring, which cannot establish temporality.
  5. Methodological quality is low. 84.7% of systematic reviews in the umbrella review were rated critically low, and design differences explained many conflicts PMID: 33814465.
  6. Geographic heterogeneity (organochlorines positive in Asia, null in West; PPIs significant in Asia/Europe, null in US) suggests unmeasured gene–environment or exposure-mix confounding.
  7. Manganism vs idiopathic PD must not be conflated — Mn produces a related but pathologically distinct parkinsonism.
  8. No exposure predicts iRBD phenoconversion in current data; autonomic markers do.

Proposed Follow-up Actions

  1. Curate each disease's environmental section independently. Do not propagate PD exposures to DLB, MSA or iRBD. Explicitly flag the reversed smoking direction in iRBD, the null smoking in DLB, and the null pesticide finding in MSA.
  2. Link exposures to mechanism nodes per the mapping table above, tagging the mode (triggers / exacerbates / predisposes / protects) and the evidence tier (human meta-analysis > cohort > case-control > animal mechanism).
  3. Tag every inverse association with the reverse-causation/survival-bias caveat, and every case-control exposure with the recall-bias caveat, as structured metadata.
  4. Record explicit null/absent entries — MSA pesticides (JEM-null), DLB smoking (null), influenza/herpesviruses (null), appendectomy (null/unresolved), and pure autonomic failure (no data) — so the knowledge base distinguishes "studied and null" from "not studied."
  5. Prioritise future primary research on DLB and MSA exposure epidemiology and on whether any environmental exposure predicts iRBD phenoconversion, which is the highest-value prevention target.
  6. Separate manganese welding parkinsonism as its own entity linked to basal-ganglia/dopaminergic injury rather than Lewy-body α-synucleinopathy.
