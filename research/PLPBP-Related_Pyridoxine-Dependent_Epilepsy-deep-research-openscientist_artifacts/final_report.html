PLPBP-Related Pyridoxine-Dependent Epilepsy (PLPHP Deficiency): Comprehensive Disease Characterization

Target disease: PLPBP-Related Pyridoxine-Dependent Epilepsy (Developmental and Epileptic Encephalopathy 2024; vitamin B6-dependent epilepsy due to PLPHP deficiency) MONDO: MONDO:0015005 (pyridoxine-dependent epilepsy, umbrella). Category: Mendelian, autosomal recessive. Evidence base: 27 literature sources reviewed; 11 confirmed findings recorded. This is a literature-based synthesis (no primary patient dataset provided). Evidence types are flagged as human clinical, model organism, in vitro/biochemical, or computational/structural.

Important caveat on rarity: As of 2026 only ~54 patients have been reported worldwide (Alsini et al. 2026, 41671913). Many disease parameters (precise prevalence, penetrance, QoL metrics, survival curves) are therefore not formally established and are noted as such.

1. Disease Information

PLPBP-related pyridoxine-dependent epilepsy is a rare autosomal-recessive inborn error of vitamin B6 (pyridoxal 5'-phosphate, PLP) homeostasis that presents as a neonatal-onset developmental and epileptic encephalopathy. Seizures are typically refractory to conventional antiepileptic drugs (AEDs) but respond to vitamin B6 (pyridoxine or PLP). It was first defined molecularly in 2016 when biallelic PROSC (now PLPBP) mutations were shown to "disrupt cellular pyridoxal phosphate homeostasis and cause vitamin-B6-dependent epilepsy" (Darin et al., 27912044). Information source: aggregated individual case reports and small cohorts/systematic reviews (not population EHR).

Key identifiers - Gene: PLPBP (formerly PROSC); HGNC:30042; NCBI Gene 65015; Ensembl ENSG00000147471; UniProt O94903 (PLPHP_HUMAN). Locus 8p11.23. - Protein: PLPHP / PLP-BP (pyridoxal 5'-phosphate homeostasis protein). - OMIM: gene PLPBP #604436; phenotype #617290 (Developmental and epileptic encephalopathy 2024, formerly EIEE/pyridoxine-dependent). - Orphanet: within "Pyridoxine-dependent epilepsy" (ORPHA:3006 umbrella; PLPHP deficiency as a molecular subtype). - ICD-11: 8A61 (developmental and epileptic encephalopathies) / 5C50.A (disorders of vitamin B6 metabolism). ICD-10: G40.4 + E53.1 (vitamin B6 deficiency) as proxies. - MeSH: "Seizures" / "Epilepsy" + "Vitamin B 6"; no dedicated MeSH heading for PLPHP deficiency. - MONDO: MONDO:0015005 (umbrella, pyridoxine-dependent epilepsy).

Synonyms / alternative names: PLPHP deficiency; PLPBP deficiency; PROSC-related/PROSC deficiency epilepsy; Vitamin B6-dependent epilepsy (PLPBP type); Pyridoxine-dependent epilepsy type 2/PLPBP; Developmental and epileptic encephalopathy 2024.

2. Etiology

Primary cause — genetic. Biallelic (homozygous or compound heterozygous) pathogenic variants in PLPBP. "Biallelic pathogenic variants in PLPBP (formerly called PROSC) have recently been shown to cause a novel form of vitamin B6-dependent epilepsy" (Johnstone et al. 2019, 30668673). No environmental or infectious primary cause; the disease is monogenic.

Genetic risk factors. The disease-causing variants themselves are the risk factor. Consanguinity strongly elevates risk (many reported families are consanguineous; e.g., a first-cousin Pakistani family, Ahmed et al. 2020, 33425341). A recurrent/founder deletion c.370_373del (p.Asp124fs) is the single most frequent variant (18.5% of alleles; Alsini 2026, 41671913; also a founder allele reported by Pal et al. 2021, 33977028).

Environmental risk factors. None established as causal. However, intercurrent viral illness/fever can trigger breakthrough seizures or metabolic crises (Takle et al. 2025, 42563912) — a gene–environment interaction where physiological stress increases demand on an already-compromised PLP-homeostasis system. Age: risk window is prenatal/neonatal (congenital metabolic demand). Sex: no predilection (AR).

Protective factors. No validated genetic protective alleles or modifier genes are published. The principal modifiable protective factor is early vitamin B6 supplementation, which is treatment rather than a constitutional protective factor. Residual protein function (hypomorphic missense vs. null) is inferred to be protective against the most severe phenotype (genotype–severity correlation, Finding 10).

Gene–environment interaction. Illness-provoked crises (above) are the clearest example; otherwise GxE data are not available for this ultra-rare disease.

4. Genetic / Molecular Information

  • Causal gene: PLPBP (HGNC:30042; OMIM 604436; 8p11.23). Encodes PLPHP (296 aa; UniProt O94903).
  • Variant spectrum (Alsini 2026, 41671913; Johnstone 2019, 30668673): missense ≈48% (most common class), plus frameshift, nonsense, splice-site, and small indels. Representative variants: c.370_373del (p.Asp124fs) — most frequent/founder (18.5%); c.46_47insCA (p.Leu17Hisfs) (Ahmed 2020, 33425341); c.695C>T (p.Ala232Val) missense (İpek 2022, 36308585).
  • Classification (ACMG/AMP): reported variants are Pathogenic/Likely pathogenic in ClinVar; biallelic requirement.
  • Allele frequency: individually ultra-rare in gnomAD (consistent with AR disease; carrier frequency not precisely established, inferred very low outside founder contexts).
  • Origin: germline (constitutional). No somatic involvement.
  • Functional consequence: predominantly loss of function — null alleles or missense that reduce protein stability and/or PLP-binding. "The most severe phenotypes were associated with variants leading to loss of function of PLPBP or significantly affecting protein stability/PLP-binding" (Johnstone 2019, 30668673).
  • Modifier genes / epigenetics / chromosomal abnormalities: none established; no reported CNVs, methylation signatures, or cytogenetic lesions. Not applicable / not available.

5. Environmental Information

  • Environmental factors / toxins / occupational exposures: none causal (not applicable).
  • Lifestyle factors: not relevant to a neonatal monogenic disorder; however dietary vitamin B6 status modulates the biochemical state, and B6 supplementation is therapeutic.
  • Infectious agents: none cause the disease, but viral illnesses act as triggers for seizure/metabolic decompensation (Takle 2025, 42563912). NCBI Taxonomy not applicable to etiology.

6. Mechanism / Pathophysiology

Ordered causal chain (initiating lesion → clinical manifestation)

  1. Biallelic loss-of-function PLPBP variants → reduced amount or stability/activity of PLPHP protein (demonstrated: protein-stability/PLP-binding modeling, Johnstone 2019 30668673).
  2. Loss of PLPHP → failure of intracellular PLP homeostasis/chaperoning — the cell can no longer safely buffer and deliver the reactive aldehyde PLP to apo-enzymes (demonstrated in vitro/structurally: He 2022 35897955; "mediates PLP homeostasis by delivering PLP to target enzymes or by performing a protective function").
  3. This leads to two branches:
  4. Branch A (substrate starvation): many PLP-dependent enzymes receive insufficient correctly-delivered PLP → reduced holoenzyme activity.
  5. Branch B (toxic vitamer accumulation): aberrant accumulation of pyridoxine 5'-phosphate (PNP) (demonstrated in bacteria/in vitro: Ito 2020 31677193; Ito 2022 34978460), which directly inhibits PLP-dependent enzymes (e.g., the glycine cleavage system).
  6. Both branches result in dysregulation of amino-acid and neurotransmitter metabolism — notably GABA synthesis (glutamate decarboxylase, GAD), glycine catabolism, and catecholamine/monoamine synthesis (aromatic L-amino-acid decarboxylase, AADC) (model-organism evidence: zebrafish plpbp−/− shows "disruptions in amino acid metabolism as well as GABA and catecholamine pathways", Johnstone 2019 30668673).
  7. Imbalanced excitatory/inhibitory neurotransmission (inferred: ↓GABA, altered glycine/glutamate) → neuronal hyperexcitability → refractory neonatal seizures (phenotype demonstrated; precise neurotransmitter stoichiometry inferred).
  8. Sustained seizures + metabolic derangement during a critical developmental window → encephalopathy, white-matter injury, impaired brain growth (microcephaly) → developmental delay / intellectual disability; untreated → status epilepticus and death in infancy (Johnstone 2019 30668673).
  9. Therapeutic branch: exogenous vitamin B6 (pyridoxine → PLP, or direct PLP) raises PLP availability, partially restoring enzyme function → seizure control (demonstrated in humans and rescued in zebrafish, 30668673).

Supporting detail by category

  • Molecular pathways / metabolic changes: vitamin B6 (PLP) metabolism and dependent pathways — amino-acid metabolism (glycine cleavage, aspartate/serine), neurotransmitter biosynthesis (GABA via GAD, dopamine/serotonin via AADC), transsulfuration. KEGG: vitamin B6 metabolism (map00750). GO: vitamin B6 metabolic process (GO:0042816), pyridoxal phosphate biosynthetic process (GO:0042823), neurotransmitter biosynthetic process (GO:0042136).
  • Protein dysfunction: PLPHP has a type III TIM-barrel fold (He 2022, 35897955) with a conserved phosphate-coordinating PLP pocket; pathogenic missense destabilize the fold or the PLP site (loss of function; no dominant-negative or gain-of-function reported). PDB homologs exist for bacterial YggS; human structure via AlphaFold (O94903).
  • Biochemical abnormalities: functional insufficiency of multiple PLP-dependent enzymes; accumulation of PNP; nonspecific hyperglycinemia and lactic acidemia (Jensen 2019, 31741821).
  • Moonlighting / non-canonical functions: PLPHP is also an RNA-binding protein (Graziani 2024, 39604152) and has a mitochondrial pool linked to proteostasis (accumulates when mitochondrial peptidase CLPP is lost; Key 2023, 38139332); PLP-independent functions are reported for the cyanobacterial ortholog PipY (Llop 2026, 41820517). These broaden the mechanism beyond cytosolic PLP delivery and may explain mitochondrial-disease mimicry and nonspecific lactic acidemia.
  • Cellular processes / tissue damage: neuronal hyperexcitability and excitotoxic/seizure-related injury (inferred); no primary inflammatory or autoimmune component. Immune involvement: not applicable.
  • Cell types / GO-CC: neurons (CL:0000540), especially GABAergic neurons (CL:0000617); glia secondary. Subcellular: cytosol (GO:0005829) and mitochondrion (GO:0005739); molecular function pyridoxal phosphate binding (GO:0030170), RNA binding (GO:0003723).
  • CHEBI chemical entities: pyridoxal 5'-phosphate (CHEBI:18405), pyridoxine (CHEBI:16709), pyridoxine 5'-phosphate (CHEBI:58589), γ-aminobutyric acid (CHEBI:16865), glycine (CHEBI:15428).
  • Molecular profiling (omics): no human transcriptomic/proteomic/metabolomic disease atlas published; metabolic perturbations characterized in zebrafish and bacterial models (model-organism evidence).

7. Anatomical Structures Affected

  • Organ/system: central nervous system / nervous system (UBERON:0001016) is the primary target; brain (UBERON:0000955). No consistent extra-CNS organ disease.
  • Regional (imaging, Alsini 2026, 41671913): abnormalities in 65% — white matter (UBERON:0002316) abnormalities 54%, periventricular/temporal cysts 27%, corpus callosum (UBERON:0002336) anomalies 15.4%, global brain underdevelopment with broad gyri/shallow sulci; cerebral cortex (UBERON:0000956) involvement.
  • Tissue/cell: nervous tissue; neurons (CL:0000540), GABAergic neurons (CL:0000617); glia/white-matter tracts secondarily.
  • Subcellular: cytosol (GO:0005829) and mitochondrion (GO:0005739).
  • Lateralization: bilateral, diffuse/global (consistent with a metabolic encephalopathy); not focal/lateralized.

8. Temporal Development

  • Onset: congenital/neonatal. Seizures begin very early — 55.5% within the first 24 h and 76% within the first week of life (Alsini 2026, 41671913); rare later-onset (infantile) cases exist. Onset pattern: acute seizures on a congenital metabolic background. Some fetal seizure activity/antenatal anomalies reported.
  • Progression: acute refractory phase → treatment-induced remission of seizures on B6 → chronic, largely static encephalopathy with persistent developmental impairment. Disease course for seizures is episodic (with illness-triggered breakthroughs) superimposed on a stable deficit.
  • Duration: chronic, lifelong; requires lifelong B6 supplementation (withdrawal → seizure recurrence).
  • Remission: treatment-induced (vitamin B6), not spontaneous.
  • Critical period: the neonatal/early-infantile window is the key opportunity for intervention; earlier treatment limits (but does not eliminate) neurodevelopmental damage.

9. Inheritance and Population

  • Inheritance: autosomal recessive. Biallelic variants required; parents are obligate carriers.
  • Epidemiology: ultra-rare. Overall pyridoxine-dependent epilepsy birth incidence ≈ 1:396,000 (definite/probable) to 1:252,000 (incl. possible) in the Netherlands (Been 2005, 16159904) — figures dominated by ALDH7A1. PLPBP is a small minority: ~30 patients by 2020 (Ahmed, 33425341), 54 by 2026 (Alsini, 41671913). Point prevalence: not formally established (<1/1,000,000, inferred).
  • Penetrance: appears complete for biallelic LoF (all reported biallelic patients are affected); formal penetrance estimates unavailable.
  • Expressivity: variable — severity correlates with residual protein function (LoF/unstable = severe).
  • Anticipation: not applicable (not a repeat-expansion disorder).
  • Germline mosaicism: not reported.
  • Founder effects: c.370_373del acts as a recurrent/founder allele (Pal 2021, 33977028; Alsini 2026).
  • Consanguinity: major contributor; many homozygous cases in consanguineous families.
  • Carrier frequency: very low, not precisely quantified.
  • Demographics: reported worldwide (Europe, Middle East/Saudi Arabia, Pakistan, North America, East Asia); higher counts in consanguineous populations. Sex ratio ≈ 1:1 (AR). Age distribution: overwhelmingly neonates/infants at presentation.

10. Diagnostics

  • No specific diagnostic metabolite (unlike ALDH7A1 → ↑α-AASA/pipecolic acid, or PNPO). Biochemistry is nonspecific: "diagnostic pitfalls, including lactic acidemia as well as hyperglycinemia, highlighting the importance of a pyridoxine trial, and genetic testing" (Jensen 2019, 31741821); elevated glycine/glutamine may occur (Takle 2025, 42563912). LOINC-codable labs: plasma amino acids (glycine), lactate, CSF neurotransmitter metabolites.
  • Clinical/functional tests: EEG (multifocal/burst-suppression or spasms; infantile spasms sometimes without hypsarrhythmia — Kalser 2023, 36577449); therapeutic trial of pyridoxine (± PLP) with seizure response is a key diagnostic maneuver.
  • Imaging: brain MRI frequently abnormal (65%) — white-matter changes, periventricular/temporal cysts, callosal anomalies, atrophy (Alsini 2026, 41671913). RadLex: cerebral white matter lesion.
  • Genetic testing (definitive): WES or WGS, or epilepsy/vitamin-B6 gene panels including PLPBP; single-gene PLPBP sequencing when clinically suspected. WES often succeeds after nondiagnostic panels (Takle 2025, 42563912). CMA/karyotype/FISH generally unrevealing (no CNVs reported). Mitochondrial DNA/repeat-expansion testing not indicated (though mitochondrial disease is a common misdiagnosis).
  • Clinical criteria & differential diagnosis: diagnosis rests on B6-responsive neonatal epilepsy + biallelic PLPBP variants. Differentials: ALDH7A1 (classic PDE; ↑α-AASA), PNPO deficiency (PLP-responsive), hypoxic-ischemic encephalopathy, mitochondrial encephalopathy, folinic-acid-responsive seizures (allelic with ALDH7A1), AADC deficiency, nonketotic hyperglycinemia, and opsoclonus-myoclonus-ataxia syndrome (OMAS) (Johnstone 2019 30668673; Takle 2025 42563912).
  • Screening: not on standard newborn screening (no specific metabolite). Cascade/carrier testing in families with a known variant and prenatal/preimplantation testing are feasible once the familial variants are known.

11. Outcome / Prognosis

  • Survival/mortality: Untreated disease is fatal — "can progress to status epilepticus and death in infancy" (Johnstone 2019, 30668673). With treatment, survival into childhood/adulthood is expected; formal survival rates unpublished.
  • Morbidity/function: Guarded — >2/3 of patients classified moderate-to-severe on a combined neurodevelopmental/seizure-control severity score (Alsini 2026, 41671913). Common sequelae: intellectual disability, developmental delay, microcephaly, ongoing epilepsy risk.
  • Disease course/complications: chronic static encephalopathy; breakthrough seizures during illness; feeding/motor impairment in severe cases.
  • Recovery potential: seizures are usually controllable with B6, but neurocognitive recovery is incomplete; earlier treatment improves but does not normalize outcomes.
  • Prognostic factors: variant class (LoF/unstable → worse), treatment delay, neonatal seizure burden, degree of MRI abnormality/microcephaly. Prognostic biomarkers: none validated; genotype is the best available predictor. QoL: no EQ-5D/SF-36/PROMIS data (not available).

12. Treatment

  • Pharmacotherapy (disease-specific): Vitamin B6. "neonatal onset seizures responsive to treatment with vitamin B6 available as pyridoxine (PN) or as the biologically active form pyridoxal 5-phosphate (PLP)" (Ahmed 2020, 33425341). Seizures are "generally resistant to traditional antiepileptic drugs and show a dramatic response to high-dose pyridoxine" (İpek 2022, 36308585). Some patients respond to pyridoxine, others require PLP; lifelong supplementation is mandatory (withdrawal → recurrence). NCIT: Pyridoxine (C745), Pyridoxal Phosphate (C84305), Pyridoxine Therapy. CHEBI: pyridoxine (CHEBI:16709), PLP (CHEBI:18405). DrugBank: Pyridoxine DB00165; Pyridoxal phosphate DB00114.
  • Pharmacogenomics / personalized: treatment is genotype-directed — confirming PLPBP distinguishes it from ALDH7A1/PNPO and dictates B6 (not diet) therapy. PN vs PLP choice is individualized.
  • Supportive AEDs: conventional AEDs are largely ineffective as monotherapy but may be adjuncts; standard supportive/rehabilitative care (PT/OT/speech), developmental support, nutrition.
  • Therapies that do NOT apply: lysine-reduction therapy (dietary lysine restriction ± arginine), which benefits ALDH7A1 PDE by lowering α-AASA (Coughlin 2022, 36008148), is mechanistically irrelevant to PLPBP (no α-AASA accumulation).
  • Advanced therapeutics: No approved gene, cell, or RNA therapy. Gene replacement is conceptually attractive (monogenic LoF) but not in clinical trials for PLPBP (not available). No disease-specific NCT trials identified.
  • Treatment outcomes / adverse events: B6 generally controls seizures; high-dose pyridoxine carries a risk of sensory peripheral neuropathy (a general class effect) — dosing should balance efficacy and toxicity (monitor). FAERS: pyridoxine neuropathy at high chronic doses.

13. Prevention

  • Primary prevention: not possible at the individual level (monogenic, congenital). Genetic counseling for carrier couples (25% recurrence risk per pregnancy, AR) and reproductive options (prenatal diagnosis, PGT-M) where the familial variants are known.
  • Secondary prevention: early recognition and prompt B6 trial in any neonate with unexplained AED-resistant seizures; early molecular diagnosis (WES/WGS) to start targeted therapy within the critical window.
  • Tertiary prevention: lifelong B6 to prevent seizure recurrence; supplement during intercurrent illness to avert illness-triggered crises; developmental/rehabilitative support to mitigate disability.
  • Immunization / public-health / environmental measures: not applicable (no infectious or environmental cause), though routine childhood vaccination to reduce febrile-illness triggers is prudent supportive advice.
  • Counseling: AR genetic counseling is the cornerstone of prevention (NSGC/ACMG frameworks); cascade carrier testing for relatives.

14. Other Species / Natural Disease

  • Evolutionary conservation: the YggS/Ybl036c/PLPBP (COG0325) family is "widely conserved across the three domains of life" (Xu 2026, 42803569) — bacteria, archaea, eukaryotes.
  • Orthologs / NCBI identifiers: human PLPBP (Gene 65015); mouse Plpbp (NCBI Taxon 10090); zebrafish plpbp (Taxon 7955, ZFIN); E. coli yggS (Taxon 562); Salmonella enterica yggS (Taxon 28901); S. cerevisiae YBL036C (Taxon 4932); cyanobacterial PipY.
  • Natural disease in animals: no naturally occurring companion-animal/wildlife disease documented (OMIA: none established) — human disease and engineered models only. Veterinary relevance: minimal/not applicable.
  • Comparative biology: loss of the ortholog disrupts amino-acid metabolism via PNP across species (bacteria: Ito 2019/2020 PMIDs 30902856, 31677193; Salmonella aspartate metabolism, Vu & Downs 2021 34498310), indicating deep conservation of the PLP-homeostasis mechanism.
  • Transmission / zoonosis: not applicable (genetic disease).

15. Model Organisms

  • Zebrafish (Danio rerio, Taxon 7955) — best vertebrate model. CRISPR/Cas9 plpbp−/− larvae: behavioural, biochemical, and electrophysiological seizures by 10 dpf, death by 16 dpf, with disrupted amino-acid, GABA and catecholamine metabolism; pyridoxine rescues the phenotype and extends lifespan (Johnstone 2019, 30668673). Phenotype recapitulation: high (seizures, early lethality, B6-responsiveness). Limitation: does not capture human cortical white-matter/structural changes or long-term cognition. Resource: ZFIN.
  • Bacterial models — mechanistic workhorses. E. coli yggS and Salmonella yggS mutants reveal PNP accumulation, glycine-cleavage-system inhibition and amino-acid metabolic disruption (Ito 2019/2020 PMIDs 30902856, 31677193; Vu & Downs 2021 34498310). Limitation: no nervous system; purely biochemical.
  • Yeast (S. cerevisiae YBL036C) and cyanobacterial PipY — structure/function and PLP-independent/moonlighting roles (Llop 2026, 41820517).
  • In vitro / structural: bacterial YggS crystal structures (TIM-barrel; He 2022 35897955); human PLPHP biochemistry incl. RNA-binding (Graziani 2024 39604152); AlphaFold model (UniProt O94903).
  • Mammalian (mouse Plpbp): ortholog exists and PLPBP biology probed indirectly (e.g., CLPP-KO mitochondrial study, Key 2023 38139332); no widely-reported dedicated Plpbp mouse epilepsy model (gap / not available). Resources: MGI, IMPC.

Summary of Supported vs. Open Questions

Supported (high confidence): - Monogenic AR disease from biallelic PLPBP LoF variants disrupting PLP homeostasis (27912044 30668673). - Neonatal-onset, AED-resistant, B6-responsive epileptic encephalopathy (41671913 36308585 33425341). - Mechanism: PLPHP chaperones/buffers PLP and prevents toxic PNP; loss impairs PLP-dependent amino-acid/neurotransmitter enzymes (35897955 31677193 34978460). - No specific biomarker → diagnosis by B6 trial + molecular testing (31741821). - Guarded prognosis despite treatment; genotype–severity correlation (41671913 30668673). - Deep cross-species conservation; zebrafish/bacterial models; lysine-reduction therapy inapplicable (42803569 30668673 36008148).

Open / not available: - Precise prevalence, penetrance, carrier frequency, survival/QoL metrics. - Human omics atlases (transcriptomic/proteomic/metabolomic) of affected brain. - Dedicated mammalian (mouse) epilepsy model; approved advanced therapeutics (gene therapy). - Full in-vivo clarification of the GABA/glycine/catecholamine contributions and the moonlighting RNA-binding/mitochondrial roles in human pathogenesis.
