Gonococcal Conjunctivitis — Comprehensive Disease Characterization Report

Disease: Gonococcal Conjunctivitis · MONDO: MONDO:0015455 · Category: Infectious Disease Evidence base: Human clinical case series/surveillance, in vitro (human cell) and humanized-mouse mechanistic studies, and recent reviews/RCTs (2005–2026). PMIDs cited throughout.

Summary (Answer to the Research Question)

Gonococcal conjunctivitis is an acute, hyperacute purulent conjunctivitis caused by the obligate human Gram-negative diplococcus Neisseria gonorrhoeae. It occurs in two epidemiologically distinct forms: adult conjunctivitis/keratoconjunctivitis, acquired by genital-to-eye autoinoculation or sexual contact (usually unilateral, predominantly young men), and gonococcal ophthalmia neonatorum (GON), acquired by vertical transmission through an infected birth canal. It is a sight-threatening ophthalmic emergency: because N. gonorrhoeae can invade intact corneal epithelium, delayed treatment leads to rapid stromal melting, corneal perforation, and blindness. The disease is purely infectious (no host genetic causation); pathogenesis proceeds from pilus/LOS/Opa-mediated adhesion to conjunctival columnar epithelium, through CEACAM-receptor-mediated invasion, to intense neutrophil-driven inflammation and protease-mediated keratolysis. First-line treatment is systemic ceftriaxone (± azithromycin) plus saline lavage; escalating multidrug resistance (now including ceftriaxone via penA alleles) threatens therapy. Prevention rests on safe-sex behavior, STI/prenatal screening, and neonatal ocular prophylaxis; no licensed gonococcal vaccine exists.

1. Disease Information

Overview. An acute bacterial conjunctivitis caused by N. gonorrhoeae characterized by copious purulent discharge, marked conjunctival hyperemia/chemosis, eyelid edema, and a propensity for corneal involvement. Classified as hyperacute bacterial conjunctivitis (onset within ~12–24 h, rapid progression). "Gonococcal conjunctivitis, a diagnostic and therapeutic emergency, is a potentially blinding sexually transmitted disease" (24210280).

Key identifiers: - MONDO: MONDO:0015455 (gonococcal conjunctivitis) - ICD-10: A54.31 (gonococcal conjunctivitis); ophthalmia neonatorum P39.1 / A54.3 group - ICD-11: 1A72 (Gonococcal infection); ocular involvement under gonococcal infection of eye - MeSH: related terms "Gonorrhea" (D006069), "Conjunctivitis, Bacterial" (D003234), "Ophthalmia Neonatorum" (D009878) - SNOMED CT: Gonococcal conjunctivitis (disorder) - OMIM/Orphanet: Not applicable — this is an acquired infectious disease, not a Mendelian/rare genetic disorder. - Causative organism: Neisseria gonorrhoeae — NCBI Taxonomy txid485

Synonyms / alternative names: gonococcal ophthalmia; gonococcal keratoconjunctivitis (with corneal involvement); gonorrheal conjunctivitis; gonococcal ophthalmia neonatorum / GON (neonatal form); "ophthalmia neonatorum" (when neonatal, of gonococcal etiology); blennorrhea/blennorrhoea neonatorum (historical).

Data source type: Disease-level aggregated resources plus individual-patient case series/case reports and national STI surveillance (EHR-adjacent). No genetic/EHR-cohort molecular dataset applies.

2. Etiology

Disease causal factor — infectious (single agent). Causative organism is N. gonorrhoeae, "the obligate human pathogen [that] colonizes primarily the mucosal columnar epithelium … [and] can infect … epithelial cells of the conjunctiva" (31119630). There is no genetic/heritable cause: no causal human gene, inheritance pattern, penetrance, or pathogenic germline/somatic variant. ("Causal genes" belong to the pathogen — see §4.)

Transmission / routes. - Adults: "Adult gonococcal conjunctivitis (GC) typically occurs by direct inoculation and is usually unilateral" (41222259) — genital secretions transferred to the eye (autoinoculation) or via infected sexual fluids. - Neonates: vertical transmission during vaginal delivery from an infected maternal genital tract.

Environmental / behavioral risk factors: unprotected sexual intercourse, multiple/new sexual partners, men who have sex with men (MSM), concurrent urogenital/pharyngeal/rectal gonorrhea, sex work, young age (15–34), and (for neonates) untreated maternal gonorrhea and lack of prenatal screening. Male predominance is marked (male:female ≈ 11:1 in gonorrhea surveillance; 41385198). Bacterial STIs including gonorrhea disproportionately affect MSM and are linked to HIV acquisition (41147878).

Genetic risk/modifier factors (host): none causal. At the cellular level, host CEACAM receptor expression modulates susceptibility to gonococcal invasion (29760215) — a host-factor modifier of infection efficiency, not a disease-risk allele.

Protective factors: condom use, partner reduction, prompt genital hygiene, prenatal STI screening/treatment, and neonatal ocular prophylaxis (behavioral/public-health, not genetic). No known protective host genetic variant.

Gene–environment interactions: Not applicable in the classical human-genetics sense. The relevant "gene–environment" axis is pathogen genotype (resistance/adhesin phase variation) × host receptor repertoire × behavioral exposure.

Characteristics: Onset — hyperacute (hours). Age — bimodal: neonatal (days 2–5 of life for GON) and young adults. Severity — moderate-to-severe/variable; can rapidly become sight-threatening. 4. Genetic / Molecular Information

Not a human genetic disease — no causal human gene, pathogenic variant (ACMG/AMP), allele frequency, or chromosomal abnormality. The "molecular" determinants are pathogen genes and host receptors:

Pathogen virulence genes (N. gonorrhoeae): - pilE / pilin (type IV pili) — initial attachment; antigenic/phase variation. - opa (opacity-associated Opa proteins) — phase-variable adhesins binding host CEACAMs (27442026 21204865). - porB (porin PorB), iron-regulated proteins (e.g., TbpB), lgtA–E (LOS biosynthesis), secreted enzymes including IgA1 protease (16532656). - Resistance determinants: penA (mosaic allele 60.001 → ceftriaxone resistance; 42430114), penicillinase plasmid (blaTEM, PPNG) (42475726), tet(M), gyrA/parC (fluoroquinolone resistance), mtrR efflux, 23S rRNA/mosaic mtr (azithromycin). - "Instability of gonococcal antigens, and frequent formation of new antigenic variants … [is] the main cause of the absence of postinfectious immunity" (16532656).

Host receptor/molecular players: CEACAM1, CEACAM3, CEACAM5 (CEA), CEACAM6 (HGNC gene symbols CEACAM1/3/5/6); differential CEACAM expression determines infection outcome (29760215 17517873).

Epigenetics / chromosomal abnormalities: Not applicable to host. (Bacterial phase variation is mediated by slipped-strand mispairing at homopolymeric/repeat tracts — a pathogen genetic mechanism.)

5. Environmental Information

  • Infectious agent: Neisseria gonorrhoeae (NCBI:txid485), Gram-negative diplococcus, family Neisseriaceae. Frequent co-infection with Chlamydia trachomatis (41017068).
  • Environmental/occupational toxins, radiation, pollution: not implicated.
  • Lifestyle factors: high-risk sexual behavior is the dominant modifiable factor (unprotected sex, multiple partners, MSM networks, transactional sex); alcohol/drug use (e.g., chemsex) as behavioral amplifiers. Neonatal disease reflects maternal infection and absent prenatal care/screening.

6. Mechanism / Pathophysiology

Ordered causal chain (initiating infection → clinical manifestation)

  1. Inoculation of N. gonorrhoeae onto the conjunctival columnar epithelium (autoinoculation, sexual contact, or birth-canal passage) → leads to surface colonization (demonstrated) (31119630).
  2. Type IV pili mediate initial long-range attachment and twitching motility; LOS and Opa/porin promote intimate adherence → results in stable epithelial binding (demonstrated in vitro).
  3. Phase-variable Opa proteins engage host CEACAM receptors (CEACAM1/5/6 on epithelium) → triggers bacterial uptake/invasion: epithelial CEACAMs drive cholesterol-raft-dependent internalization, whereas granulocyte CEACAM3 drives Src-family-kinase-dependent phagocytosis (demonstrated) (17517873 27442026). Branch: "Opa binding to CEACAM1 on … epithelia enhances gonococcal association and penetration into these tissues" (29760215).
  4. Epithelial invasion and transcytosis → leads to subepithelial bacterial access and release of LOS/peptidoglycan (PAMPs) → activates innate immune signaling (TLR4/lipid A, NOD1/peptidoglycan; inferred for conjunctiva by analogy to genital mucosa) → results in cytokine/chemokine release.
  5. Chemokine gradients → drive massive neutrophil (PMN) recruitment: "N. gonorrhoeae infection is characterized by local and abundant recruitment of neutrophils" (35343795) → produces the hallmark purulent exudate.
  6. N. gonorrhoeae survives within neutrophils via phase-variable Opa expression and antioxidant defenses → sustains persistent inflammation (demonstrated) (35343795 25346239).
  7. Neutrophil and bacterial proteases / collagenolytic activity plus oxidative injury → cause corneal stromal keratolysis ("stromal melting") (mechanistically inferred from clinical rapidity) (32787586).
  8. Progressive stromal destruction → leads to corneal perforation (mean ~11 days post-onset if untreated; 18064057) → results in loss of vision/globe integrity (clinical manifestation).
  9. In parallel, antigenic/phase variation of pili and Opa → enables immune evasion → results in absence of protective immunity and repeated susceptibility (16532656).

Mechanistic detail (checklist mapping)

  • Molecular pathways: Src-family-kinase (SFK) signaling downstream of CEACAM3 (17517873); cholesterol-rich membrane microdomain/lipid-raft uptake for epithelial CEACAMs; innate PRR signaling (TLR4-MD2, NOD1) inferred.
  • Cellular processes: bacterial adhesion (GO:0007155), receptor-mediated endocytosis/phagocytosis (GO:0006909), inflammatory response (GO:0006954), leukocyte chemotaxis/migration (GO:0050900), respiratory burst (GO:0045730).
  • Protein dysfunction: host — none intrinsic; pathogen exploits normal CEACAM adhesion function. Antigenic variation of Opa/pilin.
  • Immune involvement: acute neutrophilic inflammation; incomplete/no adaptive immunity; complement evasion (21204865).
  • Tissue damage mechanisms: protease-mediated collagen degradation, oxidative stress, necrosis of corneal stroma → keratolysis/perforation.
  • Molecular profiling (omics): No disease-specific human transcriptomic/proteomic/metabolomic signatures of gonococcal conjunctivitis were identified; mechanistic data derive from in vitro cell models and humanized mice.

Cell types (CL): neutrophil (CL:0000775), conjunctival/corneal epithelial cell (CL:0000066), granulocyte. GO components: plasma membrane raft (GO:0045121), phagolysosome (GO:0032010).

7. Anatomical Structures Affected

  • Primary organ/site: the eye — specifically the conjunctiva (UBERON:0001811), palpebral and bulbar; eyelids/lid margin (UBERON:0000441).
  • Secondary involvement: cornea (UBERON:0000964) — epithelium and stroma (keratitis, ulceration, stromal melt, perforation); periorbital soft tissue (pre-septal cellulitis); pre-auricular lymph nodes. Endophthalmitis/panophthalmitis in extreme untreated cases.
  • Body systems: ocular/visual system (primary); reproductive/genitourinary system (source reservoir); rarely musculoskeletal/systemic via disseminated gonococcal infection (DGI) — arthritis-dermatitis syndrome, septic arthritis (42273458).
  • Tissue types: stratified/columnar epithelial tissue (conjunctiva, cornea); corneal connective tissue (stroma).
  • Cell populations (CL): conjunctival epithelial cells, corneal epithelial cells (CL:0000575), corneal stromal keratocytes (CL:0002363), infiltrating neutrophils (CL:0000775).
  • Subcellular (GO CC): plasma membrane / lipid raft (GO:0045121); phagosome–phagolysosome (GO:0045335/GO:0032010).
  • Localization / laterality: adults usually unilateral; neonates typically bilateral; severe adult disease may be bilateral (41222259).

8. Temporal Development

  • Onset: hyperacute (symptoms within ~12–24 h of inoculation). Age: neonatal GON typically presents days 2–5 of life; adult form in sexually active young adults.
  • Progression: rapid. Untreated adult keratoconjunctivitis can progress to corneal perforation at a mean of ~11 days after conjunctivitis onset (18064057). Frequent early misdiagnosis as viral/epidemic keratoconjunctivitis delays therapy (18064057).
  • Disease course: acute, self-limited to the episode with treatment; not chronic or relapsing (though reinfection can occur). Treatment-induced remission is the norm — "a complete cure was achieved without sequelae" in an uncomplicated case (24210280).
  • Critical period: the days-to-~1-week window before perforation is the key opportunity for sight-saving intervention; prompt systemic antibiotics + lavage prevent progression.

9. Inheritance and Population (Epidemiology)

  • Inheritance: None — acquired infection; no Mendelian pattern, penetrance, expressivity, anticipation, mosaicism, founder effect, consanguinity, or carrier frequency applies.
  • Global burden of gonorrhea: >80 million new cases annually worldwide (42773319). Ocular gonococcal infection is an uncommon extragenital manifestation but is rising with the STI resurgence: a 20.5-year Zurich series found "a marked increase in gonococcal cases … between 2021 and 2025" (17 gonococcal vs 121 chlamydial ocular cases) (42795570).
  • Incidence example (gonorrhea overall): Poland 2023 — incidence doubled to 3.5/100,000 (1,322 cases), still far below EU/EEA average (~25/100,000); adult conjunctival infections rose to 1% of cases (vs 0.3% in 2022) (41385198). US county-level gonorrhea rates declined 2021–2023 in ~74% of counties (42641714).
  • Sex ratio: strongly male-predominant for gonorrhea (11:1 in Poland; 80% male in the Glasgow ocular series) (41385198 34211138).
  • Age distribution: concentrated in 15–34-year-olds (mean 26 in the ocular series); plus neonates.
  • Geographic: highest burden in sub-Saharan Africa, Western Pacific, and among key populations globally; ceftriaxone-resistant lineages expanding in Asia (e.g., ST8123 in Hangzhou, China; 42430114).
  • Neonatal GON: now rare in high-income countries — an Italian survey of 419 centers (>1 million neonates/3 y) reported zero gonococcal (and 12 chlamydial) neonatal conjunctivitis cases (37697419).

10. Diagnostics

  • Gram stain of conjunctival purulent exudate: Gram-negative intracellular diplococci within neutrophils (rapid presumptive diagnosis) (24210280 32787586).
  • Culture: modified Thayer-Martin / chocolate agar; enables antimicrobial susceptibility testing (essential given resistance) (34211138).
  • NAAT / PCR: e.g., Xpert CT/NG provides same-day diagnosis and can be run on genital or ocular swabs; "Speedy and accurate diagnosis by the highly sensitive and specific Xpert CT/NG assay can provide early definite diagnosis for prompt treatment in prevention of gonococcal infection induced corneal perforations" (32787586). Caveat: "No regulatory agency-approved nucleic acid amplification tests (NAATs) exist" specifically for ocular gonococcal/chlamydial detection — used off-label (42429409).
  • Whole-genome sequencing: increasingly used for resistance surveillance/typing (penA alleles, MLST) (42430114).
  • Clinical criteria / differential diagnosis: hyperacute copious purulent unilateral conjunctivitis in a sexually active adult ⇒ suspect gonococcus. Differentials: chlamydial (inclusion) conjunctivitis (more subacute/chronic; the main mimic, 42795570), adenoviral epidemic keratoconjunctivitis (watery discharge — a frequent misdiagnosis; 18064057), other bacterial (staph/strep/Haemophilus), and (neonates) chemical conjunctivitis.
  • Ancillary: because ocular gonorrhea signals systemic STI, concurrent testing for chlamydia, syphilis, HIV and partner tracing is standard (34211138).
  • Screening: prenatal maternal gonorrhea/chlamydia screening (secondary prevention of GON); high-risk-population STI screening. No newborn/genetic screening applies.
  • Omics/imaging/electrophysiology/biopsy: generally not used for diagnosis (clinical + microbiology suffice).

LOINC-type tests: N. gonorrhoeae culture; N. gonorrhoeae rRNA/DNA NAAT; Gram stain.

11. Outcome / Prognosis

  • With prompt systemic treatment: excellent — complete cure without sequelae in uncomplicated cases (24210280).
  • With delay/keratitis: sight-threatening. Corneal perforation develops ~11 days post-onset in severe cases (18064057).
  • Surgical salvage: therapeutic keratoplasty (lamellar/penetrating/tectonic) plus systemic antibiotics restores vision — in a 5-case series "All grafts remained clear during the mean follow-up period of 34.9 months. Final best-corrected visual acuity ranged from 20/60 to 20/16" (18064057); bilateral perforation salvaged by tectonic penetrating keratoplasty (41222259).
  • Mortality: ocular disease itself is essentially non-fatal; morbidity is visual (corneal scarring, blindness) if untreated. Systemic DGI carries morbidity (septic arthritis; 42273458).
  • Prognostic factors: time-to-treatment (dominant), presence/depth of corneal involvement, antimicrobial susceptibility (resistant strains worsen outcomes — 3/5 perforation cases were ofloxacin-resistant; 18064057), immune status, and diagnostic delay/misdiagnosis.
  • Complications: keratitis, corneal ulcer/perforation, peripheral ulcerative keratitis (42305694), pre-septal cellulitis, endophthalmitis, and (rarely) DGI.

12. Treatment

Pharmacotherapy (systemic is mandatory — topical alone insufficient because of corneal invasion risk): - First-line: ceftriaxone (third-generation cephalosporin; inhibits penicillin-binding proteins/cell-wall synthesis), typically IM/IV; adult ocular disease treated with IV or IM ceftriaxone, often with azithromycin (34211138). CHEBI: ceftriaxone (CHEBI:29007); NCIT: Ceftriaxone (C1145). - Adjunct: azithromycin 1 g (macrolide; historically for dual therapy / chlamydia coverage). CHEBI:2955; NCIT: Azithromycin (C574). - Ocular supportive care: copious saline lavage of purulent discharge; topical antibiotics as adjunct; topical lubrication (34211138 24210280). - Novel oral agents (FDA-approved Dec 2025): zoliflodacin (spiropyrimidinetrione, targets bacterial type II topoisomerase) and gepotidacin (triazaacenaphthylene topoisomerase inhibitor) for uncomplicated urogenital gonorrhea — "obtained US FDA-approval for treatment of uncomplicated urogenital gonorrhea in December 2025" (42728217).

Antimicrobial resistance (defines therapy): N. gonorrhoeae "has developed resistance to all antibiotics recommended for treatment, and reports of reduced susceptibility to ceftriaxone, the last-line treatment, are increasing" (39673766). Sequential loss of sulfonamides → penicillins (PPNG) → tetracyclines → fluoroquinolones → emerging cephalosporin resistance (penA allele 60.001; FC428/ST1903 and endemic ST8123 lineages; high-level ceftriaxone MIC ≥0.5 mg/L reaching 35% in 2024 Hangzhou) (42430114 42475726).

Surgical/interventional: therapeutic/tectonic keratoplasty for perforation (NCIT: Corneal Transplantation C15326) (18064057 41222259).

Pharmacogenomics / advanced therapeutics (gene/cell/RNA/targeted/immunotherapy): not applicable to treatment of the infection.

Treatment strategy: empiric systemic ceftriaxone on clinical/Gram-stain suspicion, with culture-guided adjustment; test-of-cure and partner treatment; management shifting toward "etiology-guided and AMR-informed therapy, driven by advances in rapid point-of-care testing and whole-genome sequencing" (42728217).

13. Prevention

  • Primary prevention: safe-sex behavior (condoms), partner reduction, and sexual-health education; neonatal ocular prophylaxis at birth — historically Credé silver nitrate, now erythromycin 0.5% ophthalmic ointment (mandated in many US states) or povidone-iodine (WHO-favored in some settings). NCIT: erythromycin ophthalmic ointment; silver nitrate; povidone-iodine.
  • Secondary prevention: prenatal maternal STI screening and treatment — "Prenatal screening and treatment of pregnant women is the most effective way to prevent neonatal ophthalmia" (38781483); high-risk-population STI screening/treatment; prompt treatment to prevent transmission.
  • Tertiary prevention: rapid diagnosis + systemic antibiotics + lavage to prevent keratitis/perforation.
  • Policy evolution: universal neonatal erythromycin prophylaxis is being reconsidered — GON is "exceedingly uncommon" in the US and erythromycin is ineffective for chlamydial ophthalmia; risk-based models are favored where prenatal screening is robust (41344385 38781483). Breakthrough neonatal GON still occurs with resistant strains despite prophylaxis (41017068).
  • Vaccine: no licensed gonococcal vaccine (42773319). The meningococcal 4CMenB vaccine (shares antigens with gonococcus) shows ~30–38% cross-protection in observational studies (meta-analysis 38% risk reduction) and is deployed for high-risk groups in the UK and Galicia, Spain — but three recent RCTs (incl. GoGoVax) showed no efficacy against gonorrhoea in high-risk MSM (42499182 42773319). NCIT: 4CMenB vaccine.
  • Public health: partner notification/contact tracing, STI surveillance, and antimicrobial stewardship/resistance monitoring.

14. Other Species / Natural Disease

  • Taxonomy of pathogen: Neisseria gonorrhoeae — NCBI:txid485. Host species: Homo sapiens (NCBI:txid9606) only.
  • Natural animal disease: None — N. gonorrhoeae is an obligate/strict human pathogen with no natural animal reservoir (31119630). No veterinary counterpart; not zoonotic. VBO/OMIA: not applicable.
  • Comparative biology: the closest relative is Neisseria meningitidis, which shares adhesins and Opa-CEACAM biology; cross-protective antigens underlie 4CMenB cross-immunity (42499182). Host CEACAM genes have orthologs across mammals, but human-specific CEACAM sequences restrict gonococcal Opa binding to humans (basis for humanized-mouse models).
  • Cross-species susceptibility: effectively none under natural conditions.

15. Model Organisms

  • No natural animal model (human-restricted pathogen), so research relies on surrogate systems (31119630).
  • Humanized mouse: CEACAM-humanized transgenic mice (expressing human CEACAM1/CEACAM5) recapitulate Opa-CEACAM-dependent epithelial colonization and tissue penetration (29760215). The estradiol-treated female mouse genital-tract colonization model is the standard in vivo infection model (used widely for vaccine/therapeutic testing; general field knowledge).
  • In vitro / cellular: human epithelial cell monocultures and primary human neutrophils to study adhesion, invasion, and phagocytic killing (31119630 35343795 25346239); "protocols for examining the basic interactions between gonococci and a representative human cell line" (31119630).
  • Genetic model types: transgenic/humanized (CEACAM knock-in) mice; no knockout-of-host-gene disease models (disease is infectious).
  • Applications: dissecting adhesin–receptor biology, neutrophil evasion, and testing vaccine antigens — e.g., murine immunization/challenge with TbpB (42574476), BamA mRNA-LNP (42523399), and native outer membrane vesicle vaccines (42363875); a controlled human urethral challenge model (male volunteers) also exists for vaccine efficacy testing (e.g., 4CMenB trial NCT05294588; 42049314).
  • Limitations: models do not fully reproduce the human conjunctival/corneal microenvironment; mouse immune and CEACAM differences limit translation; no dedicated ocular animal model of gonococcal keratoconjunctivitis.
  • Resources: IMSR/MGI (transgenic mouse strains), Cellosaurus/ATCC (human epithelial cell lines).

Supported vs. Refuted Hypotheses

Supported: - H1 (supported): Adult gonococcal conjunctivitis is a hyperacute, male-predominant, usually unilateral purulent conjunctivitis with frequent corneal/pre-septal involvement (34211138). - H2 (supported): Corneal stromal melt/perforation is the principal sight-threatening complication, driven by epithelial invasion + neutrophilic protease injury (32787586 18064057). - H3 (supported): Opa-CEACAM interactions mechanistically drive epithelial invasion and modulate neutrophil handling (29760215 17517873 35343795). - H4 (supported): Systemic ceftriaxone is first-line; multidrug (incl. ceftriaxone) resistance is escalating (39673766 42430114). - H5 (supported): Prevention hinges on prenatal/behavioral measures; GON is now rare in high-income settings (38781483 37697419).

Refuted / not applicable: - Gonococcal conjunctivitis has a heritable/host-genetic cause — refuted: purely infectious; no causal human gene, inheritance, or pathogenic variant. - 4CMenB reliably prevents gonorrhea — refuted by RCTs (effective only ~30–38% in observational data, no efficacy in MSM RCTs) (42499182). - A natural animal reservoir/model exists — refuted: human-restricted pathogen (31119630).

Limitations and Future Directions

  • Evidence base for the ocular form is dominated by case series/reports (small n) and surveillance; no large ocular-specific RCTs or omics datasets exist. Mechanistic data are extrapolated from genital-tract/in-vitro models.
  • Ontology gaps: some HPO/NCIT mappings are approximate; exact conjunctival-purulent-discharge HPO code is imprecise.
  • Key uncertainties: trajectory of ceftriaxone resistance and its impact on ocular disease; whether next-gen vaccines (OMV, mRNA) will protect mucosal/ocular sites; optimal neonatal prophylaxis policy in the screening era.
  • Future work: conjunctival organoid/humanized-cornea models; ocular-specific resistance surveillance; evaluation of gepotidacin/zoliflodacin and vaccine candidates for ocular/mucosal protection.
