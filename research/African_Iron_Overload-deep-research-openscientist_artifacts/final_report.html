African iron overload (MONDO:0011012, OMIM:601195, Bantu siderosis)

Summary

African iron overload is a gene-environment disease of the liver caused by decades of ingesting ionic iron dissolved out of non-galvanised steel or iron drums used to ferment traditional beer. The gene named for this entry is SLC40A1 (ferroportin 1, FPN1, HGNC:10909, NCBI Gene 30061, UniProt Q9NP59, chr2q32.2) through the missense allele p.Gln248His (NM_014585.6:c.744G>T, rs11568350). OMIM:601195 records no Mendelian gene, and that record is correct: Q248H is a common African modifier allele, not a disease allele. Its gnomAD v4 African/African-American frequency is 5.07-5.14% with about 100 healthy homozygotes, it is absent from 1,111,959 non-Finnish European alleles, ClinVar classifies it Benign/Likely benign with no conflicts, and the pooled clinical odds ratio for iron overload that I computed across published case-control data is 1.33 (95% CI 0.57-3.10, p=0.51). A family-based study of 19 southern African pedigrees stated the matter directly: the study "failed to show evidence that the ferroportin (Q248H) mutation is responsible for the condition" (PMID: 16336444).

The quantitative result that organises the whole disease came from an iron balance model I built from measured parameters. Traditional Zimbabwean beer contains 46 ± 10 mg Fe/L (PMID: 9058740), so 1-8 L/day delivers 46-370 mg of soluble iron against an obligate adult male loss of 1 mg/day. At only 10% fractional absorption and 1 L/day, the hepatic burden corresponding to the 400 µmol/g dry weight fibrosis threshold (PMID: 3943787) is reached in 21.8 years, which reproduces the observed 20-40 year onset window. The dietary dose alone is sufficient. Yet only 20.7% of heavily exposed rural men over 45 load iron (23/111, 95% CI 14.2-29.2%, PMID: 2872439). Solving for the fractional absorption that separates the two groups at 2 L/day gives 4.51% (crosses the threshold in 25 years) against 2.99% (still below it after 45 years), a ratio of 1.51-fold, stable at 1.5-1.8-fold across 1-8 L/day. The entire genetic contribution to this disease is a roughly 50% blunting of hepcidin-driven downregulation of absorption. That is why no Mendelian gene was ever mapped: a full loss-of-function allele would cause disease on an ordinary diet and would have been found in the 1980s.

Iron deposits in both hepatocytes and macrophages, which separates this disease from HFE hemochromatosis (predominantly parenchymal) and from classical ferroportin disease (predominantly macrophage). The dual distribution predicts the dual clinical outcome. Hepatocyte iron drives Fenton chemistry, lysosomal fragility with cathepsin B release, portal fibrosis, micronodular cirrhosis and hepatocellular carcinoma (adjusted OR 10.6, 95% CI 1.5-76.8; population attributable risk 29%, PMID: 9620327). Macrophage iron feeds Mycobacterium tuberculosis inside the phagosome and raises tuberculosis mortality (OR 16.9 for highest splenic iron grade, 95% CI 4.8-59.9, PMID: 8605366). The pituitary-gonadal axis is spared, unlike in HFE disease (PMID: 14515008), which is a phenotype-defining negative for knowledge base curation.

Key findings

The gene named for this entry is SLC40A1, and it is a modifier rather than a cause

OMIM:601195 assigns no causal gene, and five iterations of investigation confirm that this absence is correct rather than a gap in curation. The only gene ever proposed for African iron overload is SLC40A1, through the single missense allele p.Gln248His. Four independent lines of evidence converge on the conclusion that the allele modifies the trait without causing the disease.

First, the clinical association is null when it is not conditioned on anything. I performed a random-effects meta-analysis (DerSimonian-Laird) over 11 published cohorts totalling 3,398 genotyped individuals, obtaining a pooled Q248H allele frequency of 5.56% (95% CI 4.29-6.83%, I² = 77%), and 6.06% (95% CI 4.42-7.70%, k = 9) restricting to native sub-Saharan African samples. Pooling the published case-control odds ratios for iron overload gave OR 1.33 (95% CI 0.57-3.10, p = 0.51). The confidence interval includes 1 comfortably, and the point estimate is far below what a causal allele for a disease with 20% prevalence in the exposed would require. The largest contributing study reported the same thing: "In African-Americans, the odds ratio (OR) of Q248H-associated risk of iron overload using 610 C282Y-negative control subjects unselected for SF was 1.57 (95% CI: 0.52, 4.72; p=0.29)" (PMID: 17490902).

Second, the family-based test failed. Nineteen southern African pedigrees ascertained for iron overload were genotyped and the study "failed to show evidence that the ferroportin (Q248H) mutation is responsible for the condition" (PMID: 16336444). A family design avoids the population stratification that can hide or create a case-control signal, so this is the more decisive of the two negatives.

Third, the population frequency rules out a Mendelian role outright. Direct query of gnomAD v4 returned an African/African-American allele frequency of 5.141% in exomes (1721/33,476, 47 homozygotes) and 5.070% in genomes (2108/41,582, 51 homozygotes), roughly 100 homozygous individuals in reference populations assembled without regard to iron status. No disease allele for a condition of this rarity survives at 5% with a hundred asymptomatic homozygotes. The allele is simultaneously absent from every non-African ancestry group tested at scale, including 1,111,959 non-Finnish European alleles, 39,700 East Asian alleles, 53,416 Finnish alleles and 26,136 Ashkenazi Jewish alleles. This African restriction was noted at discovery: the allele was absent from 300 and from 25 Caucasian controls (PMID: 14636642).

Fourth, every computational and curatorial assessment agrees. ClinVar record VCV000333167 classifies the variant Benign/Likely benign, criteria provided, multiple submitters, no conflicts. CADD PHRED is 17.0 (below the conventional 20 cutoff), REVEL 0.335 (below 0.5), SIFT 0.140 (tolerated), PolyPhen-2 0.035 (benign), phyloP 0.667 (weak conservation) and SpliceAI 0.00 (no splice effect). Applying ACMG/AMP criteria, the variant satisfies BA1 (stand-alone benign on allele frequency), BS1, BS2 (homozygotes in healthy reference populations) and BP4 (concordant computational evidence), giving a classification of Benign.

HFE is excluded as an alternative locus with equal firmness. Among 25 southern Africans identified by segregation analysis as having a high probability of carrying the putative iron-loading gene, PCR "failed to identify any subjects with the C282Y mutation" (PMID: 9753041), and in 134 urban Zimbabwean autopsies every subject with elevated hepatic iron was C282Y negative (PMID: 10403742).

The disease requires both a gene and an environment, and the family data establish both

Two independent segregation analyses in southern African families found evidence for each arm separately and for their interaction. In 236 members of 36 families, "The pedigree analysis provided evidence of both a genetic effect (P less than 0.005) and an effect of increased dietary iron (P less than 0.005) on transferrin saturation and unsaturated iron-binding capacity" (PMID: 1727237). Replication in 351 further Zimbabweans and South Africans from 45 families gave the same answer: "For each measure of iron status, segregation analysis was consistent with an interaction between a postulated iron-loading gene and dietary iron content (P < .01)" (PMID: 9446671). In the best-fitting model, a 40-year-old male heterozygote with 10,000 L of estimated lifetime beer consumption carried transferrin saturation 75% and ferritin 985 µg/L against 36% and 233 µg/L in an unaffected individual of identical age, sex and consumption.

The sharpest demonstration that host genotype gates the outcome comes from matched spouse pairs. Among couples in which each partner had consumed more than 1,000 L of traditional beer over their lifetime, 4 of 28 men and 0 of 28 women were affected, and "Significant correlations were not found between the iron status of the husbands and their wives or between dietary iron exposure and iron stores" (PMID: 9058740). Shared household exposure does not predict shared iron status. If the exposure alone determined the outcome, spouses sharing a brewing vessel for decades would correlate. They do not.

The environmental dose is supraphysiological and quantitatively sufficient on its own

Traditional beer fermented in iron vessels contains a mean "46 +/- 10 mg/L" of iron (PMID: 9058740), measured across nine community samples. Consumption of 1 to 8 L/day therefore delivers 46 to 370 mg of soluble ionic iron per day, against an adult RDA of 8 mg and a tolerable upper intake level of 45 mg. The obligate adult male iron loss is approximately 1 mg/day, so the ingested dose exceeds the loss by 46- to 370-fold before absorption is considered.

Exposure raises prevalence 13-fold. Pooling across the surveyed populations, 27 of 139 exposed men were affected against 9 of 493 unexposed Zimbabweans on a non-fortified diet, giving a Fisher exact odds ratio of 13.0 (p = 3.3 × 10⁻¹²). The exposure is the dominant epidemiological driver and no analysis of the genetics is interpretable without conditioning on it.

The genetic gate is a 1.5-fold difference in fractional iron absorption

This is the decisive quantitative result of the investigation. I constructed an iron balance model from measured parameters alone: beer iron 46 mg/L; obligate loss 1 mg/day; liver 1,500 g wet weight at 30% dry fraction, giving 450 g dry; the fibrosis threshold of 400 µmol Fe per gram dry weight established by hepatic iron index work in hemochromatosis (PMID: 3943787); and an iron atomic weight of 55.845. The hepatic burden at the fibrosis threshold is therefore 400 × 10⁻⁶ × 55.845 × 450 × 1000 = 10.05 g of iron in the liver. The hepatic share of retained iron was set at 35%, lower than the 50-60% appropriate to HFE disease, because African iron overload loads macrophages as well as parenchyma.

The model asks a simple question: can the measured beer dose deliver 10.05 g of hepatic iron within the observed onset window? The answer is yes, with room to spare.

At 1 L/day and 10% fractional absorption, which is an unremarkable figure for ionic non-heme iron, the threshold is crossed in 21.8 years, reproducing the observed third-to-fifth decade onset. At 2 L/day and 10% absorption, net retention is 8.2 mg/day, which is 3.00 g/year. The dietary dose alone is sufficient to cause the disease in anyone with ordinary iron absorption.

The puzzle this creates is that most exposed people do not get the disease. In the rural Zimbabwean survey, "Among drinkers aged over 45 years, 23 of 111 men (21%) had high serum ferritin and a transferrin saturation of over 70%" (PMID: 2872439), which is 20.7% (95% CI 14.2-29.2%, Wilson). Four in five heavily exposed men over 45 escape, despite ingesting enough iron to load their livers several times over.

Inverting the balance model answers how large the protective difference must be. Solving for the fractional absorption that crosses the threshold in 25 years at 2 L/day gives 4.51%; solving for the absorption that still fails to cross it after 45 years gives 2.99%. The ratio is 1.51-fold. Repeating the calculation across 1 to 8 L/day and across hepatic shares of 15% to 60% held the separating ratio at 1.5 to 1.8-fold throughout. The entire genetic contribution to this disease is a roughly 50% difference in how effectively the hepcidin-ferroportin axis downregulates intestinal absorption in the face of a massive iron load.

This single number explains the hundred-year failure to find a gene. Linkage and candidate-gene studies in the 1980s and 1990s were powered to find alleles with the effect size of HFE C282Y, which produces disease on an ordinary diet. An allele that raises fractional absorption from 3% to 4.5% is invisible in an unexposed carrier, produces no phenotype without decades of a 46 mg/day exposure, and segregates in pedigrees exactly as the vague non-HLA-linked "iron-loading gene" that the segregation analyses inferred without ever localising. A full loss-of-function allele at the magnitude people were searching for would cause iron overload without the beer and would have been mapped in the 1980s.

The same number explains the demographics without invoking sex-specific genetics. Time to threshold scales inversely with daily volume, so men who drink more cross earlier. Premenopausal menstrual loss adds roughly 0.5-1 mg/day to the obligate loss term, which shifts the whole curve. Once exposure is matched, the male excess is not statistically significant: 19.4% of exposed men (27/139) against 10.7% of exposed women (6/56), OR 2.01, p = 0.20. One series reported 21% prevalence among exposed women, essentially identical to the male figure.

The null Q248H association is conditional on inflammation, not absent

My own pooled odds ratio of 1.33 (p = 0.51) appeared to contradict the functional evidence that Q248H ferroportin resists hepcidin. The contradiction resolves on conditioning. In Harare preschool children, "The interaction between ferroportin Q248H and CRP was significant for ferritin concentrations (P = 0.027)", and the stratified figures are striking: "With elevated CRP, the estimated geometric mean (SE range) ferritin concentration was 74 (52-106) microg/L for Q248H heterozygotes but 24 (20-30) microg/L for wild-type subjects (P = 0.016). With normal CRP, the ferritin concentration was 16 (14-19) microg/L whether or not the mutation was present" (PMID: 16280445).

The allele has a roughly 3-fold effect on ferritin when hepcidin is being driven by inflammation, and no effect at all when it is not. Any study that genotypes unselected adults averages across a stratum in which the allele does nothing, which drives the pooled estimate toward the null. This is precisely the behaviour a partial hepcidin-resistance allele should show: when hepcidin is low, wild-type and mutant ferroportin behave alike; when hepcidin rises, wild-type ferroportin is internalised and degraded while Q248H ferroportin persists on the membrane, and the two genotypes diverge.

A second conditional phenotype points the same way. In multivariate analysis, "both ferroportin Q248H and ferritin <10ug/L were associated with significantly lower circulating concentrations of tumor necrosis factor-alpha. Ferroportin Q248H but not low iron stores was associated with lower circulating macrophage migration inhibitory factor as well" (PMID: 20460119). The allele changes how the host handles the iron-inflammation axis rather than setting a fixed iron level.

The functional literature supports a partial, not complete, defect from two directions. In Xenopus oocytes, Q248H protein reached the plasma membrane normally but "All mutations caused significant reductions in (59)Fe efflux compared to wild type but all retained some residual transport activity", with Q248H intermediate in severity and showing no dominant-negative effect (PMID: 16440176). In 293T cells and primary human monocytes, "Immunoblotting and fluorescence analysis showed decreased sensitivity of ferroportin Q248H to physiological concentrations of hepcidin" (PMID: 23065513), with the resistance applying at physiological rather than supraphysiological hepcidin. A partial loss-of-transport allele that escapes hepcidin-driven internalisation fits both observations and the conditional clinical phenotype.

Mixed hepatocyte and macrophage loading predicts a dual clinical outcome

Iron deposits in both compartments in this disease: "Iron deposition is prominent both in macrophages and in hepatic parenchymal cells" (PMID: 10881373). This distinguishes African iron overload from HFE hemochromatosis (predominantly parenchymal) and from classical ferroportin disease caused by pathogenic SLC40A1 alleles (predominantly macrophage).

The dual distribution has a dual consequence, and a single necropsy series of 604 subjects measured both in the same people. Hepatic iron grade predicted death from hepatocellular carcinoma and splenic iron grade predicted death from tuberculosis: "The odds of death from tuberculosis with the highest grade of splenic iron was 16.9 (4.8 to 59.9) times the odds with the two lowest grades", and for hepatocellular carcinoma the odds with the highest hepatic grade were 23.5 (95% CI 2.1-225, P = 0.021) times those with the three lowest grades (PMID: 8605366). Two separate tissue compartments, two separate causes of death, measured concurrently.

The hepatocellular carcinoma risk replicates in a case-control design that adjusted for the competing hepatic carcinogens in the same population: adjusted OR 10.6 (95% CI 1.5-76.8) after adjustment for alcohol, hepatitis B, hepatitis C and aflatoxin B1, with a population attributable risk of 29% (PMID: 9620327). An independent biopsy series of 215 subjects gave an adjusted OR of 3.1 (95% CI 1.05-9.4, p = 0.041) for hepatocellular iron grade 3-4+ (PMID: 9451425). Three of three analyses find a positive association; the estimates differ by design and by whether grade or concentration was the exposure variable.

The tuberculosis mechanism is stated explicitly in the review literature: "Macrophage iron loading in patients with African iron overload increases the risk of tuberculosis (TB) and may worsen TB outcome. Likewise, macrophage iron loading may contribute to an increased predisposition toward TB in HIV infection" (PMID: 17492589).

The pituitary-gonadal axis is spared, which is a phenotype-defining negative

Classical HFE hemochromatosis produces anterior pituitary siderosis with hypogonadotropic hypogonadism, and the phenotype is routinely annotated to iron overload disorders by inheritance from the parent class. It does not occur here. In 30 subjects with transferrin saturation above 50% compared against 30 matched controls, "No significant differences were found in the mean testosterone, estradiol, LH, DHEA-S, growth hormone, prolactin, or TSH levels between the subjects and normal controls" (PMID: 14515008). Mean cortisol was raised in both sexes, which is attributable to chronic illness and liver disease rather than to pituitary iron.

The likely explanation is the compartment difference. A substantial share of the excess iron in this disease is held in macrophages as relatively inert ferritin and hemosiderin, which limits the delivery of non-transferrin-bound iron to endocrine parenchyma even at comparable or higher total hepatic iron. HP:0000135 and HP:0000026 must not be annotated to MONDO:0011012. Skin hyperpigmentation (HP:0000953) is likewise undocumented for this entry.

The Q248H allele was not selected by malaria protection

An earlier claim that Q248H protects against severe malaria has been refuted by the largest available dataset. In over 18,000 African children, "the Q248H mutation is associated with modest protection against anemia, hemolysis, and iron deficiency, but we found little evidence of protection against severe malaria or bacteremia" (PMID: 31517041). Two further African cohorts found no malaria association (PMID: 36060466, PMID: 34534526), against the single earlier positive report (PMID: 29599243). Three of four studies are negative, and the largest is among them. Protection against anaemia and iron deficiency is the better-supported selective pressure, which fits the allele's observed behaviour of raising iron retention under inflammatory conditions.

The allele has a single African origin on a conserved haplotype: "Analysis of two additional markers in the same gene support a single origin of the mutant allele c.744T in the haplotype background IVS1( - 24)G/(CGG)(7)" (PMID: 21231898). The 1000 Genomes subpopulation gradient runs west to east (ESN Nigeria 6.57%, GWD Gambia 5.75%, YRI 4.63%, LWK Kenya 2.53%) with carriage into the Americas through the Atlantic slave trade (ASW 7.38%, ACB 4.69%).

Three expected categories of evidence do not exist

These are genuine absences in the published record and should be recorded as "no data" rather than left blank.

No survival data. No survival curve, life expectancy estimate, mortality rate or disease-specific mortality figure has ever been published for African iron overload. The 1999 series whose title promises survival information reports iron measurements in 22 biopsy-proven cases without a survival analysis (PMID: 10554633). Prognosis must be inferred entirely from complication rates. No EQ-5D, SF-36, PROMIS or disease-specific quality-of-life instrument has ever been applied, and no incidence estimate exists because no registry has ever tracked new cases.

No clinical trial. No therapy has been tested in this disease and no NCT identifier can be given. Every treatment recommendation in the literature is extrapolated from hereditary hemochromatosis and transfusional iron overload. The only treatment statement naming the disease places it alongside the hemochromatoses as a chelation target: "the formulation of alternatives to phlebotomy therapy for patients with iron overload associated with the HFE gene and other adult age-of-onset types of hemochromatosis, African iron overload, and African-American iron overload" (PMID: 17335681).

No omics data. No transcriptomic, proteomic, metabolomic, lipidomic or epigenomic dataset from African iron overload patients exists in GEO, ArrayExpress, PRIDE, ProteomeXchange, MetaboLights or Metabolomics Workbench. No single-cell, spatial transcriptomic or CRISPR screen has been applied. The proteomic and metabolic data available come from carbonyl-iron-fed mouse liver, which models the exposure rather than the human disease.

The disease is preventable, and the natural experiment already happened

Hepatic iron concentrations in urban South African necropsy specimens fell 40% over sixteen years as traditional beer consumption declined with urbanisation: "A 40% reduction was found in hepatic iron concentrations measured in necropsy specimens from 248 male patients who died in 1976 as compared with 220 who died in 1959 and 1960" (PMID: 443190). The intervention is to replace the fermentation vessel, which the hepatocellular carcinoma prevention literature states directly: "Iron overload in hereditary hemochromatosis can be prevented by repeated venesection and in African dietary iron overload by fermenting the home-brewed beer in iron-free containers" (PMID: 20526004). The 29% population attributable risk gives the intervention a quantified target.

A counterweight constrains how this should be done. The same beverage prevents iron deficiency anaemia in women of childbearing age: 47% of non-drinkers against 14% of drinkers were iron deficient (p = 0.002), with iron deficiency anaemia absent among drinkers (PMID: 10509769). Removing the iron without providing an alternative source would increase anaemia in young women. The correct intervention targets the vessel while pairing it with iron supplementation or fortification for women of reproductive age.

Mechanistic model

Ordered causal chain

  1. Fermentation of traditional beer in non-galvanised iron or steel drums at low pH leaches ionic Fe(II)/Fe(III) into the beverage, producing a mean iron concentration of 46 ± 10 mg/L [human, measured, PMID: 9058740].
  2. Habitual consumption of 1-8 L/day delivers 46-370 mg of soluble non-heme iron per day into the duodenal lumen, against an obligate adult male loss of 1 mg/day [derived from the measured concentration].
  3. Luminal ionic iron is taken up by duodenal enterocytes through DMT1 (SLC11A2) at the brush border and is exported to plasma through basolateral ferroportin, bypassing the regulated heme iron route [mechanism established in iron biology, human and model organism].
  4. Inferred step. In the ~20% of exposed individuals who develop iron overload, the hepcidin-ferroportin axis fails to downregulate absorption sufficiently, allowing a fractional absorption roughly 1.5-fold higher than in unaffected drinkers (4.51% against 2.99% at 2 L/day) [inferred from the balance model in this work combined with the 20.7% prevalence of PMID: 2872439; the responsible locus is unidentified].
  5. Sustained net retention of 3-17 mg Fe/day produces a cumulative burden of 3.00 g/year at 2 L/day and 10% absorption, which crosses the 400 µmol/g dry weight hepatic fibrosis threshold within 10-25 years [derived; threshold from PMID: 3943787].
  6. Retained iron loads both hepatic parenchymal cells and reticuloendothelial macrophages, unlike HFE hemochromatosis which loads parenchyma predominantly [human pathology, PMID: 10881373].
  7. Transferrin saturation exceeding 60% generates non-transferrin-bound iron in plasma, which is taken up by hepatocytes through ZIP14 and expands the cytosolic labile iron pool [saturation >60% in 93% of cases, PMID: 10554633; the NTBI uptake route is inferred from iron biology].
  8. Labile iron drives Fenton chemistry, generating reactive oxygen intermediates that cause lipid peroxidation, producing malondialdehyde and 4-hydroxy-2'-nonenal and hydroxylating deoxyguanosine to 8-hydroxy-2'-deoxyguanosine [mechanistic review, PMID: 19081672].
  9. Iron accumulates in lysosomes, causing lysosomal fragility and releasing cathepsin B into the cytoplasm, which leads to impaired protein degradation and hepatocyte apoptosis: "The liver injury was accompanied by a marked lysosomal iron overload and lysosomal fragility with release of cathepsin B into the cytoplasm" [model organism, hepcidin-knockout mouse on iron-rich diet, PMID: 24816174].

The chain then branches three ways.

Branch A, fibrotic. Hepatocyte death and macrophage M1 polarisation activate hepatic stellate cells, which deposit collagen periportally, producing portal fibrosis and then micronodular cirrhosis: "Dietary iron overload of C57Bl6 mice led to hepatic macrophage M1 activation. Iron overload also stimulated hepatic fibrogenesis" (PMID: 30835899).

Branch B, neoplastic. Oxidative DNA damage causes G:C to T:A transversions and strand breaks, which lead to hepatocellular carcinoma. This branch does not require cirrhosis. In Wistar rats fed iron for 32 months, "Altered hepatic foci and pre-neoplastic nodules were first seen at 16 months. These increased in size and number with time, were iron-free, stained positively with placental glutathione sulphydryl transferase", and 5 of 8 surviving iron-loaded rats had preneoplastic nodules or carcinoma without fibrosis or cirrhosis (PMID: 16278820).

Branch C, infectious. Macrophage iron supplies Mycobacterium tuberculosis within the phagosome, increasing the risk and severity of tuberculosis (splenic iron grade OR 16.9, PMID: 8605366).

A weaker fourth branch runs from bony iron deposition to osteopenia, supported in cattle (PMID: 11467471), alongside an association between hyperferritinemia and diabetes (adjusted OR 2.72, 95% CI 1.24-5.98, p = 0.01) where the Q248H genotype itself shows no effect (adjusted OR 1.70, 95% CI 0.52-5.58, p = 0.37) (PMID: 23680252).

This architecture resolves three standing puzzles at once. Why no gene was found: the required effect size is 1.5-fold, below the detection threshold of the family and candidate-gene studies that were applied. Why Q248H keeps appearing and keeps failing replication: it is a conditional modifier whose effect is visible only when hepcidin is elevated, so unconditioned studies average it away. Why the disease is male-predominant and late-onset: time to threshold scales inversely with daily volume, women drink less and lose iron menstrually, and matched-exposure prevalence is nearly equal between sexes.

Inheritance is multifactorial. Penetrance is exposure-dependent and incomplete (20.7% in heavily exposed men over 45, effectively zero in unexposed carriers). The reference standard is liver biopsy with Perls Prussian blue staining and quantitative hepatic iron concentration, grading hepatocellular iron 0 to 4+ and assessing Kupffer cell iron separately. Mixed hepatocyte-plus-macrophage distribution is the defining pathology. The hepatic iron index (µmol/g dry weight divided by age) separates homozygotes from heterozygotes, alcoholics and controls, and in the absence of alcoholism "fibrosis or cirrhosis in hemochromatosis was present only with hepatic iron concentrations above a threshold of approximately 400 mumoles per gm (22.3 mg per gm) dry weight" (PMID: 3943787). MRI R2 or T2 relaxometry is the modern non-invasive substitute, unvalidated in this specific population.

Genetic testing has no diagnostic role. No causal gene is known. Q248H genotyping neither confirms nor excludes the diagnosis, since roughly one in ten people of African ancestry carries it and ClinVar classifies it Benign. HFE C282Y/H63D testing is useful only to exclude the European disease. No functional test, electrophysiological study or omics assay has any established role.

The extrapolated algorithm: confirm overload with ferritin, transferrin saturation and CRP; stage the liver by biopsy iron quantitation or MRI R2*; stop the exposure; remove iron by phlebotomy to a ferritin target of 50-100 µg/L, with chelation if phlebotomy is not tolerated; screen for and treat complications (six-monthly HCC surveillance in cirrhosis, active tuberculosis case-finding, diabetes screening); treat concurrent hepatitis B, which carries a larger HCC odds ratio (adjusted OR 33.2, 95% CI 7.2-153.4) than the iron does. No pharmacogenomic marker is established and no gene, cell or RNA therapy exists or is in development. Hepcidin mimetics and cannabinoid receptor 2 agonism (PMID: 42572051) are mechanistically relevant and preclinical only.

Primary prevention is replacement of the fermentation vessel, which eliminates the environmental arm entirely. Genetic screening, carrier screening, newborn screening, prenatal and preimplantation testing are all unindicated, since no causal gene exists to screen for and avoiding the exposure prevents the disease regardless of genotype. Risk stratification uses exposure: a man over 45 with a history of daily traditional beer consumption carries roughly a one in five chance of iron overload and warrants measurement.

Other species and model organisms

The closest natural animal disease is hemochromatosis of Salers and Salers-cross cattle (Bos taurus, NCBI Taxon 9913), described in five clinical and one preclinical case (PMID: 11467471). Onset is 9 to 22 months. "Hepatic iron concentrations in clinically affected cattle were 1,500-10,500 microg/g wet weight (reference range for cattle = <300 microg/ g)." "The principal histologic changes were hepatocellular siderosis and periportal, bridging, and perivenular fibrosis." "Iron deposition in bone was associated with osteopenia." "Genetic analysis indicated a common ancestral bull in the pedigrees of five of six affected cattle", with four of five dams phenotypically normal, consistent with recessive inheritance.

The bovine disease models the wrong half of the human condition: it is heritable, early-onset and recessive on an ordinary diet, making it a model of hereditary hemochromatosis. Two features still translate. The osteopenia associated with bony iron deposition matches the osteoporosis reported in African iron overload (PMID: 10881373) and supports a direct iron-to-bone route rather than one through hypogonadism, which fits the sparing of the pituitary-gonadal axis. The siderocalcinosis of connective tissue matrix is a distribution rarely examined in human cases.

Three papers in this table challenge rather than support the mechanistic model. PMID: 25378535 reports higher IL-6 and CRP in Q248H carriers with sickle cell disease, the opposite direction from PMID: 20460119; the two settings differ in baseline hemolysis and iron compartmentalisation, and the sickle study had 14 patients per arm. PMID: 19787796 found no Q248H frequency difference by iron status in black HEIRS participants, which is consistent with the modifier interpretation but inconsistent with any causal reading. PMID: 16440176 and PMID: 23065513 attribute different molecular defects to the same allele (reduced transport against hepcidin resistance); the reconciliation offered in this report, a partial loss-of-transport allele that escapes hepcidin-driven internalisation, fits the clinical data but has not been tested directly.

Limitations and knowledge gaps

The balance model rests on assumed parameters. Fractional absorption of ionic iron from fermented beer has never been measured directly in this population. The 35% hepatic share of retained iron is an assumption calibrated to the mixed macrophage-hepatocyte distribution rather than a measurement. The conclusion is robust to these assumptions, since the separating ratio held at 1.5-1.8-fold across hepatic shares of 15-60% and volumes of 1-8 L/day, but the absolute absorption percentages (4.51% and 2.99%) should be read as order-of-magnitude rather than as point estimates.

The prevalence denominator may be biased. The 23/111 figure comes from a single rural survey. "Exposed" is self-reported drinking, not measured cumulative dose. If heavy drinkers were preferentially sampled, or if the drinking distribution within the exposed group is wide, the implied gate could be narrower or wider than 1.5-fold. The model treats all exposed men as having the same daily volume, which they certainly do not.

The pooled odds ratio aggregates heterogeneous designs. I² was 77% for allele frequency across the 11 cohorts. The two contributing case-control studies differed in ascertainment, one African-American and one native African. The conditional CRP analysis was performed in preschool children rather than in exposed adults, so the inflammation dependence is established in a different population from the one in which the disease occurs. Whether the same interaction holds in a 50-year-old heavy drinker is untested.

The major locus remains unidentified. No GWAS has ever been run in this disease. The segregation analyses inferred a single major locus but never tested that model against a polygenic alternative, which the 1.5-fold effect size makes entirely plausible. The "iron-loading gene" of the family literature may be a polygenic score rather than a single variant.

No survival, quality-of-life, incidence, trial or omics data exist. These are absences in the published record, not omissions from this report, and are described in the key findings above.

Some identifiers in this report are unverified. CHEBI identifiers, NCIT concepts, per-species NCBI Gene identifiers for SLC40A1 orthologues and the VBO Salers breed identifier were written from domain knowledge and not confirmed by query; they are flagged where they appear. The CL, UBERON and GO identifiers were verified live against EBI OLS4 and can be used as given.

No individual-level data were available. Every analysis in this investigation operated on summary statistics extracted from publications plus direct queries to gnomAD, ClinVar and OLS4. The meta-analysis, the Wilson intervals and the balance model are all reconstructions from published aggregates. No patient registry, biobank or EHR cohort exists for this disease.

Proposed follow-up experiments

Measure fractional iron absorption directly. A stable isotope study (⁵⁷Fe or ⁵⁸Fe) in 40-60 heavy traditional beer drinkers, stratified by iron status and SLC40A1 genotype, using beer as the vehicle. This is the single measurement that converts the balance model from inference to observation, and it tests the 1.5-fold prediction head-on. Expected result if the model is right: affected and unaffected drinkers differ by roughly 50% in fractional absorption, with absolute values in the 2-6% range. Feasible in Zimbabwe or South Kivu with existing mass spectrometry collaborations.

Run the first GWAS in a well-phenotyped exposed cohort. Nobody has done this. Effect sizes of 1.5-fold require several thousand exposed individuals with quantified iron status, but the exposure stratification confers a design advantage: restricting to heavy drinkers makes the comparison affected against unaffected within a near-constant environment, which is the highest-powered design available for a gene-environment trait. Include a polygenic score analysis, since the single-major-locus model has never been tested against a polygenic alternative.

Build the Q248H knock-in mouse. No model carries the human susceptibility allele. A Slc40a1 Q248H knock-in fed a carbonyl iron diet would test whether a partial hepcidin-resistance allele of this magnitude produces the mixed hepatocyte-macrophage distribution, the fibrosis timeline and the conditional inflammatory phenotype. Add an LPS or turpentine inflammatory challenge arm to test the CRP interaction mechanistically.

Test the conditional effect in exposed adults. Repeat the genotype × CRP analysis in adult traditional beer drinkers with hepcidin, ferritin, CRP, transferrin saturation and soluble transferrin receptor measured together. The prediction is that Q248H carriers diverge from wild-type only in the high-hepcidin stratum. This moves the key reconciling observation from preschool children into the population where the disease actually occurs.

Run the first treatment study. A single-arm phlebotomy study with hepatic iron by MRI R2* at baseline and 12 months in 30-50 patients with ferritin above 1,000 µg/L and documented exposure. No therapy has ever been tested in this disease. A single-arm design is defensible given the complete absence of prior data and the established efficacy of phlebotomy in HFE disease. Co-primary endpoints: hepatic iron reduction and tolerability in a population with endemic tuberculosis and hepatitis B.

Generate the first omics dataset. Bulk and single-nucleus RNA-seq on liver biopsies from iron-loaded and matched control subjects, to establish which cell populations carry the transcriptional signature and whether the stellate activation and macrophage M1 programmes observed in mice are present in human tissue. This fills the largest single gap in the entry and would be the first molecular profiling of any kind in this disease.

Evaluate a vessel-replacement intervention. A cluster-randomised or stepped-wedge trial of subsidised non-iron fermentation vessels, with serum ferritin and transferrin saturation as outcomes at 3-5 years, paired with iron supplementation for women of reproductive age to avoid increasing anaemia. The 29% population attributable risk for hepatocellular carcinoma gives the trial a quantified public health target, and the 1959-1976 secular decline shows the effect size is achievable.
