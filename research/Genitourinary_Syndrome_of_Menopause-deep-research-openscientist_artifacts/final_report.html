Genitourinary Syndrome of Menopause (GSM): A Comprehensive Disease Characteristics Report

Disease: Genitourinary Syndrome of Menopause (GSM) MONDO ID: MONDO:0001410 (postmenopausal atrophic vaginitis — the nearest MONDO class, narrower than GSM, which also covers the urinary tract) OMIM: None Causal gene: None — this is an acquired endocrine condition, not a Mendelian disease Category: Acquired endocrine / urogenital condition of the menopausal transition

Summary

Genitourinary Syndrome of Menopause (GSM) is an acquired, chronic, progressive hypoestrogenic condition of the lower urogenital tract. It has no causal gene, no OMIM entry, and no pathogenic variant. The proximate cause is the menopausal withdrawal of ovarian 17β-estradiol — and, to a lesser but real degree, of androgens and the adrenal precursor DHEA — acting on the estrogen receptor α (ESR1/ERα) and androgen receptor (AR) in the labia, clitoris, vestibule, vagina, urethra, and bladder trigone. ESR1 is the mediating (not mutated) molecule: the receptor and its ligand are present in normal physiology, and disease arises from ligand deprivation, not from a genetic lesion. GSM replaced the older term "vulvovaginal atrophy" in a 2014 ISSWSH/NAMS consensus because it more accurately captures the genital, sexual, and urinary symptom domains (PMID: 25155380; PMID: 25153131).

The central causal chain is well supported: falling estradiol reduces ESR1 signalling in the urogenital epithelium → thinning of the stratified squamous epithelium with loss of superficial/intermediate cells and depletion of epithelial glycogen → fewer lactobacilli (which ferment glycogen-derived substrates to lactic acid) → a rise in vaginal pH above ~5 → dysbiosis, impaired mucosal barrier and colonization resistance → genital symptoms (dryness, burning, dyspareunia), sexual dysfunction, and urinary symptoms including recurrent urinary tract infection (UTI). This chain is quantified at multiple steps and is confirmed genetically by Esr1-knockout and epithelial-ESR1 conditional-knockout mouse models, which reproduce vaginal atrophy and loss of glycogen/microbiome/pH control.

GSM is highly prevalent (affecting roughly half of postmenopausal women; 10–47% develop debilitating symptoms), does not spontaneously remit, and imposes substantial quality-of-life and sexual-health morbidity while causing no direct mortality. It is highly treatment-responsive: low-dose vaginal estrogen is first-line and is safe — including, after shared decision-making, in most breast and gynecologic cancer survivors — with intravaginal prasterone (DHEA), the oral SERM ospemifene, and non-hormonal moisturizers/lubricants as alternatives. Because the underlying hormone deficit persists, symptoms recur when treatment stops, making GSM effectively a chronic condition requiring ongoing therapy. This report synthesizes 13 confirmed findings across 64 reviewed papers into a complete disease-characteristics dossier spanning all 15 requested sections.

Key Findings

1. Disease Information

GSM is defined by the 2014 ISSWSH/NAMS consensus as "a collection of symptoms and signs associated with a decrease in estrogen and other sex steroids involving changes to the labia majora/minora, clitoris, vestibule/introitus, vagina, urethra and bladder" (PMID: 25155380). The syndrome includes genital symptoms (dryness, burning, irritation), sexual symptoms (lack of lubrication, discomfort/pain, impaired function), and urinary symptoms (urgency, dysuria, recurrent UTI). Symptoms must be bothersome and not better accounted for by another diagnosis.

  • Identifiers: MONDO:0001410 (postmenopausal atrophic vaginitis — narrower than GSM); MeSH: "Atrophic Vaginitis" / related menopause terms; ICD-10 N95.2 (postmenopausal atrophic vaginitis); ICD-11 GA30.0; no OMIM, no Orphanet rare-disease number (GSM is common, not rare).
  • Synonyms: GSM; vulvovaginal atrophy (VVA); atrophic vaginitis; postmenopausal atrophic vaginitis; urogenital atrophy.
  • Information source: Aggregated disease-level resources (consensus guidelines, cohort studies, RCTs) rather than individual EHR records.

This is an acquired endocrine condition of hypoestrogenism, not a Mendelian disease — there is no causal gene. The proximate cause is postmenopausal withdrawal of ovarian 17β-estradiol acting on ESR1 in urogenital tissue (Finding F001).

2. Etiology and Risk Factors

Primary cause: estrogen (and other sex-steroid) withdrawal. There are no genetic causal factors, no susceptibility loci, and no modifier genes established for GSM — it is a physiological consequence of menopause.

Risk factors / high-risk settings (Finding F007): - Natural menopause — the universal driver. - Iatrogenic / induced hypoestrogenism: aromatase inhibitors (AIs) profoundly lower estrogen and worsen GSM and dyspareunia (PMID: 42530772); risk-reducing salpingo-oophorectomy (RRSO) causes immediate surgical menopause (PMID: 42998015); cancer treatment–induced menopause (chemotherapy, radiotherapy, GnRH agonists) (PMID: 42747249). - Premature ovarian insufficiency (POI) and early menopause — worldwide prevalence ~3.7% and ~12% respectively; frequently cause GSM (PMID: 42809454). - Breast cancer survivors on endocrine therapy — "menopause … is abrupt, severe, and treatment-induced" (PMID: 42907052). - Other hypoestrogenic states: lactation, hormonal contraception, gender-affirming (testosterone) therapy, and certain medications (PMID: 42594495). - Lifestyle: smoking is a recognized contributor to vaginal atrophy (PMID: 39772973).

Protective factors: systemic or local estrogen exposure. Sexual activity and non-hormonal moisturizers help maintain tissue health. No protective genetic alleles are described (not applicable to a non-genetic condition).

In a cross-sectional cohort of peri/postmenopausal Chinese-speaking sex workers, GSM prevalence was 83.1% (49/59), with 59.2% reporting moderate-to-severe symptoms; the most prevalent symptoms were loss of libido (79.3%), loss of lubrication (78.2%), and vaginal dryness (77.2%); higher severity was significantly associated with lower job satisfaction (p=0.002) and reduced ability to work (p=0.009) (PMID: 42314595) (Finding F005). The classic estimate is that "10–47% of postmenopausal women will develop one or more debilitating symptoms" (PMID: 20868405).

Onset: adult/late-onset (perimenopausal/postmenopausal, typically >45–50 y, or earlier with iatrogenic/POI causes). Severity: variable (mild to severe). Progression: chronic and progressive without treatment, non-remitting. QoL impact: substantial — sexual dysfunction, impaired daily function, work impairment, and emotional distress.

4. Genetic / Molecular Information

There is no causal gene, no pathogenic variant, no inheritance, no penetrance, and no genetic testing applicable to GSM. This section is addressed by identifying the mediating receptor rather than any mutation.

ESR1/ERα (HGNC:3467; estrogen receptor 1) is the key effector (Finding F008). Although no mutation is involved, loss of ESR1 signalling (through ligand withdrawal) is sufficient to produce the core lesion: - In Esr1-knockout mice, "vaginal epithelial proliferation was reduced, and the epithelium became atrophic in Esr1KO mice by 29 days of age" (PMID: 25653281). - Conditional epithelial ESR1 deletion abolishes estrogen-induced epithelial proliferation, cornification, MUC1 expression, glycogen deposition, microbial stability, and pH regulation (PMID: 41279230).

The androgen receptor (AR) and local intracrine androgen synthesis also contribute (Finding F006): human vaginal smooth muscle cells express steroidogenic enzymes (HSD3β1/β2, HSD17β3/β5, 5α-reductase, sulfotransferase) more abundantly than ovary and can synthesize androgens locally from DHEA — "short-term DHEA supplementation increased Δ4-androstenedione levels … while increasing testosterone and DHT secretion after longer incubation" (PMID: 33247714).

Modifier genes, epigenetic disease mechanisms, and chromosomal abnormalities: not applicable / none established.

5. Environmental Information

  • Environmental / iatrogenic factors: aromatase inhibitors, chemotherapy, radiotherapy, GnRH agonists, oophorectomy — all reduce estrogen and precipitate or worsen GSM (Section 2; PMID: 42747249).
  • Lifestyle factors: smoking (pro-atrophic), hormonal contraceptives, SSRIs, and other medications can contribute to vaginal dryness (PMID: 39772973; PMID: 42871806).
  • Infectious agents: GSM is not caused by a pathogen. However, hypoestrogenic dysbiosis predisposes to secondary infection — recurrent UTI (commonly Escherichia coli) and altered vaginal flora. The hypoestrogenism–recurrent-UTI association is better supported than associations with bacterial vaginosis or candidiasis (PMID: 42959465).

6. Mechanism / Pathophysiology

Ordered causal chain (initiating lesion → clinical manifestation):

  1. Menopause (ovarian follicular depletion) or iatrogenic ovarian suppression leads to a fall in circulating 17β-estradiol (and, with adrenopause, declining DHEA/androgens).
  2. Reduced estradiol results in diminished ligand occupancy of ESR1/ERα in urogenital epithelial and stromal cells (inferred from receptor biology; demonstrated genetically by Esr1-KO atrophy).
  3. Reduced ESR1 signalling leads to decreased epithelial proliferation and cornification → thinning of the stratified squamous epithelium (loss of superficial/intermediate layers, parabasal predominance), reduced collagen/elastin, reduced vascularity, loss of rugae and elasticity.
  4. Epithelial thinning results in depletion of intracellular glycogen (postmenopausal free glycogen median 0.002 vs premenopausal 0.065 µg/µL, P=0.03).
  5. Glycogen depletion leads to loss of lactobacilli (which depend on glycogen-derived substrates; glycogen correlates with Lactobacillus, premenopausal r=0.68, P<0.0001).
  6. Loss of lactobacilli results in reduced lactic-acid production and a rise in vaginal pH (median 4.6 vs 4.0 premenopausal).
  7. Higher pH + thin epithelium leads to dysbiosis, impaired mucosal barrier, and reduced colonization resistance.
  8. Branch A — Genital/sexual manifestations: reduced lubrication and fragile epithelium result in dryness, burning, dyspareunia, and sexual dysfunction.
  9. Branch B — Urinary manifestations: the same hypoestrogenism thins the urethral/trigonal epithelium and periurethral tissue and reduces colonization resistance, leading to urgency, dysuria, and recurrent UTI (often E. coli).

Molecular / cellular detail. ESR1-driven transcription controls epithelial proliferation, cornification, MUC1 expression, and glycogen deposition (PMID: 41279230). Hormone exposure reprograms barrier and immune gene expression: in reconstructed vaginal epithelium, testosterone yields thinner tissue with altered cornification and changes in tight-junction and chemokine gene expression versus estradiol (PMID: 42121849). Downstream, "hypoestrogenism can weaken vaginal, urethral, and bladder epithelial resilience, reduce Lactobacillus-associated colonization resistance, and impair tissue repair" (PMID: 42959465) (Finding F011).

Upstream vs downstream: the hormonal deficit and ESR1 hypofunction are upstream; glycogen loss, dysbiosis, pH rise, and barrier failure are intermediate; infection, inflammation, and symptomatic dryness/dyspareunia/urinary complaints are downstream.

7. Anatomical Structures Affected

GSM localizes to the estrogen/androgen-responsive lower urogenital tract: "the labia majora/minora, clitoris, vestibule/introitus, vagina, urethra and bladder" (PMID: 25155380) (Finding F009). These structures share a common embryologic origin (urogenital sinus) and ER expression, explaining their co-involvement.

8. Temporal Development

  • Onset: adult/late-onset, coinciding with the menopausal transition (perimenopause through postmenopause); earlier with iatrogenic menopause or POI. Onset is insidious and chronic.
  • Progression: GSM is a "chronic, progressive hypoestrogenic condition" (PMID: 41892504) (Finding F005). Unlike vasomotor symptoms, which often improve over time, GSM tends to persist and worsen because the estrogen deficit is permanent.
  • Course: progressive, non-remitting without treatment; does not spontaneously remit.
  • Remission: treatment-induced only; symptoms recur if therapy stops (Finding F012).
  • Critical window: early initiation of vaginal estrogen yields stronger cytologic/pH responses; older women (≥60 y) show reduced cytologic responsiveness, though treatment improves symptoms at any age (PMID: 33038141).

9. Inheritance and Population

  • Epidemiology: highly prevalent. ~50% of postmenopausal women are affected overall; 10–47% develop debilitating symptoms (PMID: 20868405); up to 83.1% in some cohorts (PMID: 42314595); even higher among breast cancer survivors on endocrine therapy (PMID: 42461278).
  • Inheritance: not applicable — no inheritance pattern, no penetrance, no expressivity, no anticipation, no founder effects, no carrier frequency, no consanguinity role. This is an acquired condition.
  • Sex: affects individuals with estrogen-responsive female urogenital anatomy (natal females; also relevant to transgender men on testosterone and others in hypoestrogenic states). Sex ratio is not a meaningful metric.
  • Age distribution: concentrated in peri/postmenopausal women (typically ≥45–50 y), with iatrogenic/POI cases occurring earlier.
  • Geographic distribution: worldwide, wherever women reach menopause; no endemic pattern. Underdiagnosis and undertreatment are common globally.

10. Diagnostics

Diagnosis is clinical (Finding F004), based on bothersome symptoms plus objective signs on examination: "Diagnosis should rely on both patient-reported bothersome symptoms and objective clinical signs identified during physical examination. Assessment tools such as the Vaginal Health Index and Vestibular Trophic Health Score may assist in evaluating disease severity" (PMID: 42881642).

Objective tools: - Vaginal pH — rises above ~5 with atrophy. - Vaginal Maturation Index (VMI) — cytologic shift from superficial toward parabasal cells; estrogen therapy shifts VMI toward superficial cells (+31.3 superficial cells in one arm) (PMID: 38412393). - Vaginal Health Index (VHI) and Vestibular/Vaginal Trophic Health Score — estrogen raises VHI (~+7 points) while non-hormonal moisturizer does not (PMID: 38412393). - Vaginal cytology / wet mount — parabasal cells, sometimes with leukocytes (paradoxical inflammatory picture).

No genetic testing, karyotyping, FISH, CMA, mtDNA, or repeat-expansion testing is applicable. Omics diagnostics are research-only (microbiome 16S/metagenomics shows compartment-specific Lactobacillus loss; PMID: 42858690).

Differential diagnosis is important, particularly for dyspareunia: "intercourse-related pain may also reflect vestibular, pelvic floor, dermatologic, inflammatory, treatment-related, and psychosocial factors" (PMID: 42454524) (Finding F012). GSM must be distinguished from vulvar dermatoses (lichen sclerosus/planus), vestibulodynia, pelvic floor dysfunction, and infection.

11. Outcome / Prognosis

GSM is non-fatal — not associated with mortality — but causes substantial chronic morbidity: sexual dysfunction, recurrent UTI, and impaired quality of life and work ability (PMID: 42314595) (Finding F012). It is highly treatment-responsive: low-dose vaginal estrogen, prasterone, and ospemifene reliably improve symptoms and objective signs (PMID: 42893492; PMID: 41892504). However, symptoms recur if treatment stops because the underlying estrogen deficiency persists, so ongoing therapy is required. There is no "cure" short of restoring hormonal signalling. Complications include recurrent UTI and sexual dysfunction. Prognostic factors for cytologic response include younger age at treatment initiation (PMID: 33038141).

12. Treatment

First-line: low-dose vaginal estrogen (Finding F003). "Low-dose vaginal estrogen significantly improves GSM symptoms — including vaginal dryness, dyspareunia, and irritation — while restoring epithelial integrity and normalizing pH" (PMID: 42893492), with similar efficacy across creams, tablets, inserts, and rings, and no increased risk of CVD, VTE, or malignancy in large cohorts. In a class-level network meta-analysis of 24 trial families (4,958 participants), local vaginal estrogen was the only class with a confidence interval excluding the null (SMD 0.34, 95% CI 0.02–0.66) (PMID: 43000041).

Pharmacogenomics: not applicable. Gene/cell/RNA therapies: not applicable to routine care (investigational regenerative/exosome approaches exist in animal models, e.g. PMID: 41275445).

Mechanistic rationale for ER agonism (Finding F008): ospemifene "is the only agent with a nearly full estrogen agonist effect on the vaginal epithelium while having neutral to slight estrogenic effects in the endometrium" (PMID: 25087944), confirming ERα agonism reverses atrophy.

Recurrent UTI prevention: vaginal estrogen reduces recurrent UTI by restoring a Lactobacillus-dominant microbiome; a 2025 multisociety guideline recommends topical (not systemic) estrogen for rUTI prevention (PMID: 42893492). "Vaginal estrogen, by restoring urogenital epithelium and the Lactobacillus-dominant microbiome, is recommended in several guidelines for preventing rUTIs" (PMID: 42378201).

Cancer survivors (Finding F007): "Large observational studies and meta-analyses show no increased risk of breast cancer recurrence or mortality among users, including those with estrogen receptor-positive disease and those on tamoxifen" (PMID: 42832731). The VEMORA RCT showed vaginal estrogen improved dryness/dyspareunia versus moisturizer with low serum estradiol in AI users (PMID: 42530772). Maximal caution and shared decision-making are warranted for women on aromatase inhibitors.

13. Prevention

  • Primary prevention: menopausal/vaginal estrogen can prevent urogenital atrophy in at-risk women; smoking cessation; sexual activity maintains tissue health.
  • Secondary prevention: proactive screening — clinicians should ask about genital/urinary symptoms, which are underreported due to stigma (PMID: 41892504).
  • Tertiary prevention: ongoing vaginal estrogen to prevent recurrent UTI and progression; topical estrogen reduces rUTI recurrence (PMID: 42378201).
  • Immunization / genetic screening / genetic counseling: not applicable.
  • Behavioral: moisturizers, lubricants, pelvic floor therapy, and continued sexual activity.

14. Other Species / Natural Disease

  • Taxonomy: modeled in Mus musculus (NCBI:txid10090) and Rattus norvegicus (NCBI:txid10116) via ovariectomy.
  • Orthologous gene: mouse Esr1 (NCBI Gene 13982) is orthologous to human ESR1.
  • Natural disease: GSM as such is a human clinical construct; analogous vaginal atrophy occurs in ovariectomized/aged companion animals, but no formal veterinary disease entity is catalogued. Not a zoonosis.
  • Comparative biology: the ovariectomized (OVX) rat is "a highly predictive model of SERM activity in postmenopausal VVA" (PMID: 23665515), indicating conserved estrogen-dependent vaginal epithelial biology.

15. Model Organisms

  • Ovariectomized (OVX) rat/mouse — the standard induced model; reproduces epithelial thinning, reduced glycogen, loss of lubrication, and responds to estrogen/SERMs (PMID: 23665515; PMID: 41275445).
  • Esr1-knockout mouse — genetic proof that ESR1 loss causes vaginal atrophy (PMID: 25653281).
  • Epithelial-ESR1 conditional-knockout mouse — dissects epithelial ESR1's role in glycogen/microbiome/pH control (PMID: 41279230).
  • In vitro reconstructed vaginal epithelium — models hormone-dependent barrier/immune gene expression (PMID: 42121849).
  • Phenotype recapitulation: OVX models reproduce the atrophy and treatment responsiveness well. Limitations: rodent vaginal microbiota differs from the human Lactobacillus-dominant community, so the glycogen→lactobacillus→pH axis is harder to model directly; the human intracrine androgen capacity of vaginal tissue is not fully captured.

Mechanistic Model / Interpretation

The model is internally consistent and bidirectional: the same intervention (restoring ERα ligand) that reverses each step confirms the causal direction. Treatment restores glycogen, serum estradiol/estrone, and Lactobacillus dominance, with symptom improvement tracking these changes — "Vaginal glycogen, serum estradiol, and estrone significantly increased in women whose [symptoms] improved" (PMID: 29206774). The androgen/intracrine arm (PMID: 33247714) explains why DHEA (prasterone), which is converted locally to both androgens and estrogens, is an effective therapy and why the 2014 consensus deliberately referenced "estrogen and other sex steroids."

A broader reframing (Finding F010) notes that identical pathophysiology arises from any hypoestrogenic/hypoandrogenic state — "perimenopause, lactation, hormonal contraception, cancer therapies, gender-affirming hormone therapy, and other medications" (PMID: 42594495) — supporting a proposed etiology-based "Genitourinary Syndrome" superclass with menopause as one subtype.

Limitations and Knowledge Gaps

  • Not a genetic disease: Sections on causal genes, inheritance, penetrance, variants, genetic testing, chromosomal abnormalities, and genetic counseling are not applicable. The report addresses ESR1/AR as mediating molecules, not disease-causing mutations.
  • Prevalence heterogeneity: reported prevalence ranges widely (10–47% symptomatic to 83% in selected cohorts) because of differing definitions, populations, and underreporting due to stigma. There is no single authoritative incidence/prevalence figure per 100,000.
  • Microbiome model gaps: the glycogen→lactobacillus→pH mechanism is quantified in human observational data and partly in mice, but rodent vaginal flora differs from humans, limiting mechanistic modeling of the dysbiosis arm.
  • Energy-based therapies: CO2 laser and radiofrequency show benefit in some trials but recent sham-controlled RCTs were negative (e.g. PMID: 42879157), so evidence is weak/limited.
  • Long-term cancer-survivor safety: reassuring but based largely on observational data; longer RCTs are needed, especially for aromatase-inhibitor users.
  • Androgen contribution: the quantitative weight of the androgen/intracrine arm relative to estrogen remains incompletely defined.

Proposed Follow-up Experiments / Actions

  1. Standardize epidemiology: conduct population-based, consensus-definition prevalence/incidence studies to produce comparable per-100,000 estimates across ages and ethnicities.
  2. Longitudinal microbiome mechanism: multi-compartment (vaginal, periurethral, urinary) 16S/metagenomic cohorts with paired glycogen, pH, and estradiol to formally test the causal chain and its restoration under therapy (building on PMID: 42858690).
  3. Long-term cancer-survivor RCTs: adequately powered trials of low-dose vaginal estrogen and prasterone in AI users with recurrence/mortality endpoints to resolve residual safety uncertainty.
  4. Define the androgen arm: dose-ranging intravaginal DHEA/androgen studies with tissue intracrine readouts to quantify androgen vs estrogen contributions.
  5. Energy-device evidence: high-quality sham-controlled trials before routine CO2-laser/RF adoption, per existing negative results.
  6. Terminology adoption: evaluate the proposed etiology-based "Genitourinary Syndrome" framework (PMID: 42594495) to extend evidence-based care to non-menopausal hypoestrogenic populations.
  7. Implementation science: address underdiagnosis/undertreatment via structured menopause history-taking and clinician decision support, given documented prescribing gaps.
