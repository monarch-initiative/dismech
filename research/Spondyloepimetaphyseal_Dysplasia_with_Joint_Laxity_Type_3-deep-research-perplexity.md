## 1. Disease Information

### Overview and nosology

## 2. Etiology

### Genetic causal factors

The primary causal factor for SEMDJL3 is biallelic loss‑of‑function mutation in the EXOC6B gene, located on chromosome 2p13.2.[1][12][14][15][16] OMIM notes that the disorder is caused by homozygous EXOC6B mutations in reported families, including a nonsense variant p.Tyr302Ter identified by whole‑exome sequencing in two brothers with multiple joint dislocations and spondyloepimetaphyseal dysplasia.[1] PanelApp, summarizing Girisha et al. (PMID:26669664), likewise reports that these brothers had a homozygous EXOC6B nonsense variant (Y302X), with heterozygous parents who were unaffected, consistent with autosomal recessive inheritance.[12] Campos‑Xavier et al. (PMID:36150098) described two unrelated individuals with SEMDJL3, one harboring a homozygous frameshift exon 20 deletion and the other a homozygous nonsense variant p.Leu134Ter, further supporting EXOC6B as the disease gene.[12][16] These variants are predicted to truncate the EXOC6B protein, disrupt exocyst complex assembly, and lead to loss of function.

EXOC6B encodes “exocyst complex component 6B,” a protein that participates in the evolutionarily conserved exocyst complex required for exocytosis and targeted vesicle docking at the plasma membrane.[14][15] GeneCards describes EXOC6B as a component of the exocyst complex involved in docking exocytic vesicles to fusion sites on the plasma membrane, with annotations in pathways such as “Cytoskeleton remodeling_RalA regulation pathway” and “Ciliary landscape.”[15] The exocyst complex is essential for exocytosis, which is crucial for cell growth, polarity, and migration, and EXOC6B disruption has been associated with phenotypes exhibiting multiple symptoms including intellectual disability and developmental delay in some contexts.[15] In SEMDJL3, however, intelligence appears to be usually normal, suggesting tissue‑specific vulnerability, particularly in skeletal and connective tissues.[2][17] KEGG’s SEMD‑JL disease entry explicitly lists EXOC6B as the causal gene for type 3 and emphasizes its role in the exocyst and ciliary machinery.[16]

Given the biallelic truncating variants, the functional consequence is best interpreted as a germline, loss‑of‑function lesion in EXOC6B, leading to a partial or complete absence of functional protein.[1][12][16] All reported variants are germline, and there is no evidence that somatic mutations in EXOC6B contribute to this specific skeletal phenotype, though EXOC6B may have somatic roles in other contexts such as cancer or neurodevelopment which are beyond the current scope.[15][9] There are no reported cases of SEMDJL3 due to missense EXOC6B variants, splice site mutations, or structural rearrangements, but this may reflect the very small number of genotyped individuals rather than a true restriction of mutational mechanisms.[1][12][16] Overall, EXOC6B variants that abolish protein function in all tissues (loss‑of‑function germline variants) constitute the primary genetic cause of SEMDJL3.

### Risk factors

In this Mendelian context, “risk factors” can be divided into genetic and non‑genetic components. Genetically, the primary risk factor is inheriting two pathogenic EXOC6B alleles, one from each parent, in an autosomal recessive pattern.[1][2][5][8][12][16] OMIM and Orphanet both describe SEMDJL3 as autosomal recessive, with affected individuals arising from consanguineous or endogamous families in which parents are heterozygous carriers.[1][2] PanelApp notes that the two brothers reported by Girisha et al. were born to first‑cousin parents, consistent with consanguinity facilitating homozygosity for rare EXOC6B variants.[12] Thus, consanguinity and small, genetically isolated populations where a pathogenic EXOC6B allele has risen in frequency constitute important genetic risk factors at the population level.

Beyond the primary causal variants, there is currently no evidence for additional susceptibility loci or modifier genes that consistently influence SEMDJL3 risk or severity.[1][2][9][12][16] Malacards lists multiple genes associated with “spondyloepimetaphyseal dysplasia with joint laxity,” including B3GALT6, KIF22, B4GALT7, B3GAT3, XYLT1, XYLT2, CHST14, and others, but these genes cause distinct, though overlapping, skeletal disorders (e.g., SEMDJL1, SEMDJL2, and various Ehlers–Danlos‑like conditions) rather than modifying EXOC6B‑driven SEMDJL3.[9][16][6][11][13] For example, B3GALT6 mutations cause Beighton‑type SEMD‑JL characterized by severe kyphoscoliosis and early respiratory compromise, while KIF22 mutations cause leptodactylic SEMD‑JL with autosomal dominant inheritance.[16][6][11] These genes are best conceptualized as causal for related diseases, not as risk modifiers for SEMDJL3 itself.

Regarding non‑genetic risk factors, available clinical descriptions do not report specific environmental, nutritional, infectious, or lifestyle exposures that increase risk of SEMDJL3 in individuals who do not carry biallelic EXOC6B variants.[1][2][17] Because the disorder arises from germline mutations present from conception, it is not sensitive to typical acquired risk factors such as smoking, toxin exposure, or metabolic comorbidities in the sense of disease occurrence; however, such factors may modify severity of skeletal complications, as they do in many orthopedic conditions, though this has not been systematically studied in SEMDJL3.[2][17] Sex does not appear to be a major risk factor; the limited published cases include both male and female patients, and Orphanet does not note a sex bias.[1][2][12] Age is not a risk factor for onset, given the congenital nature of joint dislocations, but aging may influence progression and secondary degenerative changes.

### Protective factors and gene–environment interactions

No specific genetic protective factors—such as common variants that reduce penetrance or ameliorate phenotype in individuals with pathogenic EXOC6B mutations—have been reported.[1][2][12][16] Given the small number of known patients, the ability to detect modifier alleles is extremely limited. It is conceivable that variability in genes involved in exocyst assembly, ciliary structure, or skeletal growth plate signaling might modulate severity, but such hypotheses remain speculative and untested.[15][16] Similarly, epigenetic variation in EXOC6B expression or regulatory elements could hypothetically influence disease severity, but no data are currently available.[14][15]

Environmental protective factors have likewise not been specifically investigated in SEMDJL3. In theory, optimal nutrition, avoidance of high‑impact trauma, early physical therapy, and proactive orthopedic management might reduce functional impairment and secondary complications, but these interventions address disease course rather than occurrence and are more akin to tertiary prevention than true protective factors.[2][17] There is no evidence that exposures such as vitamin D supplementation, specific diets, or avoidance of particular toxins change the risk of developing SEMDJL3 in individuals who carry biallelic EXOC6B variants; the disease appears fully penetrant in described cases.[1][12][16]

Gene–environment interactions have not been systematically studied for SEMDJL3, and no specific interactions between EXOC6B variants and environmental exposures have been reported.[1][2][12][16][17] At present, SEMDJL3 is best considered a “pure” Mendelian disorder where the initiating lesion (germline EXOC6B loss‑of‑function) is both necessary and sufficient for disease, with environmental factors primarily affecting symptom management rather than disease onset. This absence of evidence should, however, be interpreted cautiously given the very small number of reported patients and the lack of longitudinal cohort data.

## 4. Genetic and Molecular Information

### Causal gene EXOC6B: structure and function

EXOC6B (HGNC:17085) encodes exocyst complex component 6B, one of several subunits in the octameric exocyst complex.[14][15] The gene is located on chromosome 2p13.2, as documented by LOVD and OMIM.[1][14] GeneCards lists multiple external identifiers, including NCBI Gene 23233, Ensembl ENSG00000144036, and UniProt Q9Y2D4 (EXOC6B_HUMAN).[15] EXOC6B is a protein‑coding gene with a close paralog EXOC6, reflecting duplication and divergence within the exocyst component family.[15] The exocyst is an evolutionarily conserved multiprotein complex involved in tethering secretory vesicles to specific sites on the plasma membrane prior to fusion, thereby orchestrating polarized exocytosis.[15] As GeneCards summarizes, “This gene encodes a protein which is a part of the evolutionarily conserved exocyst, a multimeric protein complex necessary for exocytosis, which in turn, is crucial for cell growth, polarity and migration.”[15] Exocyst function is particularly important in cells requiring highly polarized secretion or membrane remodeling, such as neurons, epithelial cells, and chondrocytes.

In terms of pathway annotation, GeneCards links EXOC6B to pathways such as “Cytoskeleton remodeling_RalA regulation pathway” and “Ciliary landscape,” reflecting its roles in vesicle trafficking and cilium biology.[15] KEGG maps EXOC6B to the KO entry K19985 and associates it with SEMDJL3 in the SEMD‑JL disease module.[16] PanelApp notes that functional studies on patient fibroblast cell lines revealed “abrogation of exocytosis leading to impaired primary ciliogenesis,” directly linking EXOC6B deficiency to cilium formation defects.[12] This positions EXOC6B within a broader class of exocyst–cilium interface proteins whose disruption can produce skeletal ciliopathies and related phenotypes.[12][15][16] Appropriate Gene Ontology (GO) terms include GO:0006887 (exocytosis), GO:0007032 (endosome organization), GO:0060271 (cilium assembly), and GO:0030833 (promotion of cell adhesion, via exocyst function).

### Pathogenic variants and their characteristics

Pathogenic variants in EXOC6B causing SEMDJL3 are primarily truncating, loss‑of‑function mutations, including nonsense variants and frameshift deletions.[1][12][16] OMIM describes a homozygous nonsense mutation Y302X (p.Tyr302Ter; 607880.0001) identified in two brothers with SEMD and multiple joint dislocations, whose unaffected first‑cousin parents were heterozygous carriers.[1] PanelApp refers to the same variant (p.Tyr302Ter) and notes its association with SEMDJL3.[12] Campos‑Xavier et al. (PMID:36150098), as summarized in PanelApp and KEGG, reported two unrelated individuals with SEMDJL3: one harboring a homozygous frameshift exon 20 deletion and the other a homozygous nonsense variant p.Leu134Ter.[12][16] All of these variants are predicted to introduce premature termination codons leading to nonsense‑mediated mRNA decay or truncated, nonfunctional protein.

These variants would be classified as “pathogenic” according to ACMG/AMP guidelines, given their truncating nature in a gene where loss of function is a known mechanism of disease, consistency with the phenotype, segregation in families, and absence (or extreme rarity) in general population databases such as gnomAD.[1][12][16] ClinVar and HGMD likely contain these variants, but specific entries are not provided in the current search results; OMIM and PanelApp serve as primary sources confirming pathogenicity.[1][12] The variant types include nonsense (single‑nucleotide substitutions creating stop codons) and frameshift (small deletions altering reading frame and leading to premature stops), both within coding exons of EXOC6B.[1][12][16] No missense, splice‑site, or structural variants have been definitively linked to SEMDJL3 yet, but given the gene’s size and structure, such variants are plausible and may be discovered as more patients are identified.[14][15]

Allele frequencies for EXOC6B loss‑of‑function variants are extremely low in population databases, consistent with the rarity of SEMDJL3.[1][2][8][12][16] Pathogenic variants in reported families are not found in homozygous state in general populations, and they are rarely observed even in heterozygous state, implying strong negative selection.[12][16] Somatic variants in EXOC6B have not been implicated in SEMDJL3, which is a germline disorder; all pathogenic variants are present in all cells from conception.[1][12][16] In terms of functional consequences, these truncating variants result in loss of EXOC6B protein activity, a classic “loss‑of‑function” mechanism rather than gain‑of‑function or dominant negative effects, consistent with autosomal recessive inheritance.[1][12][15][16]

### Variant origin, frequency, and modifier effects

All reported SEMDJL3 cases involve biallelic germline EXOC6B variants inherited from heterozygous, unaffected parents, consistent with autosomal recessive inheritance.[1][12][16] The contexts described include consanguineous parents (first‑cousins) and unrelated parents both carrying the same pathogenic variant, suggesting either founder effects or recurrent mutational events.[1][12][16] However, no formal founder mutation has been documented, and the geographic distribution of variants remains poorly characterized due to the small number of cases.[1][2][12][16]

Penetrance appears to be complete in individuals with homozygous truncating variants; all reported homozygotes exhibit the characteristic SEMDJL3 phenotype.[1][12][16][17] Expressivity may show some variability, particularly in severity of spinal deformity or limb malalignment, but core features of joint dislocation, joint laxity, and skeletal dysplasia are consistent across individuals.[1][2][12][16][17] No modifier genes or epistatic interactions have been identified that alter the expression of EXOC6B loss‑of‑function in SEMDJL3, although variability arising from general genetic background and environmental factors is likely.[1][2][9][12][16]

### Epigenetic and chromosomal considerations

There is currently no evidence that epigenetic mechanisms such as aberrant DNA methylation, histone modification, or chromatin remodeling at the EXOC6B locus play a primary role in SEMDJL3 pathogenesis.[1][2][14][15] The causal mechanism is clearly linked to coding sequence variants that truncate the protein.[1][12][16] Likewise, no large‑scale chromosomal abnormalities (aneuploidy, translocations, inversions, or copy number variants) affecting EXOC6B have been reported in association with SEMDJL3.[1][12][16] The EXOC6B locus appears structurally stable in known patients, with disease arising from point mutations or small indels.

That said, EXOC6B’s genomic environment and regulation via promoters, enhancers, and long‑range chromatin interactions likely contribute to normal expression patterns, and disruptions in these regulatory elements could, in theory, cause disease or modify severity without affecting coding sequence. Such possibilities have not yet been explored for SEMDJL3. Future studies employing whole‑genome sequencing and epigenomic profiling may uncover regulatory variants or epigenetic dysregulation in unsolved cases with SEMDJL3‑like phenotypes but no coding EXOC6B variants.

## 5. Environmental Information

For EXOC6B‑related SEMDJL3, environmental factors appear to play little or no role in disease occurrence, which is driven by germline, biallelic EXOC6B loss‑of‑function.[1][2][12][16][17] Unlike multifactorial or polygenic disorders, SEMDJL3 does not show epidemiologic associations with specific toxins, radiation, pollution, occupational exposures, infection, diet, or lifestyle factors.[1][2][17] The rarity of the disease, combined with its congenital onset, makes it unlikely that exogenous exposures during childhood or adulthood initiate the phenotype; instead, environmental factors may influence the severity of complications (e.g., trauma aggravating joint dislocations) or the success of rehabilitation.

Lifestyle factors such as physical activity, obesity, and smoking would be expected to affect orthopedic symptomatology in general, but their specific impact on SEMDJL3 has not been studied.[2][17] For example, high‑impact sports might exacerbate joint instability, whereas tailored low‑impact exercise programs could help maintain muscle strength and joint support, but these are management considerations rather than etiologic factors. Infectious agents are not implicated in SEMDJL3 pathogenesis, and there are no reports of infectious triggers precipitating disease onset in genetically susceptible individuals.[1][2][17] Overall, SEMDJL3 is best conceptualized as an environmentally insensitive Mendelian disease, with non‑genetic factors playing supportive or modifying roles in clinical course rather than causal roles.

## 6. Mechanism / Pathophysiology

### Ordered causal chain from mutation to clinical phenotype

Step 1: Biallelic loss‑of‑function mutations in EXOC6B lead to marked reduction or absence of functional EXOC6B protein in the exocyst complex.[1][12][14][15][16]  

Step 2: Loss of EXOC6B function results in impaired exocyst‑mediated exocytosis in patient fibroblasts, as demonstrated by functional assays.[12][15][16]  

Step 3: Impaired exocytosis leads to defective primary ciliogenesis, with reduced number and abnormal morphology of primary cilia in patient cells, based on in vitro studies.[12][15]  

Step 4: Disruption of primary cilium structure and assembly alters cilia‑dependent signaling pathways (such as Hedgehog and Wnt) in skeletal cells, an inference supported by general knowledge of ciliary biology but not yet directly measured in SEMDJL3.  

Step 5: Abnormal cilia‑mediated signaling in growth plate chondrocytes and osteoblasts perturbs proliferation, hypertrophy, differentiation, and matrix deposition, leading to epiphyseal and metaphyseal dysplasia and delayed ossification; this step is inferred from skeletal ciliopathy literature and radiographic correlations in SEMDJL3.[1][2][16][17]  

Step 6: Altered skeletal development of long bones, spine, and ribs leads to gracile short tubular bones, platyspondyly, irregular vertebral endplates, narrow interpedicular distances, and constricted femoral necks, as documented radiographically.[1][2][17]  

Step 7: Concurrently, EXOC6B‑mediated defects in exocytosis and extracellular matrix organization in ligaments and joint capsules result in generalized joint laxity and structural instability of large joints.[1][2][4][17]  

Step 8: Generalized ligamentous laxity, combined with dysplastic epiphyses and metaphyses, causes multiple joint dislocations (particularly hips and knees) at birth and limb malalignment (genu valgum/varum) as weight‑bearing begins.[1][2][17]  

Step 9: Cumulative skeletal deformity in the spine and appendicular skeleton leads to progressive scoliosis, kyphosis, hyperlordosis, short stature, and short neck, producing the full clinical picture of SEMDJL3.[1][2][17]  

Step 10: These anatomical and biomechanical abnormalities result in chronic pain, reduced mobility, and disability, impacting quality of life, even though cognition and major visceral organ function remain largely intact.[2][17]

In this chain, steps 1–3 are directly supported by genetic and functional data in patient fibroblasts, whereas steps 4–5 rely on extrapolation from broader ciliary and skeletal biology, and steps 6–10 are supported by clinical and radiographic observations in SEMDJL3.[1][2][12][15][16][17]

### Molecular pathways and cellular processes

At the molecular level, the central pathways implicated in SEMDJL3 involve exocytosis and primary cilium biology. EXOC6B’s role in the exocyst complex connects the disease to GO processes such as exocytosis (GO:0006887), vesicle docking involved in exocytosis (GO:0006904), and regulation of cell polarity (GO:0030010).[15] PanelApp notes that functional studies of fibroblasts from patients with EXOC6B mutations showed “abrogation of exocytosis,” demonstrating that EXOC6B deficiency impairs vesicle trafficking to the plasma membrane.[12] GeneCards’ assignment of EXOC6B to “Ciliary landscape” pathways indicates that exocyst‑mediated exocytosis is intimately linked to the biogenesis and maintenance of the primary cilium.[15] In fibroblasts, exocyst components localize to the base of the cilium and are required for targeting vesicles carrying ciliary membrane proteins and signaling receptors.

The primary cilium is a microtubule‑based organelle that functions as a hub for multiple signaling pathways, including Hedgehog, Wnt, PDGF, and others, which are critical for skeletal development.[15][16] PanelApp states that functional studies revealed “impaired primary ciliogenesis” in fibroblasts from SEMDJL3 patients, directly connecting EXOC6B loss to cilium formation defects.[12] Appropriate GO terms include GO:0060271 (cilium assembly), GO:0030992 (primary cilium), and GO:0007229 (integrin‑mediated signaling), given the exocyst’s role in cell adhesion and signaling. In skeletal cells such as growth plate chondrocytes (CL:0000138) and osteoblasts (CL:0000143), primary cilia sense mechanical and biochemical cues that regulate proliferation, hypertrophy, and matrix production; disruption of ciliogenesis can lead to disorganized growth plate architecture and abnormal ossification, as seen in skeletal ciliopathies.

Cellular processes affected in SEMDJL3 thus include vesicle trafficking, polarized membrane insertion, cilium assembly, and downstream signaling cascades that coordinate skeletal morphogenesis. The exocyst–cilium axis likely affects not only chondrocytes and osteoblasts but also fibroblasts in ligaments and joint capsules (CL:0000057), influencing extracellular matrix composition and mechanical properties.[12][15][17] Although apoptosis and autophagy have not been specifically studied in SEMDJL3, disturbances in exocytosis and ciliary signaling could indirectly influence these processes through altered receptor trafficking and stress responses, contributing to long‑term tissue remodeling.

Metabolic changes per se have not been reported in SEMDJL3; there is no evidence of systemic metabolic disturbances such as energy metabolism defects, lipid abnormalities, or amino acid metabolism issues.[1][2][17] Instead, molecular abnormalities are largely structural and signaling‑related. Immune system involvement, such as chronic inflammation or autoimmunity, has not been described.[1][2][17] Tissue damage mechanisms appear to be mechanical and developmental rather than inflammatory; aberrant skeletal architecture leads to abnormal load distribution and degenerative changes over time, but there is no primary inflammatory or fibrotic process.

### Tissue-level consequences and skeletal growth plate biology

At the tissue level, the primary consequences of EXOC6B dysfunction manifest in endochondral ossification sites, including growth plates of long bones and vertebral bodies. Epiphyseal and metaphyseal dysplasia in SEMDJL3 reflect disrupted organization of growth plate zones (resting, proliferative, hypertrophic) and aberrant matrix mineralization.[1][2][17] Narrow interpedicular distance and irregular vertebral endplates in the lumbar spine suggest abnormal vertebral body growth and remodeling.[1][17] These patterns resemble other skeletal ciliopathies, in which impaired ciliary signaling disrupts chondrocyte proliferation and hypertrophy, leading to short stature and platyspondyly, although SEMDJL3 has its own distinctive radiographic signature.[1][2][16][17]

Ligaments and joint capsules exhibit generalized laxity, indicating altered connective tissue composition and cross‑linking at the tissue level.[2][4][17] The exocyst–cilium axis in fibroblasts may regulate secretion of extracellular matrix components (collagens, proteoglycans) and cell–matrix interactions, and EXOC6B deficiency might reduce matrix deposition or alter fibril organization, though this has not yet been directly studied.[12][15][17] The combination of dysplastic articular surfaces and lax ligaments sets the stage for joint instability and dislocation. Knee and hip joints, which bear high mechanical loads, are particularly vulnerable, explaining the prominence of congenital dislocations in these locations.[1][2][17]

Spinal tissues, including vertebral bodies, intervertebral discs, and paraspinal ligaments, also suffer from structural abnormalities. Mild platyspondyly and irregular vertebral endplates compromise spinal alignment and support, contributing to progressive scoliosis, kyphosis, and hyperlordosis.[1][2][17] Short neck arises from altered cervical vertebral growth and alignment.[17] Over time, these spinal deformities can lead to secondary compression of neural elements and respiratory compromise, although such complications are better documented in Beighton‑type SEMD than in SEMDJL3.[16][6] In SEMDJL3, spinal deformity appears moderate but progressive, underscoring the need for careful orthopedic monitoring.

### Systems-level pathophysiology and clinical correlates

At the systems level, SEMDJL3 primarily involves the musculoskeletal system, with secondary effects on mobility, pain perception, and overall function. The cardiovascular, respiratory, gastrointestinal, and nervous systems are largely spared, although spinal deformity may secondarily affect respiratory mechanics in severe cases.[2][17] The absence of major organ involvement and normal intelligence differentiates SEMDJL3 from systemic ciliopathies such as Jeune asphyxiating thoracic dystrophy or Bardet–Biedl syndrome, despite shared ciliary biology.[2][15][16][17]

Clinically, the pathophysiology manifests as a combination of congenital joint dislocations, generalized hypermobility, progressive axial deformity, and short stature. Congenital hip and knee dislocations require early orthopedic evaluation and, often, surgical reduction or bracing; failure to achieve stable alignment can lead to early osteoarthritis, pain, and gait abnormalities.[1][2][17] Generalized joint laxity predisposes to recurrent dislocations and subluxations, making daily activities challenging and potentially causing chronic pain. Progressive scoliosis and kyphosis can alter body posture, contribute to back pain, and in severe cases reduce pulmonary function, although the degree of respiratory involvement in SEMDJL3 is not yet well characterized.[2][17]

Quality of life is impacted by these musculoskeletal limitations, but the absence of cognitive impairment and major organ dysfunction allows patients to pursue education and social participation with appropriate support.[2][17] The pathophysiologic chain highlights that EXOC6B loss‑of‑function acts upstream at the cellular level (exocytosis, ciliogenesis), with downstream consequences in skeletal tissue organization and whole‑body musculoskeletal function. Upstream mechanisms include germline EXOC6B mutation and exocyst disruption; midstream mechanisms involve impaired cilium assembly and growth plate signaling; downstream mechanisms encompass skeletal dysplasia, joint laxity, dislocations, and spinal deformity.

To annotate SEMDJL3 pathophysiology in ontologic frameworks, key GO biological process terms include GO:0006887 (exocytosis), GO:0006904 (vesicle docking involved in exocytosis), GO:0060271 (cilium assembly), GO:0048799 (chondrocyte differentiation), GO:0001501 (skeletal system development), and GO:0003418 (growth plate cartilage development). Relevant GO cellular component terms include GO:0005813 (centrosome), GO:0030992 (primary cilium), and GO:0000145 (exocyst complex). For cell types, CL:0000138 (growth plate chondrocyte), CL:0000143 (osteoblast), CL:0000057 (fibroblast), and CL:0000097 (osteoclast) capture the main musculoskeletal cell populations involved. These terms provide a structured vocabulary for linking EXOC6B function to SEMDJL3 phenotypes in multi‑omics databases and mechanistic models.

## 7. Anatomical Structures Affected

### Organ- and system-level involvement

The primary organ system affected in SEMDJL3 is the skeletal system, encompassing the axial skeleton (spine) and appendicular skeleton (limbs and girdles).[1][2][17] UBERON terms such as UBERON:0002418 (skeletal system), UBERON:0002415 (vertebral column), and UBERON:0002101 (lower limb) are appropriate to describe organ‑level involvement. Vertebral bodies of the cervical, thoracic, and lumbar spine exhibit platyspondyly, irregular endplates, and altered interpedicular distance, leading to scoliosis, kyphosis, and hyperlordosis.[1][2][17] The hip joints (UBERON:0001465, hip joint) and knee joints (UBERON:0001474, knee joint) are prominently affected by congenital dislocation and malalignment.[1][2][17] Patellar dislocation (UBERON:0001470, patella) is also documented.[1][17]

Secondary involvement includes the muscular system (UBERON:0001630, skeletal muscle organ), as muscles must compensate for joint instability and spinal deformity, and chronic pain may lead to muscle spasm or weakness.[2][17] The nervous system and major visceral organs are not directly affected by EXOC6B mutation, although spinal deformity could secondarily compress neural structures or alter respiratory mechanics in severe cases.[2][17] The cardiovascular system appears generally normal, with no characteristic cardiac malformations reported, distinguishing SEMDJL3 from some syndromic skeletal dysplasias.[1][2][17] The overall body system involvement is thus predominantly musculoskeletal, with potential but not obligatory secondary effects on respiratory and neurologic systems via mechanical factors.

### Tissue and cell-level localization

At the tissue level, SEMDJL3 involves cartilage (UBERON:0002385), bone (UBERON:0001474 for long bones, UBERON:0002191 for vertebra), ligament (UBERON:0001629), and joint capsule tissues. Growth plate cartilage in long bones shows disorganized architecture and abnormal ossification, reflecting epiphyseal and metaphyseal dysplasia.[1][2][17] Ligaments and joint capsules exhibit altered connective tissue composition resulting in generalized laxity and instability.[2][4][17] The articular cartilage surfaces of hips, knees, and other joints may be dysplastic, contributing to poor congruence and dislocation.

Cellular populations directly affected include growth plate chondrocytes (CL:0000138), osteoblasts (CL:0000143), osteocytes (CL:0000128), and fibroblasts in ligaments and joint capsules (CL:0000057).[12][15][17] Chondrocytes rely on primary cilia for sensing mechanical and hormonal signals, and EXOC6B‑mediated ciliogenesis defects disrupt their function, leading to skeletal dysplasia.[12][15][16][17] Osteoblasts and osteocytes also possess primary cilia and may be affected, altering bone modeling and remodeling. Fibroblasts in connective tissues depend on exocytosis for extracellular matrix deposition and cell–matrix adhesion; exocyst dysfunction in these cells likely contributes to ligamentous laxity.[12][15][17] These cell types together orchestrate the tissue‑level manifestations of SEMDJL3.

### Subcellular compartments and exocyst–cilium axis

At the subcellular level, SEMDJL3 implicates the exocyst complex (GO:0000145) and primary cilium (GO:0030992), including the basal body and ciliary membrane. EXOC6B localizes to sites of vesicle docking at the plasma membrane and to the base of the primary cilium, where the exocyst targets vesicles carrying ciliary components.[15] Loss of EXOC6B affects vesicle trafficking from Golgi and endosomal compartments (GO:0005794, Golgi apparatus; GO:0005789, endoplasmic reticulum; GO:0005773, vacuole) to the plasma membrane, reducing delivery of membrane proteins and receptors necessary for cilium assembly.[12][15] As PanelApp notes, patient fibroblasts show abrogated exocytosis and impaired primary ciliogenesis, directly implicating these subcellular compartments.[12]

Primary cilia act as antennae for signaling pathways; defects in their structure or composition can alter localization of receptors such as Patched and Smoothened (Hedgehog pathway) or Frizzled (Wnt pathway), and downstream transcriptional responses.[15][16] While specific receptor trafficking has not been studied in SEMDJL3, the general exocyst–cilium axis suggests that EXOC6B loss affects ciliary membrane composition and signaling. Other cellular compartments potentially impacted include the cytoskeleton (microtubules, actin filaments), as exocyst positioning involves cytoskeletal interactions, and the plasma membrane itself, where exocytosis occurs.[15] Overall, SEMDJL3 is a subcellular disorder of vesicle docking and cilium assembly, manifesting at higher levels as skeletal dysplasia.

### Anatomical localization and laterality patterns

Anatomical localization of SEMDJL3 manifestations includes bilateral involvement of hips and knees, as all reported cases show bilateral dislocations of these joints.[1][2][17] The bilateral nature suggests a systemic developmental defect rather than localized pathology. Scoliosis may exhibit right‑ or left‑convex curves, and limb malalignment can involve symmetrical genu valgum or varum; laterality patterns in these deformities have not been systematically reported but are likely influenced by mechanical factors as children grow.[2][17]

Localized features include gracile metacarpals and metatarsals in the hands and feet, which are symmetrical but may vary in degree among digits.[1][2][17] Spinal deformities can be localized to thoracic or lumbar regions, with cervical involvement manifesting as short neck. Radiographic features such as narrow interpedicular distance are localized to the lumbar spine.[1][17] Overall, SEMDJL3 presents a diffuse skeletal involvement with bilateral, symmetrical patterns in most manifestations, consistent with its genetic, systemic nature.

## 8. Temporal Development

### Onset characteristics

SEMDJL3 has a congenital onset for its core joint manifestations. Multiple joint dislocations, particularly of the hips and knees, are present at birth in all described cases.[1][2][17] Orphanet’s disease definition for EXOC6B‑related SEMD‑JL emphasizes that hip and knee dislocations are present at birth, and MedGen reiterates that “joint dislocations of the hips and knees are present at birth in all individuals reported to date.”[2][17] This places SEMDJL3 firmly in the category of congenital skeletal dysplasias. The age of onset category in Orphanet for SEMD with joint laxity leptodactylic type is “Infancy, Neonatal,” which is consistent with SEMDJL3.[6]

Growth deficiency and short stature develop postnatally, becoming apparent during infancy and childhood as longitudinal growth lags behind peers.[2][17] Orphanet notes “growth deficiency developing in the post‑natal period resulting in short stature,” and MedGen states that “growth deficiency develops postnatally” leading to short stature and short neck.[2][17] Radiographic spondylar dysplasia and metaphyseal changes may not be fully evident at birth but evolve over time as ossification progresses. Delayed carpal and tarsal ossification are typically recognized in early childhood when hand and foot radiographs are obtained; delayed bone age is also documented.[1][2][5][17] Thus, the onset pattern is a combination of congenital joint dislocations and early childhood radiographic and growth abnormalities.

### Disease progression and stages

Disease progression in SEMDJL3 can be conceptualized in stages, although formal staging systems have not been developed. An early stage encompasses the neonatal period, where multiple joint dislocations and generalized joint laxity are present, but growth deficiency and spinal deformity are still emerging.[1][2][17] Orthopedic interventions in this stage focus on reducing dislocations and stabilizing joints. A childhood stage involves progressive development of short stature, limb malalignment (genu valgum/varum), and radiographic signs of epiphyseal and metaphyseal dysplasia, along with delayed carpal/tarsal ossification.[2][5][17] Functional impact includes delayed walking and clumsiness due to joint instability.[5][17]

An adolescent stage sees further progression of spinal deformities—scoliosis, kyphosis, hyperlordosis—and worsening limb malalignment as weight‑bearing and activity increase.[2][17] At this stage, pain and fatigue may become prominent, and orthopedic surgery for spinal stabilization or limb alignment may be considered. An adult stage is characterized by chronic musculoskeletal pain, possible secondary osteoarthritis in dysplastic joints, and functional limitations in mobility and self‑care. Based on SEMDJL2 longitudinal data, progressive joint limitation and disability can develop, although such detailed progression has not yet been documented for SEMDJL3.[11] The overall progression rate appears slow but continuous, consistent with a lifelong skeletal dysplasia.

The disease course pattern is chronic and progressive rather than episodic or relapsing‑remitting.[1][2][17] There are no acute exacerbations driven by inflammatory flares; instead, mechanical and structural changes accumulate over time. Disease duration is lifelong, with no spontaneous recovery of skeletal abnormalities. Complications such as degenerative joint disease and spinal stenosis could arise, but specific data for SEMDJL3 are lacking.

### Critical periods and windows for intervention

Critical periods in SEMDJL3 correspond to developmental windows during which interventions can most effectively influence long‑term outcomes. The neonatal period is critical for managing congenital hip and knee dislocations; early reduction and stabilization may improve joint congruence and reduce later osteoarthritis.[1][2][17] Orthopedic practice in analogous conditions (e.g., developmental dysplasia of the hip) underscores the importance of early intervention for optimal outcomes. Early infancy and childhood are critical windows for growth monitoring and initiation of physical therapy to strengthen muscles around unstable joints, promoting motor development while minimizing injury.[2][17]

Adolescence is a critical period for addressing progressive scoliosis and kyphosis, as spinal growth accelerates and deformities may worsen. Timely bracing or surgical correction can prevent severe deformity and potential cardiopulmonary compromise.[2][17] Adult life is critical for managing chronic pain, preserving function, and preventing secondary complications such as falls and fractures. While these windows are inferred from general orthopedic principles and limited SEMDJL3 data, they provide a framework for staging intervention priorities.

Remission patterns are not applicable to SEMDJL3, as the disease does not exhibit periods of spontaneous improvement; at best, symptoms such as pain may be partially alleviated with treatment, but underlying skeletal abnormalities persist.[1][2][17]

## 9. Inheritance and Population

### Inheritance pattern, penetrance, and expressivity

SEMDJL3 follows an autosomal recessive inheritance pattern.[1][2][5][8][12][16] OMIM explicitly states that “Spondylometaphyseal dysplasia with joint laxity type 3 is an autosomal recessive disorder,” referencing Girisha et al. and Campos‑Xavier et al.[1] Orphanet lists EXOC6B‑related SEMD‑JL as autosomal recessive.[2] Monarch Initiative and DOID similarly categorize SEMDJL3 under “autosomal recessive disease” and note its material basis in germline EXOC6B mutation.[5][8]

Penetrance appears complete in individuals with biallelic truncating EXOC6B variants; all reported homozygotes exhibit characteristic skeletal and joint features.[1][2][12][16][17] No asymptomatic or subclinical homozygous carriers have been described, although extremely mild cases might be under‑recognized. Expressivity shows some variability, particularly in the severity of spinal deformity and limb malalignment, but core features such as joint dislocation, generalized joint laxity, and skeletal dysplasia are consistent across individuals.[1][2][12][16][17] There is no evidence of genetic anticipation, as the mechanism does not involve repeat expansions or known unstable elements.[1][2][12][16]

Germline mosaicism has not been reported in SEMDJL3. Given the autosomal recessive pattern, parental germline mosaicism would be less relevant than in dominant disorders; carriers are heterozygous and unaffected, and mosaicism would primarily influence carrier status rather than disease expression.[1][2][12] Founder effects—population‑specific recurrent variants—have not been systematically characterized, but the presence of identical variants (e.g., p.Tyr302Ter) in consanguineous families suggests that local founder mutations may exist.[1][12][16] Carrier frequency for pathogenic EXOC6B variants is unknown but likely extremely low (<1/10,000) given the rarity of SEMDJL3 and absence of these variants in population databases.[1][2][8][12][16]

### Epidemiology, geographic distribution, and demographics

SEMDJL3 is an ultra‑rare disorder. Orphanet’s EXOC6B‑related SEMD‑JL entry does not provide a specific prevalence estimate, but the broader SEMD with joint laxity leptodactylic type (SEMDJL2) is estimated at <1/1,000,000.[2][6] Given that SEMDJL3 has fewer published cases than SEMDJL2, its prevalence is likely at or below this level. No incidence data (new cases per year) have been reported, and there are no disease registries focused on SEMDJL3.[1][2][8][12][16][17]

Geographic distribution reflects the locations of case reports rather than true prevalence. Girisha et al.’s report of two brothers with EXOC6B‑related SEMD originated from India, as inferred from author affiliations, suggesting that SEMDJL3 occurs in South Asian populations.[12][16] Campos‑Xavier et al.’s cases may have originated from European populations, indicating broader geographic distribution.[12][16] There is no evidence that SEMDJL3 is restricted to particular ethnic groups; instead, it likely occurs worldwide at very low frequency, with consanguineous families at higher risk.[1][2][12][16]

Sex ratio among reported cases appears roughly balanced, with both males and females affected.[1][2][12][16][17] Orphanet and OMIM do not note a sex bias. Age distribution of affected individuals spans neonates to adults, based on case descriptions, but there are no large cohort data to define mean age at diagnosis or survival. Given the congenital onset and chronic course, SEMDJL3 affects individuals throughout life.

### Consanguinity and population genetics

Consanguinity plays a notable role in SEMDJL3, as at least one reported family involved first‑cousin parents, leading to homozygosity for a rare EXOC6B variant.[12] Autosomal recessive conditions are more common in populations with high consanguinity rates, and SEMDJL3 fits this pattern. Population genetics data for EXOC6B in gnomAD and other databases show extremely low frequencies of loss‑of‑function alleles, consistent with strong purifying selection.[1][12][16] Thus, the main population‑level risk factor is consanguineous marriage in communities where a pathogenic EXOC6B allele exists.

## 10. Diagnostics

### Clinical and radiographic assessment

Diagnosis of SEMDJL3 begins with careful clinical evaluation of congenital joint dislocations and generalized joint laxity. Neonates presenting with bilateral hip and knee dislocations, dislocated patellae, and generalized hypermobility should prompt consideration of SEMD‑JL disorders.[1][2][17] Additional clinical signs include short stature and growth deficiency developing in early childhood, slender fingers with distal tapering, limb malalignment (genu valgum/varum), short neck, and progressive spinal deformities (scoliosis, kyphosis, hyperlordosis).[2][4][17]

Radiographic evaluation is critical. X‑rays of the spine reveal mild platyspondyly, irregular vertebral end plates, and narrow interpedicular distance in the lumbar spine, consistent with spondylar dysplasia.[1][17] Long bone radiographs show severely dysplastic epiphyses at the proximal humerus and femur, metaphyseal irregularities, constricted femoral necks, and mild platyspondyly.[1][2][17] Hand and foot radiographs demonstrate gracile metacarpals and metatarsals, delayed ossification of carpal and tarsal bones, and carpal bone hypoplasia.[1][2][5][17] These features distinguish SEMDJL3 from generalized hypermobility syndromes without skeletal dysplasia, such as Ehlers–Danlos syndrome, and from other skeletal dysplasias like sponastrime dysplasia.[13][2][6]

Standard laboratory tests (blood chemistries, inflammatory markers, enzyme assays) are typically normal in SEMDJL3; there is no characteristic laboratory biomarker for this disorder.[1][2][17] Imaging modalities beyond plain radiography—such as MRI or CT—may be used to assess spinal canal dimensions, joint congruence, or soft tissue structures, but they are not diagnostic per se. There are no specific electrophysiological or biopsy findings uniquely associated with SEMDJL3; histopathology of cartilage and bone would show nonspecific dysplastic changes.

### Genetic testing strategies

Given the overlap among SEMD‑JL subtypes and other skeletal dysplasias, genetic testing is essential for definitive diagnosis of SEMDJL3. ### Differential diagnosis and screening considerations

Differential diagnosis for SEMDJL3 includes other SEMD‑JL subtypes, generalized hypermobility syndromes, and other spondyloepimetaphyseal dysplasias. ## 11. Outcome / Prognosis

### Survival and mortality

Specific survival and mortality data for SEMDJL3 have not been systematically reported, owing to the small number of cases and lack of long‑term follow‑up in published reports.[1][2][12][16][17] However, available descriptions suggest that SEMDJL3 does not typically lead to early death due to visceral organ failure, unlike Beighton‑type SEMD‑JL, where severe kyphoscoliosis and thoracic asymmetry can cause respiratory compromise and early mortality.[16][6] EXOC6B‑related SEMD‑JL appears to be compatible with survival into adulthood, although the oldest reported patients are not as old as the 66‑year‑old SEMDJL2 case.[11][12][16]

Life expectancy in SEMDJL3 is likely near normal given normal cognition and absence of major organ involvement, but musculoskeletal complications could reduce quality of life and, in severe cases, contribute to morbidity that indirectly affects longevity.[2][17] Mortality rate specific to SEMDJL3 is unknown. Disease‑specific mortality (deaths directly attributable to SEMDJL3) would primarily arise from complications such as severe spinal deformity leading to respiratory compromise, but such scenarios have not been documented in published EXOC6B‑related cases.[1][2][17]

### Morbidity, disability, and quality of life

Morbidity in SEMDJL3 is substantial due to musculoskeletal disability. Multiple joint dislocations at birth require intensive orthopedic management and may result in residual instability and deformity despite treatment, leading to chronic pain and limited mobility.[1][2][17] Progressive scoliosis, kyphosis, and lordosis can cause back pain, postural difficulties, and potential neurologic symptoms if spinal canal narrowing occurs.[1][2][17] Limb malalignment (genu valgum/varum) affects gait and balance, increasing the risk of falls. Short stature and short neck can have psychosocial impacts and may complicate anesthesia or airway management in surgical procedures.

Based on SEMDJL2 longitudinal data, progressive joint limitation and disability can culminate in early retirement and dependence for daily activities.[11] While SEMDJL3 may be somewhat less well characterized, its overlapping skeletal features suggest similar risks of disability, though severity may vary. Quality of life measures (EQ‑5D, SF‑36, PROMIS) have not been directly applied to SEMDJL3 patients, but inferentially, domains such as mobility, self‑care, pain/discomfort, and usual activities are likely compromised.[2][17] Normal intelligence and absence of major organ disease allow individuals to engage intellectually and socially, but physical limitations can restrict full participation.

Disability outcomes include reduced ability to perform physically demanding jobs, dependence on assistive devices (canes, walkers), and potential need for assistance in personal hygiene and household tasks in severe cases. Orthopedic surgeries, although beneficial, carry their own morbidity and rehabilitation demands. The International Classification of Functioning, Disability and Health (ICF) framework would map SEMDJL3 primarily to impairments in body structure (bones and joints), body functions (mobility, muscle power), and activity limitations (walking, self‑care).

### Prognostic factors and biomarkers

Prognostic factors in SEMDJL3 likely include severity of initial joint dislocations, degree of spinal deformity, success of early orthopedic interventions, and presence of comorbidities, although specific prognostic models have not been established.[1][2][17] Early and effective reduction of hip and knee dislocations may improve long‑term joint stability and reduce pain. Mild initial scoliosis may be more amenable to conservative management than severe deformity, influencing long‑term function.

No molecular biomarkers predicting disease course are known. EXOC6B variant type (nonsense vs frameshift) has not been correlated with severity, but all reported variants are truncating, so genotype‑phenotype differences may be subtle.[1][12][16] Functional biomarkers such as degree of exocytosis impairment or ciliogenesis defects in fibroblasts have not been used prognostically, although in principle, more severe cellular defects could correlate with more severe skeletal manifestations.[12][15] In the absence of validated biomarkers, clinical and radiographic assessments remain the main tools for prognostication.

## 12. Treatment

### Current management: orthopedic and rehabilitative care

There is no disease‑modifying pharmacologic or gene‑based therapy for SEMDJL3 at present; treatment focuses on orthopedic management and rehabilitation to optimize function and reduce pain.[1][2][17] NCIT terms relevant to this approach include “orthopedic surgery” (NCIT:C15273), “physical therapy” (NCIT:C49667), and “occupational therapy” (NCIT:C15279). Congenital hip and knee dislocations are addressed with orthopedic interventions such as closed or open reduction, casting, bracing, and, in some cases, surgical reconstruction.[1][2][17] Early intervention during infancy is critical to improve joint congruence and reduce long‑term osteoarthritis risk.

Generalized joint laxity necessitates careful guidance on safe physical activity. Physical therapy aims to strengthen muscles around unstable joints, improve proprioception, and maintain range of motion without exacerbating dislocations.[2][17] Occupational therapy helps individuals adapt daily tasks to accommodate limitations. Bracing or orthotic devices may support joints and improve alignment, particularly in genu valgum/varum. Spinal deformities are monitored with serial radiographs; treatments include bracing for mild scoliosis and surgical spinal fusion for severe, progressive deformity.[2][17]

Rehabilitation is ongoing, with periodic reassessment as patients grow and their musculoskeletal demands change. NCIT terms such as “rehabilitation therapy” (NCIT:C15275) and “supportive care” (NCIT:C61412) capture this aspect. The goal is to prevent or delay disability, maintain independence, and minimize pain through a multidisciplinary team involving orthopedic surgeons, physiatrists, physical therapists, and occupational therapists.

### Pharmacologic and supportive care

Pharmacologic treatment in SEMDJL3 is symptomatic, focusing on pain management and, when needed, muscle spasm control. Analgesics such as acetaminophen (CHEBI:46195) and nonsteroidal anti‑inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) such as ibuprofen (CHEBI:5855) are used to manage musculoskeletal pain, with NCIT terms “analgesic therapy” (NCIT:C12219) and “nonsteroidal anti‑inflammatory agent” (NCIT:C610). ### Surgical and interventional strategies

Surgical interventions are central in the management of SEMDJL3. NCIT terms include “joint reconstruction surgery” (NCIT:C21058) and “spinal fusion surgery” (NCIT:C48189). ### Emerging and experimental therapies

Advanced therapeutics such as gene therapy, RNA‑based therapies, or small‑molecule modulators of exocytosis or ciliogenesis have not been developed for SEMDJL3. Gene therapy approaches for skeletal dysplasias are in early stages, and targeting EXOC6B would require safe and efficient delivery of gene constructs to skeletal and connective tissues, which is currently a major challenge.[1][2][15][16][17] CRISPR‑based gene editing could, in principle, correct EXOC6B mutations, but off‑target risks and delivery limitations make this speculative at present.

No clinical trials specifically enroll SEMDJL3 patients for experimental therapies. However, basic research on exocyst–cilium biology may identify targets for small‑molecule modulation of ciliogenesis, which could have applications in skeletal ciliopathies, including SEMDJL3.[12][15][16] At present, treatment remains supportive and orthopedic, with no disease‑modifying experimental options.

### Treatment outcomes and personalized medicine

Treatment outcomes in SEMDJL3 are determined by early and effective orthopedic interventions, adherence to rehabilitation, and avoidance of complications. While specific outcome statistics (e.g., treatment response rates) are not available, general orthopedic principles suggest that early reduction of dislocations and timely correction of deformities improve functional outcomes.[1][2][17] Side effects and adverse events include surgical complications (infection, hardware failure), anesthetic risks, and rehabilitation‑related injuries if exercises are not properly guided.

Personalized medicine in SEMDJL3 focuses on tailoring orthopedic and rehabilitative strategies to the individual’s anatomy, growth trajectory, and functional goals, rather than on genotype‑guided pharmacotherapy. ## 13. Prevention

### Primary, secondary, and tertiary prevention

Given its Mendelian etiology, primary prevention of SEMDJL3 focuses on reproductive choices rather than modification of environmental risk factors. For at‑risk couples with known EXOC6B pathogenic variants, primary prevention options include preimplantation genetic diagnosis (PGD), prenatal testing, and avoiding consanguineous unions that increase the probability of homozygosity.[1][2][12] NCIT terms such as “genetic counseling” (NCIT:C18188) and “preimplantation genetic diagnosis” (NCIT:C12584) apply. Population‑level primary prevention (vaccination, risk factor modification) is not relevant.

Secondary prevention involves early detection and management of complications. Neonatal physical examination and radiography can detect congenital hip and knee dislocations, allowing early orthopedic interventions to prevent long‑term disability.[1][2][17] Growth monitoring and periodic spinal screening can identify emerging deformities, and early bracing or surgical correction can prevent severe spinal curvature. These strategies reduce morbidity but do not change the underlying genetic disease.

Tertiary prevention encompasses measures to prevent complications in individuals already affected by SEMDJL3. This includes physical therapy to maintain muscle strength and joint stability, fall prevention strategies, pain management, and orthotic use to reduce further joint damage.[2][17] Tertiary prevention also involves psychosocial support to mitigate the impact of disability on mental health and social participation.

### Genetic counseling and reproductive options

Genetic counseling is central to prevention in SEMDJL3. Once a proband is identified, carrier testing of parents and siblings allows determination of recurrence risk and informs reproductive decisions.[1][2][12] For autosomal recessive inheritance, each subsequent pregnancy has a 25% risk of producing an affected child if both parents are carriers. Prenatal diagnosis via chorionic villus sampling or amniocentesis with EXOC6B sequencing can detect affected fetuses, enabling informed choices.[1][2][12] PGD with in vitro fertilization allows selection of embryos free of EXOC6B pathogenic variants.

Counseling also addresses implications for extended family, particularly in consanguineous communities. Education about autosomal recessive inheritance and carrier risks may reduce incidence if high‑risk unions are avoided. However, given SEMDJL3’s extreme rarity, population‑level impact is modest.

### Public health and behavioral aspects

Public health interventions specific to SEMDJL3 are not feasible given its rarity. Broader public health efforts promoting genetic literacy, access to genetic counseling, and awareness of consanguinity‑related risks may indirectly reduce incidence of SEMDJL3 and other recessive disorders.[1][2][12] Environmental interventions (e.g., reducing pollution) are not relevant to SEMDJL3 occurrence.

Behavioral interventions center on lifestyle modifications to reduce complications, such as avoiding high‑impact activities that stress unstable joints, adopting ergonomically supportive postures, and engaging in tailored exercise programs that strengthen muscles without causing dislocations.[2][17] These are individualized rather than population‑wide measures.

Comparative pathology suggests that primary cilium and exocyst functions in skeletal cells are conserved across vertebrates, meaning that EXOC6B loss‑of‑function could theoretically produce skeletal dysplasia in animals similar to human SEMDJL3. However, most exocyst research in animals focuses on neural development, epithelial polarity, and exocytosis in non‑skeletal tissues, leaving skeletal phenotypes less characterized.[15][16] Evolutionary conservation of EXOC6B’s role in ciliogenesis and exocytosis supports the biological plausibility of cross‑species skeletal effects, even if specific pathological entities have not been identified.

Transmission of SEMDJL3 from animals to humans is not applicable; the disease is noninfectious and arises from human germline mutations. Cross‑species susceptibility relates more to experimental modeling than to zoonotic risk.

Cellular models recapitulate upstream mechanistic features (exocytosis impairment, ciliogenesis defects) but not tissue‑level skeletal phenotypes. Model organisms and cellular systems can be used to study EXOC6B’s role in exocytosis and cilium assembly, identify interacting proteins, and test small‑molecule modulators. ## Conclusion

EXOC6B‑related spondyloepimetaphyseal dysplasia with joint laxity type 3 (SEMDJL3) is an ultra‑rare, autosomal recessive skeletal dysplasia defined by a distinctive constellation of congenital multiple large‑joint dislocations, severe generalized joint laxity, progressive spondylar and epiphyseal–metaphyseal dysplasia, gracile short tubular bones, delayed carpal and tarsal ossification, and postnatal growth deficiency leading to short stature.[1][2][5][8][17] The causal mechanism is biallelic germline loss‑of‑function mutation in EXOC6B, an exocyst complex component essential for exocytosis and primary ciliogenesis.[1][12][14][15][16] Functional studies in patient fibroblasts demonstrate abrogated exocytosis and impaired primary cilium formation, linking EXOC6B deficiency to cilium‑dependent signaling disruptions in skeletal and connective tissues.[12][15][16] These upstream molecular defects translate into tissue‑level abnormalities in growth plate cartilage, bone, ligaments, and joint capsules, producing the clinical phenotype of SEMDJL3.

Clinically, SEMDJL3 must be distinguished from related SEMD‑JL subtypes (Beighton type, leptodactylic type) and generalized hypermobility syndromes through careful assessment of radiographic skeletal dysplasia, inheritance pattern, and genetic testing.[1][2][6][11][13][16][17] Whole‑exome sequencing and skeletal dysplasia gene panels are key diagnostic tools, with EXOC6B now recognized as an established causal gene.[1][12][16] Management is supportive and orthopedic, focusing on early reduction of joint dislocations, correction of limb and spinal deformities, and long‑term rehabilitation to maintain mobility and reduce pain.[1][2][17] Intelligence is typically normal, and visceral organs are spared, so life expectancy may be near normal, although musculoskeletal disability can significantly impact quality of life.[2][17]

From a research perspective, SEMDJL3 illustrates how gene discovery and functional studies refine rare disease nosology, moving from phenotypic labels such as “spondyloepimetaphyseal dysplasia with joint laxity” to genetically defined subtypes with distinct mechanisms.[1][2][10][16] EXOC6B connects SEMDJL3 to broader exocyst and cilium biology, revealing a skeletal ciliopathy within the exocyst–cilium axis.[12][15][16] Future work should aim to expand the clinical cohort, characterize natural history and quality of life more rigorously, elucidate downstream signaling pathways in skeletal cells, and explore potential therapies targeting exocytosis or ciliogenesis. Model organisms and patient‑derived cellular systems will be essential in these efforts.

For disease knowledge base integration, SEMDJL3 can be annotated with MONDO:0032724, OMIM:618395, ORPHA:642085, DOID:0112200, and SNOMED CT:1286834000, with EXOC6B (HGNC:17085, OMIM:607880, NCBI Gene:23233, UniProt:Q9Y2D4) as the causal gene.[1][2][5][8][14][15][16] Key HPO terms include HP:0002651 (spondyloepimetaphyseal dysplasia), HP:0002761 (generalized joint laxity), HP:0004322 (short stature), HP:0003434 (delayed carpal ossification), and HP:0000926 (platyspondyly).[1][2][4][5][17] Mechanistic annotation should leverage GO terms for exocytosis and cilium assembly and CL terms for chondrocytes, osteoblasts, and fibroblasts.[12][15][16] Such structured representation will facilitate cross‑disease comparisons, mechanistic modeling, and future translational research aimed at improving outcomes for individuals with this rare but informative skeletal disorder.
