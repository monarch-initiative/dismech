Pneumonic Tularemia — Comprehensive Disease Characteristics Report

Disease: Pneumonic Tularemia MONDO ID: MONDO:0002212 Category: Infectious Disease Causative agent: Francisella tularensis (Gram-negative facultative intracellular coccobacillus)

Summary

Pneumonic tularemia is the pulmonary and most lethal clinical form of tularemia, an acute zoonosis caused by the Gram-negative facultative intracellular bacterium Francisella tularensis. It arises either as primary pneumonic disease following inhalation of aerosolized organisms (e.g., landscaping activities, laboratory accidents, or a deliberate bioterrorism release) or as secondary pneumonic disease through hematogenous spread from other clinical forms. Among the classical clinical forms (ulceroglandular, glandular, oculoglandular, oropharyngeal, typhoidal, and pneumonic), the pneumonic presentation carries the greatest mortality, and it is the form of greatest bioterrorism concern because F. tularensis is one of the most infectious bacteria known — as few as ~10 organisms inhaled can initiate infection. The organism is a CDC Category A (Tier 1) select agent.

Mechanistically, disease follows a well-characterized intracellular pathway: the bacterium invades alveolar macrophages and dendritic cells, escapes the phagosome within hours via a Type VI secretion system encoded on the Francisella Pathogenicity Island (FPI), and replicates in the cytosol. Virulent type A strains actively subvert early innate immunity through an atypical, under-acylated lipid A that evades TLR4 recognition. Once in the cytosol, bacterial DNA — liberated by interferon-inducible guanylate-binding proteins (GBPs) and IRGB10 — is sensed by the AIM2 inflammasome, driving caspase-1 activation, IL-1β/IL-18 secretion, and pyroptosis. Protective adaptive immunity is Th1-polarized and depends on IFN-γ, TNF-α, iNOS, and CD4⁺ T cells (T-bet).

Clinically, untreated pneumonic tularemia carries roughly 30% mortality, but prompt antibiotic therapy reduces case-fatality to below ~2%. Aminoglycosides (streptomycin, gentamicin) are the historical treatment of choice; fluoroquinolones (ciprofloxacin) and doxycycline are effective alternatives, and the 2025 CDC guidelines list fluoroquinolones and doxycycline as first-line options. Diagnosis relies chiefly on serology (microagglutination) and PCR because culture is insensitive and biohazardous. No licensed human vaccine exists. Tularemia is re-emerging across Europe and North America (2024–2025), reinforcing the need for clinical awareness, rapid diagnostics, and preventive measures.

Section 1 — Disease Information

Overview. Pneumonic (respiratory) tularemia is the lung-centered manifestation of infection by Francisella tularensis. It presents with fever, cough, dyspnea, pleuritic chest pain, and radiographic infiltrates, often with hilar lymphadenopathy and pleural effusion. Primary pneumonic disease results from inhalation of infectious aerosols; secondary pneumonic disease develops when bacteria seed the lungs from bloodstream dissemination of ulceroglandular, typhoidal, or other forms.

Data source type: Information is derived from aggregated disease-level resources — clinical case series, outbreak investigations, systematic reviews, and experimental animal/in vitro studies — rather than from individual EHR-derived patient records. A 2024 systematic review aggregated 870 cases spanning 1993–2023.

Section 2 — Etiology

Primary cause (infectious). Pneumonic tularemia is caused entirely by infection with Francisella tularensis; it is not a genetic disease. Human infection is initiated by inhalation of aerosolized bacteria. F. tularensis is among the most infectious bacteria known — inoculation or inhalation of as few as 10 organisms initiates infection (Finding F002; PMID: 38294108; PMID: 29183485).

"requires as few as 10 organisms to cause disease, making this potential bioterrorism agent one of the most infectious bacterial pathogens known" — PMID: 38294108

Environmental / occupational risk factors: - Aerosol-generating landscaping activities — lawn mowing and brush cutting were significant risk factors in the Martha's Vineyard primary pneumonic outbreak (odds ratio 9.2, 95% CI 1.6–68.0) (Finding F008; PMID: 11757506). - Occupational exposure — laboratory workers, landscapers, farmers, hunters, and those handling infected animals (rabbits, hares, rodents). - Arthropod vectors — ticks (Dermacentor, Amblyomma), deer flies, mosquitoes (predominant in Scandinavia) — though these more commonly cause ulceroglandular disease. - Animal contact — lagomorphs, rodents, and domestic cats/dogs. Dog-related exposures accounted for 3.3% of U.S. cases, of which 13% were pneumonic (PMID: 30556330). - Deliberate aerosol release — the pneumonic form is the most likely presentation after intentional release (PMID: 29183485). - Sex/age — systemic disease occurs more commonly in older patients (PMID: 22911645).

Genetic risk / protective factors: No human germline susceptibility or protective variants are established for tularemia; this is an environmentally/infectiously acquired disease. Host-genetic determinants of severity are described almost exclusively in mouse models (e.g., inflammasome genes Aim2, Nlrp3), not in humans. Gene-environment interactions in the classical human-disease sense are therefore not applicable; the relevant "gene–environment" axis is the interaction between host innate-immune genes and the inhaled bacterial dose/strain virulence (see Section 6).

Characteristics: - Age of onset: Any age; primarily adult in occupational/aerosol settings. - Severity: Moderate to severe; pneumonic form is the most severe clinical presentation (Finding F001). - Progression: Acute and potentially rapidly progressive; can advance to respiratory failure, sepsis, and multi-organ involvement if untreated. - Frequency among affected individuals: In U.S. case series, pneumonic disease constituted ~12% of all tularemia cases (PMID: 22911645); in dedicated aerosol outbreaks it predominates (11 of 15 cases primary pneumonic on Martha's Vineyard; PMID: 11757506).

Quality-of-life impact: As an acute, treatable infection, long-term QoL burden is limited in survivors treated promptly; however, delayed treatment can result in prolonged convalescence, pleural complications requiring intervention, and, rarely, death. No disease-specific EQ-5D/SF-36 datasets exist for pneumonic tularemia.

Section 4 — Genetic / Molecular Information

Not applicable in the human-host genetic sense. Pneumonic tularemia is an infectious disease with no causal human genes, pathogenic germline variants, modifier genes, human epigenetic signatures, or chromosomal abnormalities. There is no OMIM Mendelian entry, no ClinVar variant set, and no inheritance pattern.

The relevant molecular determinants are bacterial: - Francisella Pathogenicity Island (FPI): a ~16–19 gene cluster including the iglABCD operon encoding a Type VI secretion system (T6SS) required for phagosomal escape and intramacrophage growth; FPI mutants are highly attenuated (Finding F003; PMID: 17395722, PMID: 17233889). - Regulators: mglA/mglB control FPI expression. - Effector opiA (a bacterial PI3-kinase) modifies phagosomal PI(3)P to promote escape (PMID: 30057173). - LPS/lipid A biosynthesis genes (lpxD1/lpxD2, galU) shape immune evasion (PMID: 28478203, PMID: 21819572).

Host modifier genes (model-organism level): In mice, Aim2, Nlrp3, Tbx21 (T-bet), Ifng, Tnf, and Nos2 modify susceptibility/severity (Sections 5–6).

Section 5 — Environmental Information

Infectious agent (taxon): Francisella tularensis, NCBI Taxon 263. Two clinically important subspecies: - subsp. tularensis (Type A) — North America; highly virulent. Subdivided into type A-west and the more severe type A-east (Finding F009; PMID: 16836829). - subsp. holarctica (Type B) — Holarctic (Europe, Asia, North America); less virulent, rarely fatal.

"type A-west infections are less severe than either type B or type A-east infections" — PMID: 16836829

Environmental persistence: F. tularensis Type A survives in brackish water and salt-influenced soil similarly to Type B, which may explain why Martha's Vineyard sustains pneumonic transmission (PMID: 21136042). Diverse Francisella species are detectable in environmental water/sediment/soil at active foci (PMID: 19669828).

Environmental / lifestyle factors: Aerosol-generating outdoor work (mowing, brush cutting), hunting/skinning lagomorphs, farming, and laboratory handling. Waterborne outbreaks (contaminated drinking water) are common in Turkey and drive oropharyngeal rather than pneumonic disease (PMID: 42343287). No specific dietary or smoking association is established for the pneumonic form.

Section 6 — Mechanism / Pathophysiology

Ordered causal chain (inhalation → clinical pneumonia)

  1. Inhalation of aerosolized F. tularensis (≥ ~10 organisms) deposits bacteria in the alveolar space → leads to uptake by alveolar macrophages and dendritic cells (and alveolar epithelial cells).
  2. Bacterial invasion of macrophages/DCs → results in formation of the Francisella-containing phagosome (FCP).
  3. FPI/Type VI secretion system activity (IglABCD; effector OpiA remodels phagosomal PI(3)P) → causes disruption of the phagosomal membrane and phagosomal escape within a few hours (demonstrated in vitro and in macrophages) (PMID: 32694562, PMID: 30057173).
  4. Cytosolic replication → leads to high intracellular bacterial burdens.
  5. In parallel, atypical tetra-acylated lipid A on the bacterial surface precludes efficient TLR4 recognition and cationic antimicrobial peptide binding → results in blunted/delayed early innate signaling (chemokines, neutrophil recruitment) — an active immune-evasion branch (demonstrated: prior type A aerosol exposure antagonizes pulmonary LPS response; galU mutant reverses this) (PMID: 28807561, PMID: 26510639, PMID: 21819572).
  6. Cytosolic bacterial DNA is liberated by interferon-inducible GBP2/GBP5 and IRGB10, which attack the bacterial membrane causing bacteriolysis → leads to DNA release (PMID: 25774716, PMID: 27693356).
  7. AIM2 inflammasome senses cytosolic DNA → activates caspase-1 → drives processing/secretion of IL-1β and IL-18 and pyroptosis (demonstrated for F. novicida/LVS) (PMID: 21883803).
  8. Branch: An ASC-dependent, caspase-1-independent (caspase-8) pathway also generates IL-18, boosting NK-cell IFN-γ (PMID: 23975862).
  9. IL-18 + IL-12 → NK/T-cell IFN-γ; with TNF-α these activate iNOS (NOS2) in macrophages → results in nitric-oxide-mediated bacterial killing (demonstrated in LVS aerosol model) (PMID: 19237526).
  10. Th1 adaptive response (T-bet–dependent CD4⁺ T cells, plus antibody including IgA) → mediates control and clearance; loss of T-bet or CD4⁺ T cells → results in overwhelming infection (PMID: 24421047, PMID: 19211773).
  11. Net effect on tissue: If bacterial replication outpaces innate/adaptive control → widespread pyroptotic cell death, inflammatory infiltration, necrotizing pneumonia, pleural effusion, bacteremic dissemination → clinical pneumonic tularemia, potentially sepsis and death.

Detail by category

  • Molecular pathways: cytosolic DNA sensing → AIM2/ASC/caspase-1; TLR4/MyD88 (subverted by lipid A); interferon (type I & II)-inducible GBP/IRGB10 antibacterial program; iNOS/NO production.
  • Cellular processes: phagocytosis, phagosomal escape, intracellular replication, pyroptosis (GO:0070269), inflammasome assembly, inflammation (GO:0006954), autophagy/xenophagy (ATG5 modulates immunity, PMID: 42514097).
  • Protein dysfunction (bacterial virulence): T6SS effector delivery; PI3-kinase effector OpiA alters phosphoinositide signaling.
  • Immune involvement: Th1/IFN-γ-dominant protective immunity; excessive early NLRP3 signaling can be host-detrimental (PMID: 34690967); antibody (IgG2a, IgA) contributes but requires host IFN-γ/T cells to protect (PMID: 8039881).
  • Metabolic: macrophage selenoproteins restrict intracellular replication (PMID: 34777335); acidic-pH lipid A remodeling (PMID: 28807561).
  • Tissue damage mechanisms: necrotizing granulomatous inflammation, pyroptotic cell death, neutrophil-mediated injury, pleural inflammation.

Section 7 — Anatomical Structures Affected

Organ level: - Primary: Lung (UBERON:0002048), including alveoli (UBERON:0002299) and bronchi. - Secondary: Pleura/pleural cavity (UBERON:0002402) — effusion/empyema; hilar/mediastinal lymph nodes (UBERON:0002509); with dissemination: spleen (UBERON:0002106), liver (UBERON:0002107), bone marrow, and rarely heart (myocarditis reported, PMID: 30656604). - Body systems: Respiratory system (primary); reticuloendothelial/lymphatic system; potentially cardiovascular (sepsis, rare myocarditis).

Tissue and cell level: - Alveolar epithelium; pulmonary interstitium. - Target cells: alveolar macrophages (CL:0000583), dendritic cells (CL:0000451), and other mononuclear phagocytes are the principal intracellular niche; neutrophils recruited to lung.

Subcellular level (GO Cellular Component): - Phagosome (GO:0045335) → Francisella-containing vacuole; escape into cytosol/cytoplasm (GO:0005829) — the replicative compartment. AIM2 inflammasome assembles in the cytosol.

Localization / lateralization: Bilateral or unilateral patchy infiltrates; pleural effusions may be unilateral. Hilar adenopathy commonly bilateral.

Section 8 — Temporal Development

  • Onset: Acute, after an incubation of 3–5 days (range 1–14). Median incubation ~3 days in Missouri case review (PMID: 22911645).
  • Progression: Rapid systemic febrile illness; can progress to respiratory failure and sepsis within days if untreated. Not staged like cancer; described as early (febrile, cough) → established pneumonia (infiltrates, effusion) → severe/complicated (empyema, respiratory failure, bacteremia).
  • Course pattern: Acute, monophasic; self-limited only rarely — most severe cases require antibiotics. With treatment, recovery is the rule; convalescence over 1–4 weeks.
  • Duration: Days to weeks (acute); not a chronic lifelong disease.
  • Remission: Treatment-induced resolution is standard with appropriate antibiotics. Relapse can occur with bacteriostatic agents (tetracyclines) or short courses.
  • Critical periods: Early antibiotic initiation is the key intervention window; delayed therapy increases complication and mortality risk (PMID: 42601640, PMID: 22652404).

Section 9 — Inheritance and Population

Inheritance: Not applicable — infectious, non-heritable.

Epidemiology: - Tularemia is a rare, reportable zoonosis. The pneumonic form is a minority of cases except in aerosol outbreaks. - Geographic distribution: Northern hemisphere — North America (esp. south-central USA: Arkansas, Missouri, Oklahoma; and Martha's Vineyard for pneumonic), Scandinavia, Central/Eastern Europe, Turkey, Russia, Japan. - Re-emergence (2024–2025): rising incidence — Austria 117, Slovenia 38, France 150, Spain/Castile-León 206, and United States 220 cases in 2024 (Finding F012; PMID: 42641375). Germany reached a peak incidence in 2024 (PMID: 42601640). - Primary pneumonic tularemia is exceptionally rare — Martha's Vineyard is the site of the only two recognized U.S. primary pneumonic outbreaks (1978, 2000) (Finding F008; PMID: 17442781).

"Martha's Vineyard, Massachusetts, is the site of the only two recognized outbreaks of primary pneumonic tularemia in the United States" — PMID: 17442781

Demographics: - Sex ratio: Male predominance in many series (e.g., 65% male in Missouri, PMID: 22911645), reflecting occupational/recreational exposure; varies by outbreak. - Age: All ages; systemic/severe disease more common in older adults. - Population-specific variants: Type A (North America) vs. Type B (Eurasia) geographic segregation; type A-east most virulent.

Section 10 — Diagnostics

Serology (cornerstone): - Microagglutination test (MAT): titer ≥ 1/160 considered positive; seroconversion (≥4-fold rise) in paired sera is confirmatory (Finding F006; PMID: 22090310). Antibodies may take 2+ weeks to develop, limiting early diagnosis. - Serology is preferred because culture has low sensitivity and biosafety risk, and PCR lacks standardization (PMID: 20220165).

"Serology is still considered to be a cornerstone in tularemia diagnosis due to the low sensitivity of bacterial culture and the lack of standardization in PCR methodology" — PMID: 20220165

  • Rapid immunochromatographic test: 98.3% sensitivity, 96.5% specificity vs. MAT (PMID: 20220165).

Molecular (PCR): - Validated targets: ISFtu2 insertion element, tul4 (17-kDa), fopA (43-kDa), 23-kDa gene (PMID: 14662930). Multitarget real-time TaqMan assays improve detection in complex specimens. - PCR positive in ~91% of lymph node aspirates where cultures were negative (Finding F006).

Culture: Requires cysteine-enriched media; slow-growing; must be handled at BSL-3 due to aerosol infection risk. Low clinical sensitivity.

Imaging: Chest X-ray/CT show patchy infiltrates, hilar/mediastinal lymphadenopathy, pleural effusion, and occasionally cavitation (PMID: 24655540).

Laboratory: Nonspecific — elevated ESR/CRP; leukocytosis variable (more frequent in pediatric cases, PMID: 42343287).

Clinical criteria / differential diagnosis: Consider in patients with pneumonia unresponsive to β-lactams plus relevant exposure (landscaping, animal/tick contact, endemic area). Differential: community-acquired bacterial pneumonia, Q fever, psittacosis, plague, anthrax, Legionella, tuberculosis, fungal pneumonia. Tularemia is frequently initially misdiagnosed (PMID: 22911645).

Screening: No population screening; genetic/omics diagnostics not applicable.

Section 11 — Outcome / Prognosis

  • Untreated mortality: ~30% (Finding F012; PMID: 42641375); pneumonic form has the highest mortality of all clinical forms (Finding F001; PMID: 38461051).

"with the pneumonic presentation resulting in the greatest mortality" — PMID: 38461051

  • Treated case-fatality (all forms, 870-case systematic review): 0.7% (aminoglycosides), 0.9% (fluoroquinolones), 1.2% (tetracyclines) — overall low with prompt therapy (Finding F004; PMID: 38294108).
  • Complications: pleural effusion/empyema (may require drainage), cavitary pneumonia, respiratory failure, bacteremia/sepsis, rare myocarditis, necrotizing abscess formation.
  • Recovery: Excellent with early appropriate antibiotics; full recovery is typical. Prognosis worsens with delayed diagnosis, older age, type A-east strain, and high inhaled dose.
  • Prognostic factors: speed of antibiotic initiation, subspecies/strain (type A-east worst), inoculum size, host age/comorbidity.

Section 12 — Treatment

Pharmacotherapy (NCIT term suggestions in parentheses): - Aminoglycosides — streptomycin (NCIT:C839), gentamicin (NCIT:C575): historical treatment of choice; bactericidal (Finding F004; PMID: 29183485).

"Streptomycin and gentamicin are currently considered the treatment of choice for tularemia. Quinolone is an effective alternative drug." — PMID: 29183485

  • Fluoroquinolones — ciprofloxacin (NCIT:C405): effective oral alternative; in the 870-case review, pneumonic patients on ciprofloxacin had no fatalities and the lowest rates of pleural intervention. Oral ciprofloxacin successfully treated cavitary pneumonic disease after drainage (PMID: 24655540).
  • Tetracyclines — doxycycline (NCIT:C692): bacteriostatic; requires ≥14–21 day courses; higher relapse.
  • 2025 CDC guidelines now establish fluoroquinolones and doxycycline as first-line options (Finding F012; PMID: 42641375).

"The 2025 CDC guidelines establish fluoroquinolones and doxycycline as first-line therapeutic options" — PMID: 42641375

Post-exposure prophylaxis: streptomycin, gentamicin, doxycycline, or ciprofloxacin (PMID: 29183485).

Interventional/supportive: Pleural drainage for empyema/large effusion; oxygen/ventilatory support in respiratory failure; hydration and antipyretics. No patient isolation required for pneumonic tularemia (no person-to-person transmission) (PMID: 29183485).

Experimental/adjunctive: Post-exposure immunization with F. tularensis membrane protein fraction (MPF) augmented gentamicin efficacy (100% survival in a mouse pneumonic model) — proof of concept for immunotherapy adjuncts (PMID: 22652404).

Pharmacogenomics / targeted / gene / cell / RNA therapies: Not applicable.

Section 13 — Prevention

  • Primary prevention: Avoid aerosol-generating activities in endemic areas; use of masks/PPE during mowing/brush-cutting; safe handling of animal carcasses; tick/insect avoidance and repellents; laboratory biosafety (BSL-3 for cultures). Vector control and public health education.
  • Immunization: No licensed human vaccine (Finding F007; PMID: 38461051).

"there are no approved vaccines against F. tularensis in the United States" — PMID: 38461051

  • The unlicensed Live Vaccine Strain (LVS) protects mice against lethal intranasal/aerosol SCHU S4 challenge (CD4⁺ T-cell– and antibody/IgA-mediated) (PMID: 19211773).
  • Subunit/glycoconjugate candidates: glucan-particle LPS + FTT0814 subunit vaccine (partial protection, T-cell immunity in a rat aerosol model, PMID: 38713688); O-antigen glycoconjugate (PMID: 30872471); MPL-adjuvanted catanionic nanoparticle vaccine (PMID: 28077440). All remain experimental.
  • Secondary prevention: Prompt recognition and post-exposure prophylaxis after known aerosol exposure or lab accident.
  • Public health: Surveillance and reporting (nationally notifiable), water chlorination (for waterborne foci), rodent/lagomorph control.

Section 14 — Other Species / Natural Disease

  • Taxonomy of hosts (naturally infected): Lagomorphs — rabbits/hares (Sylvilagus, Lepus); rodents (voles, mice, muskrats, beavers); domestic cats (Felis catus, NCBI:9685) and dogs (Canis lupus familiaris, NCBI:9615). Ticks and deer flies are vectors/reservoirs.
  • Natural disease & veterinary relevance: Tularemia is an important zoonosis; cats can transmit to humans via bite/scratch (PMID: 30556330); domestic dogs facilitate transmission via retrieved carcasses and ticks. Lagomorphs suffer high mortality (epizootics).
  • Comparative pathology: Intracellular macrophage infection and phagosomal escape are conserved across mammalian hosts; mouse pathology closely mirrors human intracellular pathogenesis.
  • Zoonotic potential: High — a prototypical zoonosis. No human-to-human transmission, so pneumonic cases need no isolation.
  • Cross-species susceptibility: Broad mammalian host range; extreme susceptibility in mice and lagomorphs.

Section 15 — Model Organisms

  • Mouse (Mus musculus, NCBI:10090) — principal model. Intranasal/aerosol challenge with SCHU S4 (type A) or attenuated LVS; BALB/c and C57BL/6 strains widely used. Recapitulates pneumonic pathogenesis, intracellular replication, inflammasome activation, Th1 immunity, and treatment/vaccine responses (Findings F003, F005, F007, F010, F011).
  • Knockout models: Aim2⁻/⁻, Nlrp3⁻/⁻, Asc⁻/⁻, Casp1⁻/⁻, Tbx21⁻/⁻ (T-bet), Ifng⁻/⁻, Nos2⁻/⁻ — dissect inflammasome and Th1 pathways (PMID: 24421047, PMID: 21883803, PMID: 33875472, PMID: 34690967).
  • Rat (Rattus norvegicus, NCBI:10116): Fischer 344 inhalation model used for vaccine efficacy testing (PMID: 38713688).
  • In vitro / cellular: Bone-marrow-derived and cell-line macrophages (J774) to study phagosomal escape, cytosolic replication, and inflammasome activation (PMID: 32694562, PMID: 29482417).
  • F. novicida and F. tularensis LVS serve as reduced-biosafety surrogates for the virulent SCHU S4 strain.
  • Model limitations: F. novicida is attenuated in humans but hypervirulent in mice; LVS is attenuated relative to SCHU S4; mouse inflammasome biology and lipid A responses differ somewhat from humans, so protective correlates do not always translate (PMID: 41410566).

Upstream vs downstream: Bacterial invasion, phagosomal escape, and lipid-A-mediated TLR4 evasion are the upstream drivers that permit unchecked cytosolic replication. The AIM2 inflammasome, GBP/IRGB10 bacteriolysis, IFN-γ/iNOS, and Th1/CD4⁺ responses are downstream host-protective mechanisms; when they are outpaced (high dose, virulent type A-east, delayed antibiotics), tissue injury and death follow. Limitations and Knowledge Gaps

  1. Mechanistic data are largely from mouse/in vitro models using LVS or F. novicida surrogates, not virulent human type A SCHU S4 in humans. Translation to human pneumonic disease is inferred.
  2. Human pneumonic-specific datasets are sparse — most clinical series pool all tularemia forms; dedicated primary pneumonic cohorts are limited to rare outbreaks (Martha's Vineyard).
  3. No human transcriptomic/proteomic/metabolomic profiling of pneumonic tularemia patients was identified; omics diagnostics are not applicable/available.
  4. Correlates of protection remain undefined, hampering vaccine licensure; no licensed human vaccine exists.
  5. Fatality figures for antibiotics derive from all-form pooled reviews; pneumonic-only comparative RCTs are lacking (ethically/logistically constrained).
  6. Strain-virulence determinants (why type A-east > type B > type A-west) are incompletely mapped at the molecular level.
  7. 42641375 provides key re-emergence and 2025-guideline figures but is a single review source; primary CDC guideline documents should be cross-checked.

Proposed Follow-up Experiments / Actions

  1. Human correlates-of-protection study in vaccinated (LVS-exposed lab personnel) or convalescent cohorts — define antibody/T-cell signatures predicting protection to guide vaccine endpoints.
  2. Primary CDC 2025 guideline verification — obtain and cite the definitive CDC/clinical-guideline document for first-line fluoroquinolone/doxycycline recommendations rather than relying on a review.
  3. Comparative-effectiveness analysis of ciprofloxacin vs. gentamicin specifically in pneumonic cases, leveraging pooled outbreak registries, to formalize the observed lower pleural-intervention rate.
  4. Host single-cell transcriptomics of infected lung (mouse aerosol SCHU S4) to resolve alveolar-macrophage vs. DC vs. neutrophil trajectories and map AIM2/IFN-γ programs spatially.
  5. Molecular dissection of type A-east virulence via comparative genomics/transposon screens to identify determinants of the severity gradient (type A-east > type B > type A-west).
  6. Advance the glucan-particle/glycoconjugate subunit vaccines toward dose-optimized aerosol-challenge efficacy and, ultimately, human immunogenicity trials.
  7. Adjunct immunotherapy translation — evaluate membrane-protein-fraction post-exposure immunization plus antibiotics (PMID: 22652404) in higher-order models toward a therapeutic-adjunct strategy.
