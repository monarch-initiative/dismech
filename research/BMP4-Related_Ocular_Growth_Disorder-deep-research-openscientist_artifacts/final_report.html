BMP4-Related Ocular Growth Disorder (MONDO:0100613) — Comprehensive Disease Characterization

MONDO: MONDO:0100613 · Causal gene: BMP4 (Bone Morphogenetic Protein 4; HGNC:1071; NCBI Gene 652; chromosome 14q22.2) · OMIM: 607932 (Microphthalmia, syndromic 6) · Category: Mendelian (autosomal dominant)

Summary

BMP4-Related Ocular Growth Disorder (MONDO:0100613; corresponding to OMIM 607932, Microphthalmia, syndromic 6 [MCOPS6]) is a rare, congenital, autosomal-dominant developmental eye malformation caused by heterozygous loss-of-function (LOF) variants in, and 14q22.2 contiguous-gene deletions of, the gene BMP4 — which encodes Bone Morphogenetic Protein 4, a secreted morphogen of the TGF-β/BMP superfamily. The disease name is a serial descriptor; its causal gene is unambiguously BMP4, not any of the many other anophthalmia/microphthalmia genes (e.g., OTX2, SOX2, STRA6, SALL4, NAA10), which cause clinically overlapping but genetically distinct conditions.

The mechanistic core of the disorder is morphogen dosage sensitivity. BMP4 is the dorsal morphogen of the optic cup: the level of BMP4 signaling sets dorso-ventral patterning through a Tbx2/Tbx3/Tbx5-versus-Vax1/Vax2 gene expression system, operating within an integrated BMP4–Sonic hedgehog (Shh)–FGF8 signaling network and antagonized by Shh ventrally. When one BMP4 allele is lost, this dosage-dependent patterning and the growth of the eye are disrupted, producing microphthalmia/anophthalmia with coloboma, anterior-segment anomalies (sclerocornea, Axenfeld-Rieger-type malformation), retinal dystrophy, and high myopia. Because BMP4 is expressed in the optic vesicle, retina, lens, pituitary region, and developing digits, the disorder is pleiotropic, frequently accompanied by brain malformation, pituitary/hypopituitary anomalies, polydactyly/syndactyly, growth delay, skeletal defects, and — across the allelic spectrum — Stickler-syndrome-like and renal (CAKUT) features.

Clinically, the condition shows incomplete penetrance and highly variable expressivity, modified in part by the Hedgehog pathway (digenic "low-penetrance" BMP4 + HH-pathway cases are documented). There is no gene-specific or disease-modifying therapy; care is supportive, reconstructive (socket/orbital expansion, prosthetics, with good cosmetic outcomes), and multidisciplinary, anchored by genetic counseling. This report consolidates nine confirmed findings drawn from human clinical genetics, mouse/zebrafish/chick developmental models, and in vitro studies across 27 reviewed papers.

Key Findings

Finding 1 — BMP4 loss-of-function causes a syndromic microphthalmia/anophthalmia spectrum (MCOPS6)

The foundational human-genetic evidence comes from Reis et al. 2011 (PMID: 21340693), who screened 133 patients with ocular developmental disorders and identified disease-causing BMP4 lesions in several. These included 2 patients with BMP4 deletions (presenting with SHORT syndrome and anterior-segment anomalies) and 3 intragenic mutations:

  • a nonsense variant c.592C>T (p.R198X) in a patient with anophthalmia/microphthalmia with sclerocornea, right-sided diaphragmatic hernia, and hydrocephalus;
  • a frameshift c.171dupC (p.E58RfsX17) in half-siblings with anophthalmia/microphthalmia, polydactyly, and poor growth;
  • a missense c.362A>G (p.H121R).

The authors documented variable expressivity and non-penetrance — one variant was carried by an unaffected mother. In their words: "A patient with anophthalmia, microphthalmia with sclerocornea, right-sided diaphragmatic hernia, and hydrocephalus was found to have a c.592C >T (p.R198X) nonsense mutation in BMP4," and these results "significantly expand the number of reported loss-of-function mutations, further support the critical role of BMP4 in ocular development, and provide additional evidence of variable expression/non-penetrance of BMP4" (PMID: 21340693). This establishes the loss-of-function/haploinsufficiency mechanism that defines the disorder.

Finding 2 — BMP4 acts within an Lhx2-regulated network driving optic cup formation and lens induction

Yun et al. 2009 (PMID: 19906857) showed that in Lhx2-null mouse embryos, eye development arrests before optic cup formation. Of the signaling pathways examined, "only BMP signaling is noticeably altered and Bmp4 and Bmp7 mRNAs are undetectable." Supplying exogenous BMP to Lhx2-null optic vesicle/lens ectoderm restored expression of lens-inducing determinants (Pax2, Fgf15, Sox2). This places BMP4 downstream of Lhx2 and upstream of lens induction, mechanistically linking BMP4 loss to failed eye morphogenesis and explaining why BMP4 haploinsufficiency can produce an absent or severely reduced eye.

Finding 3 — BMP4 mutations cause overlapping eye–brain–digit anomalies, with a BMP4–Hedgehog interaction

The landmark gene-identification study, Bakrania et al. 2008 (PMID: 18252212), identified a frameshift c.226del2 (p.S76fs104X) segregating with anophthalmia-microphthalmia (AM), retinal dystrophy, myopia, brain anomalies, and polydactyly in a family, plus a non-conservative missense c.278A>G (p.E93G) in another. The authors reported that they "analyzed the BMP4 (Bone Morphogenetic Protein-4) gene and identified a frameshift mutation (c.226del2, p.S76fs104X) that segregated with AM, retinal dystrophy, myopia, brain anomalies, and polydactyly in a family and a nonconservative missense mutation (c.278A-->G, p.E93G)." In human embryos, they "demonstrate expression of BMP4 in optic vesicle, developing retina and lens, pituitary region, and digits strongly supporting BMP4 as a causative gene for AM, pituitary, and poly/syndactyly," providing the spatial basis for the disorder's pleiotropy. Critically, they also "identified four cases, some of whom had retinal dystrophy, with 'low-penetrant' mutations in both BMP4 and HH," supporting a digenic / modifier model in which the Hedgehog pathway tunes BMP4 penetrance. They also reported two new 14q22-q23 deletion AM cases (one with pituitary anomalies).

Finding 4 — 14q22 contiguous deletions and mouse Bmp4+/− recapitulate the eye + digit phenotype

Hayashi et al. 2008 (PMID: 18925664) reported "a 2.7-Mb heterozygous deletion at 14q22.1-q22.3 harboring 18 protein-coding genes. Among the genes, BMP4 was a candidate for the gene causing the abnormalities of both the eye and digits" in a girl with psychomotor retardation, bilateral congenital corneal opacity, and bilateral postaxial polysyndactyly of the feet. They noted that "Bmp4 heterozygous null mice revealed skeletal abnormalities including polydactyly and ocular anterior segment abnormalities." This provides two convergent lines of support: a contiguous-gene deletion mechanism in humans and a mouse haploinsufficiency model that recapitulates the core phenotypes. (GMFB within the deleted interval is a candidate for the psychomotor retardation, illustrating that deletion cases carry additional contiguous-gene contributions.)

Finding 5 — BMP4 is the dosage-sensitive dorsal morphogen of the optic cup

Behesti et al. 2006 (PMID: 17173667) demonstrated in mouse whole-embryo culture that "the level of BMP4 signaling is critical for the regulation of distinct Tbx2, Tbx3, Tbx5 and Vax2 gene expression domains along the dorso-ventral axis of the mouse optic cup." BMP4/Noggin beads shifted these domains and altered growth. This dosage-sensitivity is the direct mechanistic basis for haploinsufficiency: a 50% reduction in BMP4 is sufficient to mis-pattern and under-grow the eye.

This dorsal role is embedded in an integrated signaling system. Kobayashi et al. 2010 (PMID: 20298259) showed that "bone morphogenetic protein 4 (BMP4) and Sonic hedgehog (Shh) constitute a dorsal-ventral signaling system together with fibroblast growth factor 8 (FGF8)," specifying optic-vesicle polarity and governing ocular morphogenesis. Zhao et al. 2010 (PMID: 19854269) showed that "In the dorsal part of the developing retina, Bmp4 is expressed and antagonizes the ventralizing effects of Shh signaling through the activation of Tbx5 expression." In zebrafish, Gosse & Baier 2009 (PMID: 19164594) found Gdf6a(Radar) induces Bmp4/Tbx5/Tbx2b and suppresses Vax2 to establish dorsal retinal identity — demonstrating evolutionary conservation of the BMP4-driven dorsalization cascade. Pax6 also regulates the FGF8/BMP4 balance during optic-cup patterning (Kim & Lauderdale 2007, PMID: 17381541).

Finding 6 — BMP4 is pleiotropic across an allelic spectrum; the disorder is genetic/developmental with no major environmental trigger

BMP4 LOF produces phenotypes beyond the eye. Nixon et al. 2019 (PMID: 30568244) identified "A novel heterozygous BMP4 variant causing a premature stop codon, c.130G>T, p.(Gly44Ter), which segregated with clinical features of Stickler syndrome in multiple family members" — with associated renal dysplasia. Reis et al. 2011 (PMID: 21340693) reported this was "the first report indicating a role for BMP4 in SHORT syndrome, Axenfeld-Rieger malformation, growth delay, macrocephaly, and diaphragmatic hernia." BMP4 is also implicated in congenital anomalies of the kidney and urinary tract (CAKUT) via BMP/SMAD signaling (PMID: 29197384) and has been examined as a candidate (and excluded) in a syndromic cleft lip/oculodentodigital case (PMID: 22699666), reflecting its craniofacial/tooth roles. This is a Mendelian developmental disorder: no infectious agent and no established specific teratogen act on the BMP4 genotype itself. A EUROCAT study of Chernobyl radiological contamination (PMID: 3148908) found no general increase in anophthalmos/microphthalmos frequency, arguing against a broad environmental driver for the phenotype.

Finding 7 — Diagnosis relies on clinical/imaging identification plus genomic testing

Diagnosis combines clinical examination, ocular ultrasound/MRI (globe size, optic nerve, orbital cysts), and brain/pituitary MRI, followed by molecular confirmation. The Oculome 429-gene NGS panel (Patel et al. 2019, PMID: 30653986) includes a dedicated "microphthalmia-anophthalmia-coloboma (MAC; 86 genes)" subpanel covering BMP4. Chromosomal microarray (CMA) detects 14q22-q23 contiguous-gene deletions. Whole-genome sequencing adds yield: in 100 previously undiagnosed families with eye developmental disorders, WGS found (likely) pathogenic variants in 18 patients, and "fourteen of these variants would have been missed by conventional genes panels or CGH-array, underscoring the broader diagnostic scope of WGS" (PMID: 42448966). Even so, fewer than half of congenital eye-malformation patients receive a definitive molecular diagnosis.

Finding 8 — Management is supportive, reconstructive, and multidisciplinary

No disease-modifying therapy exists. McLean et al. 2003 (PMID: 12812886) studied 34 patients (40 orbits) with microphthalmos/anophthalmos and orbital cysts, using surgical cyst removal (42.5%), cyst retention with conformers (42.5%), silicone expanders, and staged approaches. They reported that "33 out of 34 patients had a good cosmetic result which illustrates that the orbital cyst in microphthalmos or anophthalmos performs a useful role in socket expansion and that the majority of patients with this condition can expect a good cosmetic outcome." Management of the newborn is a shared neonatologist–ophthalmologist responsibility (PMID: 40038803).

Finding 9 — Inheritance, epidemiology, and prognosis (consolidated)

The disorder is autosomal dominant, caused by heterozygous BMP4 LOF variants or 14q22.2 deletions, frequently de novo but transmitted in families, with incomplete penetrance (an unaffected obligate carrier is documented, PMID: 21340693) and highly variable expressivity, modified by the Hedgehog pathway (PMID: 18252212). BMP4 is a rare cause within the anophthalmia-microphthalmia (A/M) spectrum. Phenotype-level birth prevalence is "anophthalmia at 0.6-4.2 per 100,000 births and microphthalmia at 2-17 per 100,000 births, with a combined prevalence of up to 30 per 100,000" (PMID: 40038803), and "around 25% of patients have an underlying genetic syndrome that is diagnosable" (PMID: 22005280). Structural defects are congenital and non-progressive; life expectancy is usually normal unless severe syndromic complications (diaphragmatic hernia, major brain malformation, hypopituitarism) occur. The principal lifelong morbidity is congenital visual impairment/blindness, with good cosmetic outcomes from socket/orbital rehabilitation. No sex or ethnic predilection is established; pathogenic alleles are ultra-rare in gnomAD.

1. Disease Information

Overview. BMP4-Related Ocular Growth Disorder is a rare congenital developmental disorder of eye growth and patterning caused by reduced dosage of the BMP4 morphogen. It presents within the anophthalmia–microphthalmia–coloboma (A/M/C) spectrum, often syndromic with brain, pituitary, digit, growth, skeletal, and renal involvement.

Key identifiers. - MONDO: MONDO:0100613 - OMIM: 607932 (Microphthalmia, syndromic 6 / MCOPS6); BMP4 gene OMIM 112262 - Gene: BMP4 (HGNC:1071; NCBI Gene 652; chromosome 14q22.2) - Orphanet / ICD / MeSH: Indexed under A/M categories (ICD-10 Q11.x for anophthalmos/microphthalmos; MeSH "Microphthalmos," "Anophthalmos"). A BMP4-specific Orphanet code is not clearly recorded; best indexed via MONDO:0100613 / OMIM 607932. - Synonyms: Microphthalmia syndromic 6 (MCOPS6); BMP4-related microphthalmia/anophthalmia with extraocular anomalies.

Source of information. Derived from aggregated disease-level resources (OMIM, published case series, model-organism studies) rather than individual EHR data.

2. Etiology

  • Primary cause: Genetic — heterozygous BMP4 loss-of-function (nonsense, frameshift, splice, deleterious missense) variants and 14q22.2 contiguous-gene deletions (PMID: 21340693, PMID: 18252212, PMID: 18925664).
  • Genetic risk/modifier factors: Hedgehog-pathway variants act as digenic modifiers producing "low-penetrant" phenotypes (PMID: 18252212).
  • Environmental risk factors: None specifically established for the BMP4 genotype. Chernobyl radiological contamination did not raise A/M frequency (PMID: 3148908).
  • Protective factors: None established.
  • Gene–environment interactions: Not specifically characterized for BMP4; the dominant interaction is gene–gene (BMP4 × HH pathway).

4. Genetic / Molecular Information

  • Causal gene: BMP4 (OMIM 112262; HGNC:1071; 14q22.2).
  • Variant types: Nonsense (c.592C>T p.R198X; c.130G>T p.Gly44Ter), frameshift (c.171dupC p.E58RfsX17; c.226del2 p.S76fs104X), missense (c.362A>G p.H121R; c.278A>G p.E93G), and whole-gene/contiguous deletions (14q22.1–q22.3, ~2.7 Mb).
  • Classification: Truncating variants are pathogenic/likely pathogenic by ACMG criteria (PVS1-type for a haploinsufficient gene); missense variants require functional/segregation support.
  • Allele frequency: Pathogenic alleles are ultra-rare/absent in gnomAD.
  • Origin: Germline; both de novo and inherited.
  • Functional consequence: Loss of function / haploinsufficiency of a dosage-sensitive morphogen.
  • Modifier genes: Hedgehog-pathway genes (PMID: 18252212).
  • Epigenetics: Not specifically characterized for this disease.
  • Chromosomal abnormalities: 14q22.2/14q22-q23 deletions (detectable by CMA).

5. Environmental Information

No specific toxins, infectious agents, or lifestyle factors are established as causes of the BMP4 genotype or its penetrance. The condition is genetically determined. Population-level A/M surveillance did not detect an environmental (radiological) signal (PMID: 3148908).

6. Mechanism / Pathophysiology

Causal chain (initiating lesion → clinical manifestation):

  1. A heterozygous BMP4 LOF variant or 14q22.2 deletion results in ~50% reduction of functional BMP4 morphogen (haploinsufficiency). [demonstrated — human genetics]
  2. Reduced BMP4 leads to loss of the normal dorsal-high BMP4 signaling gradient across the optic vesicle/cup. [inferred from dosage experiments]
  3. Reduced dorsal BMP4 results in mis-patterning of dorso-ventral gene domains — downregulation of Tbx2/Tbx3/Tbx5 and failure to restrict Vax1/Vax2 — within the BMP4–Shh–FGF8 system. [demonstrated — mouse/zebrafish]
  4. Because BMP4 normally antagonizes ventralizing Shh via Tbx5 activation, its loss leads to ventralization and disturbed optic-cup polarity. [demonstrated — PMID: 19854269]
  5. Disrupted patterning, acting downstream of Lhx2, results in impaired optic-cup morphogenesis and defective lens induction (BMP4 required for Pax2/Fgf15/Sox2 induction). [demonstrated — PMID: 19906857]
  6. These failures lead to reduced eye growth and structural malformation → microphthalmia/anophthalmia, coloboma, anterior-segment and retinal anomalies. [clinical correlate]
  7. In parallel, loss of BMP4 in its other expression domains (pituitary region, digits, kidney, craniofacial/skeleton) results in extraocular anomalies — the branch producing brain/pituitary, polydactyly, renal (CAKUT), growth, and Stickler-like features. [demonstrated — expression + clinical]
  8. Hedgehog-pathway genetic background modulates steps 3–6, tuning penetrance/expressivity (digenic low-penetrant cases). [demonstrated — PMID: 18252212]

Pathway/process detail: - Molecular pathway: TGF-β/BMP–SMAD1/5/8 signaling; integrated with Shh and FGF8; upstream regulators Lhx2, Pax6, Gdf6a(Radar). - GO biological processes: eye development (GO:0001654), optic cup formation involved in camera-type eye development (GO:0003409), dorsal/ventral pattern formation (GO:0009953), BMP signaling pathway (GO:0030509), lens development in camera-type eye (GO:0002088). - Cell types (CL): retinal pigment epithelial cell (CL:0002586), neural retina progenitor cell, lens fiber/epithelial cells, optic-vesicle neuroepithelium. - Protein dysfunction: Haploinsufficiency (reduced secreted ligand), not aggregation or dominant-negative (though some missense alleles may act as hypomorphs).

7. Anatomical Structures Affected

  • Primary organ: Eye/globe (UBERON:0000970) — retina (UBERON:0000966), lens (UBERON:0000965), cornea (UBERON:0000964), anterior segment, optic nerve.
  • Secondary involvement: Brain (UBERON:0000955), pituitary gland (UBERON:0000007), autopod/digits (UBERON:0002544), kidney/urinary tract (UBERON:0002113), diaphragm (UBERON:0001103), skeleton.
  • Body systems: Visual, nervous, endocrine, musculoskeletal, renal/urinary.
  • Subcellular (GO CC): extracellular space/region (GO:0005615) for the secreted ligand; nucleus for downstream SMAD-mediated transcription.
  • Lateralization: Frequently bilateral, but can be unilateral/asymmetric.

8. Temporal Development

  • Onset: Congenital (prenatal, during optic-cup/lens morphogenesis in weeks 3–10 of human gestation).
  • Progression: Structural malformations are non-progressive/stable after birth; secondary complications (e.g., glaucoma, retinal detachment, amblyopia) can evolve.
  • Critical period: Early optic-vesicle/optic-cup stage — the window in which BMP4 dosage is decisive; there is no post-natal window to correct the primary malformation.
  • Duration: Lifelong.

9. Inheritance and Population

  • Inheritance: Autosomal dominant; de novo and familial; incomplete penetrance; variable expressivity.
  • Epidemiology: BMP4 is a rare cause within A/M; phenotype birth prevalence up to ~30/100,000 combined (PMID: 40038803); ~25% of A/M is syndromic/diagnosable (PMID: 22005280).
  • Penetrance/expressivity: Incomplete penetrance (unaffected obligate carrier reported); highly variable expressivity, modified by HH pathway.
  • Mosaicism/founder effects/consanguinity: Not specifically established; dominant mechanism makes consanguinity non-contributory.
  • Sex ratio / ethnicity: No established predilection.

10. Diagnostics

  • Clinical/imaging: Ophthalmologic exam; ocular ultrasound/MRI (globe size, optic nerve, orbital cysts); brain and pituitary MRI; renal ultrasound when indicated.
  • Genetic testing: MAC-focused NGS gene panels (e.g., Oculome 86-gene MAC subpanel, PMID: 30653986); single-gene BMP4 testing; chromosomal microarray for 14q22 deletions; WES/WGS, with WGS adding substantial yield for variants missed by panels/CGH-array (PMID: 42448966).
  • Differential diagnosis: Other A/M genes — OTX2 (note: an OTX2 microdeletion not involving BMP4 can mimic the syndrome, PMID: 30268123), SOX2, STRA6, SALL4 (acro-renal-ocular, PMID: 27661448), NAA10 (Lenz microphthalmia, PMID: 24431331), RAX, VSX2, MAB21L2 (PMID: 15385160).
  • Screening: Cascade testing of at-risk relatives; prenatal ultrasound can detect severe microphthalmia/anophthalmia.

11. Outcome / Prognosis

  • Survival/mortality: Generally normal life expectancy; mortality is driven by severe syndromic complications (diaphragmatic hernia, major brain malformation, hypopituitarism).
  • Morbidity/function: Principal lifelong burden is congenital visual impairment/blindness; additional neurodevelopmental, endocrine, renal morbidity in syndromic cases.
  • Recovery: Primary malformation is irreversible; reconstructive socket/orbital rehabilitation yields good cosmetic outcomes (33/34 patients, PMID: 12812886).
  • Prognostic factors: Laterality and severity of ocular defect; presence/severity of extraocular (brain, pituitary, diaphragmatic) involvement.

12. Treatment

No disease-modifying or gene-specific therapy exists. Management is supportive/reconstructive and multidisciplinary: - Surgical/interventional (NCIT ~ ocular prosthesis, orbital reconstruction): staged socket/orbital expansion with conformers, silicone expanders, and self-inflating expanders; orbital cyst management (removal vs retention), which can aid socket expansion (PMID: 12812886). - Supportive/rehabilitative: low-vision services, amblyopia management for the better eye, prosthetic fitting, endocrine replacement if hypopituitary, and management of renal/respiratory/neurodevelopmental complications. - Advanced/experimental therapeutics: None established; no gene, cell, RNA, or targeted therapy in clinical use for this gene. - Pharmacogenomics: Not applicable.

13. Prevention

  • Primary prevention: Not applicable (genetic/developmental).
  • Genetic counseling: Central — AD inheritance with incomplete penetrance and variable expressivity complicates recurrence-risk estimation; options include prenatal diagnosis and preimplantation genetic testing for known familial variants.
  • Secondary/tertiary prevention: Early multidisciplinary surveillance (ophthalmology, endocrinology, nephrology, developmental pediatrics) to manage complications and optimize visual/developmental outcomes.

14. Other Species / Natural Disease

  • Orthologs: Mouse Bmp4 (NCBI Gene 12159), zebrafish bmp4, chick BMP4.
  • Model phenotypes recapitulate human disease: Bmp4+/− mice show polydactyly and ocular anterior-segment abnormalities (PMID: 18925664). Conservation of the BMP4-dorsalization cascade is demonstrated across mouse, chick, and zebrafish.
  • Natural disease in companion animals/wildlife: Not specifically recorded for BMP4; no established zoonotic relevance (non-transmissible genetic disorder).

The unifying principle is morphogen dosage sensitivity: BMP4 must be present at the correct level, in the correct dorsal domain, at the correct developmental time. A single functional allele is insufficient to maintain the gradient that patterns and grows the eye, which is why haploinsufficiency (rather than dominant-negative misfolding) is the operative mechanism. The same dosage logic, applied across BMP4's multiple expression territories, explains the pleiotropy, and the modifier logic of the co-active Hedgehog pathway explains the incomplete penetrance and variable expressivity that make genetic counseling challenging.

Challenging / nuancing evidence: PMID: 30268123 shows that a neighboring OTX2 deletion not involving BMP4 can produce a nearly identical syndromic anophthalmia — underscoring the need to confirm BMP4 involvement rather than assuming it from phenotype or locus. PMID: 27661448 (SALL4, which regulates BMP4) and PMID: 24431331 (NAA10, dysregulating BMP4/STRA6) show other genes converge on BMP4/retinoic-acid signaling, reinforcing BMP4's network centrality while also representing diagnostic mimics.

Limitations and Knowledge Gaps

  1. No formal penetrance/expressivity estimates. Penetrance is "incomplete" based on anecdotal unaffected carriers; no cohort-based penetrance figure exists.
  2. BMP4-specific prevalence unknown. Only phenotype-level (A/M) prevalence is available; the fraction attributable to BMP4 is not quantified.
  3. Genotype–phenotype correlation is weak. It is unclear why some variants give isolated ocular disease and others severe multisystem syndromes; the relative contribution of HH-pathway modifiers versus stochastic developmental noise is unresolved.
  4. Missense variant interpretation. Functional assays distinguishing hypomorphic missense alleles from benign variants are limited; most confident calls are for truncating variants and deletions.
  5. No epigenetic or omics profiling specific to BMP4-related disease in patient tissue (eye tissue is rarely accessible).
  6. No therapeutic pipeline. No gene/cell/RNA therapy programs target this disorder; all management is downstream and supportive.
  7. Contiguous-deletion confounding. 14q22 deletion phenotypes reflect multiple genes (e.g., GMFB, OTX2), complicating attribution of specific features to BMP4.
