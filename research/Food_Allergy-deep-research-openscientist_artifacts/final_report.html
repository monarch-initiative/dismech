Food Allergy (MONDO:0700226) — Comprehensive Disease Characteristics Report

Summary

Food allergy is a common, complex, polygenic immune-mediated disease with no single causal gene. Unlike Mendelian disorders named after one locus, food allergy (MONDO:0700226) arises from the interaction of many susceptibility genes — primarily epithelial-barrier genes (FLG/filaggrin, the SERPINB clade B serpin cluster at 18q21.3) and type-2 immune genes (the HLA region [peanut-specific], the IL4/IL13 cytokine cluster at 5q31.1, and C11orf30/EMSY at 11q13.5) — with a large environmental/exposome contribution. Because the research template requests a "causal gene" but food allergy is categorized as Complex, the correct answer is that susceptibility is distributed polygenically; the most robustly replicated genome-wide significant loci are FLG, the SERPINB cluster, HLA, the 5q31.1 cytokine cluster, and C11orf30/EMSY (PMID: 29051540, PMID: 29030101, PMID: 39698764).

The classic IgE-mediated form of the disease results from a failure of oral tolerance. Allergen crosses a compromised epithelial barrier, pathogenic Th2 lymphocytes drive B-cell class-switching to allergen-specific IgE, IgE arms mast cells and basophils via FcεRI, and on re-exposure the cross-linking of surface IgE triggers explosive degranulation — releasing histamine and proteases that produce urticaria, angioedema, gastrointestinal, respiratory and cardiovascular symptoms, and potentially fatal anaphylaxis. Food allergy affects as many as 8% of young children and 2% of adults in Western countries and its prevalence is rising. The disease spans IgE-mediated, non-IgE-mediated (e.g., FPIES, allergic proctocolitis), and mixed (eosinophilic esophagitis) subtypes, and it imposes a heavy psychosocial and quality-of-life burden on patients and caregivers.

Management rests on allergen avoidance plus emergency intramuscular adrenaline (epinephrine) for acute reactions, with two disease-modifying strategies now established: oral immunotherapy (OIT) and omalizumab (anti-IgE, now FDA-approved for food allergy). The single most important advance in prevention is the paradigm shift demonstrated by the LEAP and EAT trials: early oral introduction of allergenic foods in infancy prevents food allergy, reversing decades of avoidance-based advice. This report synthesizes 14 confirmed findings across all 15 requested sections, grounded in 58 reviewed papers.

Key Findings

Finding 1 — Food allergy is polygenic: barrier genes + type-2 immune genes

Food allergy has no single Mendelian cause. A genome-wide association study of oral-food-challenge–diagnosed food allergy (497 cases / 2,387 controls) identified five loci at genome-wide significance: the clade B serpin (SERPINB) gene cluster at 18q21.3, the cytokine gene cluster at 5q31.1 (which includes IL4/IL13), the filaggrin gene (FLG), the C11orf30/LRRC32 locus, and the HLA region. Notably the HLA association is peanut-specific (PMID: 29051540):

"We identify five loci at genome-wide significance, the clade B serpin (SERPINB) gene cluster at 18q21.3, the cytokine gene cluster at 5q31.1, the filaggrin gene, the C11orf30/LRRC32 locus, and the human leukocyte antigen (HLA) region."

A multi-population meta-analysis established C11orf30/EMSY as a genetic risk factor for food allergy generally and found ITGA6 near genome-wide significance for peanut allergy; several implicated genes (C11orf30/EMSY, SKAP1, CTNNA3) are involved in epigenetic regulation of gene expression (PMID: 29030101). A 2025 review consolidates the picture: 16 genome-wide significant variants and 32 epigenome-wide significant loci, with implicated genes falling into two functional groups — epithelial barrier function (FLG, SERPINB7) and immune function (HLA, IL4) (PMID: 39698764):

"Identified genes for food allergy are mainly involved in epithelial barrier function (e.g., FLG, SERPINB7) and immune function (e.g., HLA, IL4)."

Gene/ontology annotations: FLG (HGNC:3748), IL4 (HGNC:6014), IL13 (HGNC:5973), HLA-DQ/DR region, SERPINB7 (HGNC:8951), C11orf30/EMSY (HGNC:18071). Inheritance is multifactorial/polygenic (MONDO category: Complex). Evidence source: human GWAS.

Finding 2 — Early oral introduction prevents food allergy (LEAP/EAT): a primary-prevention paradigm shift

The LEAP randomized controlled trial (n=640 high-risk infants) demonstrated that early, sustained peanut consumption from infancy to age 5 prevents peanut allergy, and the protective effect persists to 12 years of age (144 months): peanut allergy was 15.4% in the original avoidance group vs 4.4% in the consumption group (P<0.001) (PMID: 38804779):

"At age 144 months, peanut allergy remained significantly more prevalent in participants in the original peanut avoidance group than in the original peanut consumption group (15.4% [38 of 246 participants] vs. 4.4% [11 of 251 participants]; P<0.001)."

A pooled intention-to-treat analysis of LEAP + EAT showed a 75% reduction in peanut allergy (p<.0001) among children randomized to early peanut consumption, with earlier introduction more effective (PMID: 36435990). Modeling estimated that a 77% population-level reduction in peanut allergy could be achieved by introducing peanut at 4 months (with eczema) / 6 months (without eczema), with the benefit diminishing for each month of delay (PMID: 36521802). Meta-analyses provide high-certainty evidence for peanut and egg (PMID: 41967460).

Finding 3 — Pathophysiology: IgE-mediated type-2 immune cascade with mast-cell degranulation

Experimental food allergy demonstrates the causal chain. Allergen is taken up across the GI epithelium (facilitated when IgE–antigen complexes bind CD23 on epithelial cells) → pathogenic Th2 lymphocytes are activated (IL-4, IL-5, IL-13) → B cells class-switch to allergen-specific IgE → IgE binds FcεRI on mast cells and basophils → re-exposure cross-links surface IgE → mast-cell/basophil degranulation (histamine, mast cell protease MMCP-1) → anaphylaxis and local GI manifestations (PMID: 19079331):

"antigen uptake can be facilitated by immunoglobulin-E (IgE)-antigen complexes binding to CD23 on the epithelial cell surface. Studies on systemic anaphylaxis or local gastrointestinal manifestations of food allergy in mice have highlighted the contribution of IgE, mast cells, and pathogenic Th2 lymphocytes in experimental food allergy."

In mouse models, ovalbumin-specific serum IgE correlates with jejunal MMCP-1+ mast-cell density and predicts diarrhea, accompanied by increased IL-4/IL-6 (PMID: 19450258). Mast-cell-deficient W/Wv mice and IL-4/IL-10 neutralization studies confirm the dependence of the response on mast cells and Th2 cytokines (PMID: 12951468, PMID: 9003216). Evidence source: in vitro + model organism + human.

Finding 4 — Treatment: avoidance + epinephrine; OIT and omalizumab are disease-modifying

Intramuscular adrenaline (epinephrine) is the only life-saving treatment for food-induced anaphylaxis but is frequently delayed or underused; patients should carry autoinjectors (PMID: 40719286, PMID: 42262574):

"Intramuscular adrenaline continues to be the primary treatment, yet its administration is often delayed or insufficiently used."

Oral immunotherapy (OIT) achieves desensitization (e.g., sesame OIT ~85–97%) with mostly mild-to-moderate adverse events (PMID: 42808451); earlier initiation of cow's-milk OIT (<24 months) is associated with fewer severe reactions and no maintenance-phase anaphylaxis (PMID: 41616970). Omalizumab (anti-IgE monoclonal) raises reaction thresholds and improves OIT tolerability; in the OUtMATCH trial, post-omalizumab dietary consumption success reached 61–70% for milk/egg/wheat vs 38–56% for peanut/tree nuts (PMID: 40447195):

"greater success was observed for milk, egg, or wheat (61%-70%) than for peanut or tree nuts (38%-56%)"

Omalizumab monotherapy is now FDA-approved for food allergy.

NCIT treatment terms: Epinephrine (NCIT:C377), Omalizumab (NCIT:C2175), immunotherapy (NCIT:C15299), antihistamine (NCIT:C265), corticosteroid (NCIT:C2271). Evidence source: human clinical trials + reviews.

Finding 5 — Substantial psychosocial burden and reduced quality of life

Food allergy imposes measurable psychological and quality-of-life costs on patients and caregivers. Mothers of children with food allergy had significantly higher state anxiety (STAI-S, P<0.001) and caregiver burden (Zarit, P<0.001) and lower quality of life (EUROHIS-QOL, P=0.009) than controls; cow's-milk protein allergy specifically increased trait anxiety (P=0.035) (PMID: 40923420):

"Mothers in the FA group had significantly higher state anxiety (STAI-S) (P < 0.001) and ZCBS scores (P < 0.001) compared to controls."

In a national US sample (N=5,940 food-allergy households), 69.9% screened at-risk for food insecurity, and FA-related anxiety was the most common psychosocial experience (25.4%/30.1% of children/adults), higher among food-insecure households (p<.0001) (PMID: 38288585):

"FA-related anxiety was the most common (25.4%/30.1% of children/adults)"

Disease-specific instruments include the Food Allergy Quality of Life Questionnaire (FAQLQ) and Food Allergy Independent Measure (FAIM) (PMID: 37695230, PMID: 38288585). Evidence source: human cross-sectional/survey.

Finding 6 — Gut dysbiosis and reduced short-chain fatty acids: the gene-environment axis

Reduced gut bacterial diversity and abundance are epidemiologically associated with food allergy. Mechanistically, microbial short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs) steer CD4 T-cell differentiation toward anergic regulatory T cells (Treg, Foxp3+) versus allergic Th2 responses, acting through GPR41/GPR43/GPR109a receptors and histone-deacetylase inhibition (PMID: 39571752):

"T-cell differentiation poses an opportunity to direct the immune cells towards an anergic regulatory T cell (Treg) or allergic T helper 2 (Th2) response."

Early-life microbial exposures — particularly rural/farming environments — may protect against food allergy by stimulating innate immune pathways and shaping the gut microbiome, while air pollutants (PM, NO2), synthetic barrier-disrupting chemicals, and ultra-processed foods increase risk (PMID: 42670723):

"Early-life microbial exposures, especially those associated with rural and farming environments, may protect against food allergies by stimulating innate immune pathways and shaping the gut microbiome."

Probiotic evidence is inconclusive overall but more promising in cow's-milk protein allergy (PMID: 39571752). CHEBI: short-chain fatty acid (CHEBI:26666), butyrate (CHEBI:17968). CL: CL:0000815 (regulatory T cell). Evidence source: human epidemiology + mechanistic review.

Finding 7 — Epidemiology: ~8% of children / ~2% of adults, rising; three immunologic subtypes

Food-allergic conditions affect as many as 8% of young children and 2% of adults in Western countries, with rising prevalence (PMID: 39257598):

"Food allergic (FA) conditions have been classified as immunoglobulin E (IgE) and non-IgE-mediated reactions that affect as many as 8% of young children and 2% of adults in Western countries, and their prevalence seems to be rising."

A systematic review/meta-analysis of US pediatric data showed prevalence rose from 5.6% (2012) to 6.6% (2018) — a 1.6 percentage-point increase per decade (95% CI 0.5–2.7) — with stabilization between 2021 (7.9%) and 2024 (7.2%); the largest increase was among 10–17-year-olds, with no difference by race/ethnicity (PMID: 42887634):

"FA increased from 5.6% in 2012 to 6.6% in 2018 (1.6 percentage-point increase per decade; 95% CI: 0.5-2.7), with stabilization between 2021 (7.9%) and 2024 (7.2%)."

Classification spans three types: IgE-mediated FA (Th2/IgE basis), non-IgE-mediated FA (e.g., FPIES, food protein-induced allergic proctocolitis), and mixed IgE/non-IgE (eosinophilic esophagitis). Regional triggers differ: peanuts/tree nuts predominate in Western countries, hen's egg/cow's milk in East Asia, seafood in Southeast Asia (PMID: 40719286). Evidence source: human epidemiology.

Finding 8 — Prognosis: usually non-fatal; rare fatal anaphylaxis with identifiable risk factors

Anaphylaxis case-fatality is estimated at 0.65–2% in children; food is the main trigger (cow's milk, peanuts, tree nuts); mucocutaneous signs occur in >90% of cases and death is usually secondary to laryngeal edema (seen in 40–50%). Risk factors for severe/fatal reactions include personal asthma history, peanut/tree-nut allergy, and adolescence (PMID: 33236416):

"Anaphylaxis fatality rate is estimated to be 0.65% to 2%. Food is the main anaphylaxis trigger in children, notably cow's milk, peanuts, and tree nuts. Mucocutaneous manifestations are observed in more than 90% of cases"

A systematic review of 32 studies found that young adults with asthma and known peanut/tree-nut allergy are at higher risk of fatal anaphylaxis; delayed adrenaline injection is associated with fatal outcomes, though timely adrenaline alone may be insufficient; cofactors include alcohol, exercise, medications, and mast cell disorders (PMID: 30288817). Many IgE-mediated childhood allergies (milk, egg) resolve with age, whereas peanut/tree-nut/seafood allergies more often persist; SPT and sIgE thresholds imperfectly predict resolution (PMID: 24132133). Evidence source: human epidemiology + systematic review.

Finding 9 — Animal models: adjuvant-based and adjuvant-free (epicutaneous) mouse models

Mouse models (e.g., Balb/c, C3H/HeJ) sensitized to ovalbumin or peanut — orally with mucosal adjuvants such as cholera toxin, or epicutaneously (adjuvant-free) — then orally challenged, reproduce allergen-specific IgE, intestinal mast-cell activation (MMCP-1), Th2 cytokines, and systemic anaphylaxis (PMID: 19079331, PMID: 19450258). Mast-cell-deficient W/Wv mice and IL-4/IL-10 neutralization establish mast-cell and Th2-cytokine dependence (PMID: 12951468, PMID: 9003216). In C3H/HeJ mice, epicutaneous immunotherapy (OVA-Viaskin patches) induced sustained protection against anaphylaxis via gastrointestinal-homing LAP+/Treg cells, demonstrating skin–gut immune crosstalk and a tissue-specific GI Treg deficiency in allergic mice (PMID: 27417020):

"epicutaneous application of antigen generated a population of gastrointestinal-homing LAP"

Phenotype recapitulation: good for IgE, mast-cell activation, Th2 cytokines, and anaphylaxis; limitations: adjuvants are artificial, and murine oral tolerance differs from human timing. NCBI Taxon: Mus musculus (10090). Evidence source: model organism.

Finding 10 — Natural (spontaneous) food allergy in companion animals

Adverse food reactions occur naturally in dogs and cats, affecting mainly the integument and GI tract. In dogs, pruritus of distal limbs/face/ears/ventrum predominates, presenting as recurrent bacterial skin infections, otitis externa, and atopic dermatitis; four breeds (German shepherds, West Highland white terriers, Labrador and golden retrievers) account for ~40% of affected dogs, with onset by 6–12 months in 22–38% (PMID: 31072328):

"Four breeds (German shepherd dogs, West Highland white terriers, Labrador and golden retrievers) accounted for about 40% of affected dogs. Most dogs diagnosed with a CAFR were pruritic"

Non-cutaneous signs include GI (diarrhea, vomiting), conjunctivitis, and even anaphylaxis (PMID: 30419909). As in humans, elimination diet followed by oral food challenge is the diagnostic gold standard (PMID: 29871756, PMID: 32448251):

"An elimination diet over several weeks using a protein source and a carbohydrate source previously not fed is still the diagnostic tool of choice."

NCBI Taxon: Canis lupus familiaris (9615), Felis catus (9685). Evidence source: veterinary clinical.

Finding 11 — Phenotype spectrum: multi-system disease, urticaria/angioedema most frequent

Food allergy is a multi-system disease. In an anaphylaxis cohort, organ involvement was cutaneous 95.7%, cardiovascular 76.8%, respiratory 74.6%; the most common manifestations were urticaria (81.9%), dyspnea (71.3%), and angioedema (69.4%); 2.2% had biphasic reactions (PMID: 18254479):

"The organs most frequently involved in the anaphylaxis were cutaneous (95.7%), cardiovascular (76.8%), and respiratory (74.6%). The most common manifestations were dyspnea (71.3%), urticaria (81.9%), and angioedema (69.4%)."

In childhood food allergy the most common skin manifestations were angioedema (48%), urticaria (31%), and atopic dermatitis (21%) (PMID: 9252873). Across food anaphylaxis, mucocutaneous signs occur in >90% and laryngeal edema in 40–50% (PMID: 33236416).

Finding 12 — Epigenetics: DNA-methylation signatures distinguish clinical allergy from sensitization

A machine-learning study on DNA-methylation (DNAm) data narrowed 405,000+ CpGs to an 18-CpG signature mapping to 13 genes (many immune-associated; 7 previously linked to food allergy) that achieved high classification accuracy distinguishing food-allergic from merely sensitized individuals — addressing the <50% accuracy of standard tests and the reliance on oral food challenges (PMID: 31216310):

"The 18-CpG signature mapped to 13 genes, on which biological insights were collected. Notably, many of the FA-discriminating genes found in this study were strongly associated with the immune system, and seven of the 13 genes were previously associated with FA."

A 2025 review reports 32 epigenome-wide significant loci in food allergy (PMID: 39698764). This positions epigenetic marks as both a mechanism and a candidate diagnostic. Evidence source: human computational/epigenomics.

Finding 13 — Alpha-gal syndrome: a tick-bite-induced, delayed, adult-onset red-meat allergy

Alpha-gal syndrome (AGS) is a delayed IgE-mediated food allergy to galactose-α-1,3-galactose (α-Gal), a carbohydrate epitope ubiquitously expressed in non-primate mammalian tissues. Sensitization follows tick bites and produces delayed (often 2–6 h) reactions to red meat and mammalian products (PMID: 42571940, PMID: 42808634):

"α-Gal has emerged as the causative antigen of alpha-gal syndrome (AGS), a delayed IgE-mediated food allergy to mammalian-derived products, including red meat."

"Alpha-gal syndrome (AGS) is an emerging red meat allergy, IgE-mediated, with delayed reactions to a carbohydrate derived from mammals: α-gal (galactose-α-1,3-galactose). Sensitization follows a tick bite."

α-Gal epitopes are thermostable, so conventional cooking may not reduce allergenicity, and AGS epidemiology is shaped by tick distribution with likely under-diagnosis (PMID: 42571940). Sensitized individuals show elevated innate-immune markers and reduced regulatory cytokines (IL-10, IL-35) (PMID: 42877672). AGS is an important example of an environmentally triggered, carbohydrate-directed, adult-onset food allergy that departs from the classic protein-sensitization, childhood-onset paradigm. CHEBI: galactose-α-1,3-galactose. Evidence source: human clinical/review.

Finding 14 — Food allergy–eosinophilic esophagitis continuum: antigen exposure shapes immune phenotype

IgE-mediated food allergy and eosinophilic esophagitis (EoE) are interconnected manifestations on a continuum determined by antigen exposure patterns, dose, and chronicity. Among children with IgE-mediated food allergy, EoE prevalence is ~4.7% (nearly 100-fold higher than the general population), and during oral immunotherapy confirmed EoE develops in 1–10% of participants. Mechanistically, antigen avoidance favors IgE-mediated responses via T follicular helper cells, whereas sustained exposure promotes a shift toward eosinophilic/EoE-type inflammation (PMID: 42575621):

"Among children with IgE-mediated food allergy, EoE prevalence is nearly 100-fold higher than the general population at 4.7%. During oral immunotherapy, gastrointestinal symptoms are common, with confirmed EoE developing in 1% to 10% of participants."

This finding clarifies a key safety consideration for OIT and links the IgE-mediated and mixed subtypes mechanistically. CL: CL:0000771 (eosinophil); UBERON: UBERON:0001043 (esophagus). Evidence source: human clinical/review.

Diagnostics (cross-cutting). The diagnostic standard is oral food challenge (OFC) — skin prick tests and serum-specific IgE are sensitive but poorly specific, frequently identifying clinically irrelevant sensitization (PMID: 42358594, PMID: 42545935). Component-resolved diagnostics (e.g., Ara h 2 for peanut, casein for milk, ovomucoid for egg) improve risk stratification; casein-specific IgE ≥2.55 kUA/L predicted milk-OFC positivity with AUC 0.878 (PMID: 42451187). Emerging functional assays include the basophil activation test (BAT) and mast cell activation test (MAT) (PMID: 42358594). Food-specific IgG testing is not a validated diagnostic for IgE-mediated allergy (PMID: 42818614). Note that skin prick testing itself can rarely provoke generalized reactions in infants (PMID: 11152165).

Key identifiers. MONDO:0700226; MeSH D005512 (Food Hypersensitivity); ICD-10 T78.0–T78.1 / Z91.01–Z91.018; ICD-11 4A85. Synonyms: IgE-mediated food allergy, food hypersensitivity, allergic disease from food material. Information is derived from aggregated disease-level resources (GWAS cohorts, registries, RCTs, reviews) rather than a single EHR patient series.

Limitations and Knowledge Gaps

  1. No single causal gene. The research template field "causal gene(s): not recorded" is correct — food allergy is polygenic. GWAS to date explain only a modest fraction of heritability; effect sizes at individual loci are small, and most loci are regulatory rather than coding. Penetrance of susceptibility alleles is low and strongly environment-dependent.
  2. Mechanistic evidence is weighted toward murine models. The core IgE/mast-cell/Th2 cascade (F3, F9) is best demonstrated in mice using artificial adjuvants; the exact triggers of human sensitization and the quantitative role of SCFA/microbiome in humans remain inferential rather than proven causally.
  3. Diagnostic imprecision. SPT and sIgE have low specificity; OFC — the gold standard — is resource-intensive and carries reaction risk. Epigenetic (DNAm) and functional (BAT/MAT) biomarkers are promising but not yet in routine clinical use.
  4. Subtype heterogeneity. Non-IgE-mediated forms (FPIES, proctocolitis) and the mixed EoE phenotype are mechanistically distinct and less well characterized than classic IgE-mediated allergy; this report is weighted toward IgE-mediated disease.
  5. Prevention generalizability. High-certainty early-introduction evidence exists for peanut and egg; data for other allergens (milk, tree nuts, seafood) are weaker, and optimal dose/duration are undefined.
  6. Treatment durability. OIT achieves desensitization but sustained unresponsiveness (true tolerance after stopping) is less certain; omalizumab requires ongoing administration. Long-term outcomes of combination strategies are still emerging.
  7. Epidemiologic measurement. Much prevalence data is self-reported, likely over-estimating true (OFC-confirmed) food allergy; regional/ethnic variation is incompletely mapped.
