Martland noted these findings and proposed a connection between these cerebral microhaemorrhages and over-punished boxers who experienced neurological symptoms after suffering repetitive head trauma.

Martland noted that when boxers visited ‘eminent physicians,’ they were often turned away after being told that there is essentially no such syndromic association between boxing and the patients’ symptoms.

Dr Harry Parker built on Martland’s thesis and presented more evidence of its existence, noting ‘punch drunkenness’ can present as a ‘medley’ of symptoms through different symptomatic timecourses (Parker, 1934). J. A. Millspaugh, a naval lieutenant, published an article in 1937 that presented more examples of cognitive dysfunction in naval boxers who suffered from dementia and disorientation (Millspaugh, 1937). Millspaugh also introduced new nomenclature to the literature, re-terming ‘punch drunk syndrome’ as dementia pugilistica, which is still used today.

Martland’s paper documented 23 examples of boxers (five of which he examined himself) to piece together the classic clinical presentation of ‘punch drunk’ boxers.
