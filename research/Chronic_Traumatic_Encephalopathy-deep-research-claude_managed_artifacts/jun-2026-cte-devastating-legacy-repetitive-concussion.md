## Text

Abstract:

Repetitive concussive and subconcussive traumatic brain injury (TBI) is increasingly linked to chronic traumatic encephalopathy (CTE), yet a central challenge remains in connecting exposure to long-term neurodegeneration through a coherent mechanistic framework. Here, we synthesize evidence across epidemiology, neuropathology, and clinical studies to define the continuum from repetitive injury to disease. Primary injury initiates secondary cascades, including mitochondrial dysfunction, metabolic stress, neuroinflammation, and axonal injury across neuronal, glial, and vascular compartments, which, over time, promote protein misfolding and progressive pathology involving tau, amyloid precursor protein (APP), and TDP-43. CTE is defined by a distinct pattern of perivascular hyperphosphorylated tau accumulation at the depths of cortical sulci, linking injury-associated biomechanical strain and vascular vulnerability to spatially localized disease progression. These pathological processes give rise to heterogeneous clinical features that are only partially captured by current diagnostic frameworks and emerging imaging and fluid biomarkers, which remain limited in specificity. Experimental models, including in vivo systems and human 3D in vitro platforms, provide complementary insight into specific aspects of CTE pathobiology, but no single model fully recapitulates the disease trajectory. Together, this synthesis reframes CTE as a mechanistically linked continuum from exposure to neurodegeneration, highlights key gaps in diagnosis and modeling, and identifies priorities for advancing in-life detection and therapeutic development.

Genetics-related paragraphs (verbatim from PMC13499041 full text):

Pathological studies demonstrate that repetitive mild trauma, even without a clinically recognized concussion, is associated with the neuropathology of CTE. Key features include the accumulation of hyperphosphorylated tau (p-tau), TDP-43 pathology, and genetic associations such as variants in transmembrane protein 106B [TMEM106B] (Stern et al., 2011) (Fig. 1C). CTE is defined neuropathologically by p-tau aggregates in neurons, astrocytes, and perivascular regions at the depths of cortical sulci. While tau pathology also characterizes other disorders such as AD and frontotemporal lobar degeneration (FTLD), CTE is distinct (McKee et al., 2023; Buckland et al., 2022). Cryo-electron microscopy has revealed that p-tau filaments in CTE exhibit distinct structural differences from those in AD, underscoring its classification as a unique tauopathy (McKee et al., 2023). Furthermore, the distribution of tau pathology is different: in CTE, aggregates localize primarily to cortical layers II/III and extend broadly across hippocampal subfields, whereas in AD (Katsumoto et al., 2019), they are concentrated in layers IV/V and largely confined to cornu ammonis 1 [CA1] (Turner et al., 2016).

Symptoms of CTE typically appear in midlife, often many years after the period of active exposure. In a large series, McKee and colleagues reported a mean age of onset of 44.3 years (range 16–83, n = 119), with symptoms beginning on average 14.5 years after retirement from athletic or military service (n = 104) (Stein et al., 2015; Stern et al., 2011). Importantly, the clinical syndrome differs from the prolonged effects of a single concussion or sub-concussive injury. Rather than representing a continuation of acute symptoms, CTE reflects progressive molecular and epigenetic alterations that accumulate over years, ultimately disrupting neuronal and glial function and giving rise to distinct clinical phenotypes (McKee et al., 2009). The clinical picture of CTE is broad and heterogeneous. Early features often include mood and behavioral disturbances such as irritability, poor judgment, risk-taking, and depression (Stern et al., 2011; Turner et al., 2016) (Fig. 2A). As the disease progresses, cognitive impairments emerge, with deficits in memory, attention, and executive function being most common (Stern et al., 2013). Motor disturbances, including gait instability, speech difficulties, and Parkinsonian features, may develop in later stages (Stein et al., 2015; Montenigro et al., 2015). These symptom domains likely reflect the regional progression of underlying pathology, with early mood and behavioral changes linked to frontal and limbic circuit involvement, followed by cognitive decline associated with broader cortical dysfunction, and later motor features corresponding to subcortical and brainstem involvement. Sleep disruption and neuropsychiatric symptoms frequently contribute to the overall burden (Stern et al., 2013; Stern et al., 2011). In older patients, CTE can coexist with other neurodegenerative diseases, including Lewy body disease (LBD) and amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS), further complicating diagnosis and clinical management (McKee et al., 2013; McKee et al., 2015).

Cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) offers a more direct link to the brain environment. The recent discovery of the glymphatic system has advanced understanding of how CSF and interstitial fluid (ISF) clear metabolites, including tau and amyloid-β, from the brain parenchyma through perivascular pathways regulated by astroglial aquaporin-4 (AQP4) water channels. TBI disrupts this clearance system: experimental evidence shows that glymphatic pathway function is reduced by approximately 60% following TBI, and this impairment persists for more than one month post-injury due to the loss of perivascular AQP4 polarization in reactive astrocytes (Iliff et al., 2014). Importantly, extracellular tau is normally cleared from the brain along these paravascular pathways, and genetic disruption of AQP4 following TBI exacerbates tau aggregation and promotes neurofibrillary pathology, providing a direct mechanistic link between post-traumatic glymphatic failure and the perivascular p-tau accumulation that defines CTE (Iliff et al., 2014). This evidence positions chronic glymphatic dysfunction as a key intermediary in the pathobiological chain linking repetitive head trauma to the hallmark perivascular tau lesions of CTE, and suggests that strategies to restore glymphatic function, such as sleep optimization or AQP4-targeted therapies, may offer a novel therapeutic avenue. CSF biomarker collection via lumbar puncture, while invasive and not always well tolerated, captures a downstream readout of this impaired clearance system. As a result, CSF biomarkers may more closely reflect central pathological processes than blood-based measures, particularly those related to impaired protein clearance and accumulation. Although many candidate CSF biomarkers have been studied, none has shown unique specificity for CTE. However, like blood-based markers, most CSF biomarkers lack disease specificity and overlap with profiles observed in other taupathies and neurodegenerative conditions. Recently, exosomal tau has attracted particular attention. Exosomes are vesicles secreted by neurons and glia that carry molecular cargo reflective of the central nervous system. Measuring tau in exosomes isolated from blood may overcome the challenge of low tau concentrations in plasma and provide a more accurate representation of underlying pathology, representing a promising but still emerging direction for CTE biomarker development, requiring further validation for specificity and reproducibility (Halicki et al., 2023). Taken together, fluid biomarkers provide accessible and potentially longitudinal measures of brain injury and pathology, but currently lack the specificity required to distinguish CTE from other neurodegenerative conditions, highlighting the need for multimodal integration with imaging and clinical data.

Despite increasing recognition of chronic traumatic encephalopathy (CTE), its genetic contributions remain poorly defined and are thought to act primarily as modifiers of disease susceptibility and progression rather than direct causal drivers. While strong genetic associations exist for other neurodegenerative disorders, including Huntington's disease, Alzheimer's disease (AD), Parkinson's disease (PD), amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS), and multiple sclerosis (MS), the influence of genetic background on CTE is still unclear. This raises a central question: what role does genetic susceptibility play in determining who develops CTE after repetitive head trauma? Current evidence suggests that genetic factors influence how individuals respond to repetitive injury, shaping vulnerability to neurodegeneration through pathways involving lipid metabolism, protein aggregation, immune activation, and cellular repair.

A small set of candidate genes has been repeatedly implicated in CTE, although most overlap with those associated with other neurodegenerative diseases (Cao et al., 2017). One example is Apolipoprotein E [APOE]. The ε4 allele of APOE, a gene involved in cholesterol and lipid metabolism in the brain, has long been associated with AD and is now implicated in CTE as well (Eisenbaum et al., 2024; Atherton et al., 2022). APOE ε4 appears to exacerbate tau hyperphosphorylation and neuroinflammation, processes central to CTE pathology (Gandy and Dekosky, 2012). In athletes or veterans with repetitive head impacts, carriers of APOE ε4 may show increased susceptibility to disease, and mechanistic studies suggest that the allele impairs lipid transport and metabolism, which are essential for neuronal repair after trauma (Mannix and Whalen, 2012). This dysfunction can reduce recovery capacity and increase vulnerability to degeneration (Yang et al., 2023). These candidate genes point toward a common theme in which genetic variation influences the brain's capacity to repair injury, clear pathological proteins, and regulate inflammatory responses following trauma.

In addition to APOE, variation in microtubule-associated protein tau [MAPT], which encodes the tau protein, further influences disease susceptibility (Colom-Cadena et al., 2013). Tau stabilizes microtubules in neurons, but variation in MAPT haplotypes influences tau aggregation (Leveille et al., 2021). The H1 haplotype is more often linked to tau aggregation, and studies report a higher prevalence of the H1/H1 genotype in CTE patients (Pedicone et al., 2024). This may influence both the regional deposition of abnormal tau and the severity of neurodegeneration (Han et al., 2025).

TMEM106B is also emerging as an important genetic factor (Cherry et al., 2018). This gene regulates lysosomal function and modulates microglial activity. A study of 86 deceased male athletes with confirmed CTE showed that a minor allele of a coding single-nucleotide polymorphism [SNP] in TMEM106B (rs3173615) was associated with reduced phosphorylated tau, lower levels of inflammation, and decreased odds of ante-mortem dementia (Cherry et al., 2018). Variations in TMEM106B may therefore confer partial protection by enhancing lysosomal clearance of aggregates, whereas dysregulation of this gene could impair degradation and promote tau accumulation and cellular stress (Feng et al., 2021).

Genome-wide approaches in post-mortem CTE brains have identified additional candidate pathways, although their interpretation is complicated by bias. Brain donors typically had symptoms and histories of repetitive trauma, excluding resilient individuals who never developed disease (Butler et al., 2025). Despite this limitation, genome-wide studies remain important for hypothesis-free discovery. One consistent finding has been upregulation of immune-related genes, particularly CCL2, which encodes chemokine ligand 2 (Cherry et al., 2020b). In postmortem tissue, CCL2 levels in the dorsolateral frontal cortex correlated with years of American football play, microglial and CD68-positive cell densities, and severity of CTE pathology (Cherry et al., 2020b). CCL2 appears to drive microglial recruitment and activation, reinforcing cycles of inflammation and tau deposition. Other transcriptomic analyses show evidence of mitochondrial dysfunction, with altered expression of genes involved in oxidative phosphorylation and the electron transport chain, suggesting that impaired energy metabolism may amplify cellular stress early after repetitive trauma (Labadorf et al., 2023).

Current evidence supports a model in which genetic background modulates the response to repetitive brain injury, influencing disease risk, regional vulnerability, and progression rather than determining disease onset independently. Candidate genes such as APOE, MAPT, and TMEM106B highlight mechanisms involving lipid metabolism, tau aggregation, and lysosomal function. Genome-wide and transcriptomic studies implicate immune responses such as CCL2 upregulation, mitochondrial dysfunction, ECM remodeling, and ribosomal stress. While most human data come from late-stage post-mortem brains, integrating genomic data from asymptomatic individuals, patients with mild repetitive trauma, and experimental models will be essential for disentangling causal drivers from secondary changes and for identifying new biomarkers and therapeutic targets. These genetic insights underscore the need to integrate molecular susceptibility with diagnostic frameworks and emerging biomarkers to enable earlier and more precise identification of CTE during life.

For decades, the pathological gold standard for chronic traumatic encephalopathy (CTE) has been post-mortem human brain examination, which defined the disease's signature lesions, mapped their anatomical distribution, and established staging frameworks that anchor the field (McKee et al., 2013; McKee et al., 2015) (Fig. 3A). This work delineated perivascular, sulcal-depth accumulations of hyperphosphorylated tau, clarified co-pathologies, and linked regional vulnerability with clinical phenotypes, providing fundamental knowledge for further hypothesis generation (McKee et al., 2023; McKee et al., 2013; McKee et al., 2015). Building upon these insights, non-invasive imaging in living humans provides valuable information on structure, connectivity, metabolism, and molecular burden, although it remains limited in its ability to resolve causal mechanisms (Kim et al., 2012; Holleran et al., 2017; Lin et al., 2015; Stern et al., 2019). Despite these advances, critical limitations remain. Post-mortem research, by definition, captures disease's late stages and cannot resolve initiating events; brain banks are biased toward donors with known head-impact histories and symptoms; and even the best clinical imaging cannot routinely deliver causal interrogation of mechanisms. Across human studies, ethical constraints, high costs, and heterogeneity in exposure, genetics, and comorbidities further complicate inference and intervention (Petraglia et al., 2014; McKee et al., 2013; McKee et al., 2015; Kim et al., 2012; Holleran et al., 2017; Lin et al., 2015; Stern et al., 2019).

In this context, experimental in vivo models emerged to address gaps that human work cannot readily fill (Fig. 3B). Rodent and large-animal platforms recreate defined biomechanics and timelines, enabling observation from the earliest minutes through progression and resolution, systematic control of injury dose and interval, and targeted pathway perturbation under reproducible conditions (Casson et al., 2014; Machado et al., 2023). These models are particularly valuable for defining causal relationships between injury parameters and downstream molecular and cellular responses, which cannot be directly tested in human studies. Widely used paradigms, including controlled cortical impact, weight-drop, lateral fluid percussion (LFP), and blast-overpressure exposures, allow investigators to probe how repeated low-level insults accumulate into persistent cellular and network dysfunction (Osier and Dixon, 2016; Machado et al., 2023; Namjoshi et al., 2014). They therefore serve as controlled platforms to dissect how injury dose, frequency, and biomechanics shape the trajectory from acute damage to chronic neurodegeneration. The LFP model, in particular, is among the most extensively validated rodent paradigms for TBI, producing a graded injury by applying fluid-pressure pulses to the intact dura. Repetitive LFP protocols have been shown to induce progressive tau phosphorylation, axonal injury, and neuroinflammation that partially recapitulate early-stage CTE pathology, making it a widely used platform for studying the transition from acute TBI to chronic neurodegeneration (Shultz et al., 2012; Fronczak et al., 2022). Across species, these approaches recapitulate elements of the human condition, such as progressive tau phosphorylation, white-matter injury, neuroinflammation, and behaviorally measurable deficits, with reports of corpus callosum involvement and neocortical p-tau after repetitive or blast injuries and severity-dependent cognitive and motor impairment in larger brains (Sugahara et al., 2025; Schwerin et al., 2021; Kinder et al., 2019). At the same time, these models have important constraints: species differences in cytoarchitecture, immune system, and lifespan limit direct translation; many models do not develop decades-scale neurodegeneration without genetic manipulation, risking divergence from native human pathobiology; and scaling to species with closer brain mechanics imposes substantial costs, infrastructure demands, and ethical burden (Casson et al., 2014; Machado et al., 2023; Kinder et al., 2019). Inducing injuries in animals remains an ethically challenging endeavor, even when scientifically justified as essential to understanding disease mechanisms. These realities motivate complementary strategies that retain experimental control and causal access while reducing reliance on high-burden animal cohorts.

A pivotal question in experimental CTE research is not merely whether a given model induces brain injury, but whether it accurately replicates the specific pathological hallmarks that characterize CTE in humans (McKee et al., 2023; Turner et al., 2015b). In rodent models, repetitive closed-head impact and LFP paradigms consistently reproduce perivascular tau phosphorylation, white matter axonal injury, microglial activation, and memory and executive function deficits, features that correspond to early-stage CTE (Stages I-II) (Petraglia et al., 2014; Namjoshi et al., 2014; Sugahara et al., 2025). However, the characteristic sulcal-depth localization of p-tau lesions, a defining neuropathological feature of human CTE, is difficult to model in lissencephalic rodent brains, which lack the cortical folding that concentrates strain at the depths of the sulci (Fagan et al., 2020; Holleran et al., 2017). Large-animal models, particularly ferrets, pigs, and sheep, offer gyrencephalic (folded) brain architecture that more closely approximates the biomechanical environment of human TBI, enabling better recapitulation of sulcal-depth strain patterns and the perivascular lesion distribution that defines CTE pathology (Schwerin et al., 2021; Kinder et al., 2019). In vitro 3D human models address a distinct yet complementary set of features: they can recapitulate early post-injury mitochondrial dysfunction, neuroinflammatory signaling, tau phosphorylation at the cellular level, and neurovascular unit disruption within a human genetic background, and can do so longitudinally over extended periods (Liaudanskaya et al., 2023; Hinrichsen et al., 2026; Liaudanskaya et al., 2020; Rouleau et al., 2020; Stanton et al., 2025). Together, these model systems are not redundant but complementary, with each addressing distinct aspects of CTE pathobiology. Importantly, no single model recapitulates the full spectrum of CTE, reinforcing the need for integrative approaches that combine mechanistic insight, biomechanical relevance, and human-specific biology.

In vitro systems address key limitations of human and in vivo models by providing human-relevant biology in controllable and scalable experimental formats (Fig. 3C). Three-dimensional engineered tissues and advanced co-cultures permit direct access to the earliest post-injury minutes and hours, longitudinal tracking over weeks to months, and causal interrogation of pathways with genetic or pharmacological perturbations that are impractical in people and costly in animals (Liaudanskaya et al., 2023; Xie et al., 2024; Cairns et al., 2016; Coe et al., 2025; Kansakar et al., 2025; Hinrichsen et al., 2025). In vitro platforms allow precise control over cellular composition, enabling investigators to define which cell types are included and in what proportions. Neurons, astrocytes, microglia, oligodendrocytes, endothelial cells, and pericytes can be combined in defined proportions, with additional control over developmental stage and donor background, including the use of patient-derived induced pluripotent stem cells that capture specific risk alleles and sex-linked differences (Liaudanskaya et al., 2023; Stanton et al., 2025; Hinrichsen et al., 2025). The type of injury applied can also be tuned to reflect human exposures, including controlled indentation, uniaxial or biaxial stretch, pressure pulses, or blast-like overpressure. Readouts from these systems span multiple biological scales, from mitochondrial and cytoskeletal integrity at the organelle level to synaptic function and multicellular network activity (Liaudanskaya et al., 2023; Liaudanskaya et al., 2020; Rouleau et al., 2020; Xie et al., 2024; Cairns et al., 2016; Coe et al., 2025; Kansakar et al., 2025; Hinrichsen et al., 2025; Liaudanskaya et al., 2019; Snapper et al., 2023; Jgamadze et al., 2023; Lancaster et al., 2013; Lovett et al., 2020; Slanzi et al., 2020). Compared with animal work, these models reduce ethical burden, lower experimental costs, enable higher-throughput designs, and make it possible to test mechanisms iteratively and systematically.

Despite these advantages, in vitro models have important limitations. They do not reproduce whole-body homeostasis, systemic immune responses, or lifestyle effects on epigenetic background. Some constructs are mechanically fragile and cannot withstand high-strain paradigms, and no in vitro system fully captures the variability of human exposures and comorbidities (Xie et al., 2024). Nonetheless, long-term cultures can support maturation and aging trajectories beyond what is possible in short-lived animal experiments, allowing analysis of disease initiation, progression, and partial recovery with resolution at the level of specific cell types (Liaudanskaya et al., 2023; Xie et al., 2024; Cairns et al., 2016; Coe et al., 2025; Kansakar et al., 2025; Hinrichsen et al., 2025). This capability uniquely positions in vitro systems to dissect early pathobiological events and cell-type-specific responses that are not accessible in human or animal studies. Critically, these models address several limitations of human and in vivo studies: they allow access to the earliest disease stages not captured in post-mortem tissue, they permit causal testing of pathways that cannot be interrogated through observational imaging, they incorporate human genetics without cross-species confounds, and they reduce reliance on large animal cohorts while retaining the ability to model repetitive, sub-concussive injuries (Liaudanskaya et al., 2023; Xie et al., 2024; Cairns et al., 2016; Coe et al., 2025; Kansakar et al., 2025; Hinrichsen et al., 2025; Snapper et al., 2023). In this way, in vitro systems complement human and animal research by linking exposure to cell-type-specific injury responses and potential biomarkers, thereby accelerating translational discovery without replacing other approaches.

Among available platforms, only a subset can reliably withstand and reproduce traumatic injury paradigms (Lovett et al., 2020). Three-dimensional scaffold-based cultures currently provide the most stable environments for applying controlled injuries within multicellular human systems, whereas organoids, assembloids, and microfluidic chips hold strong potential for mechanistic discovery but require further optimization to ensure reproducibility under injury conditions. For example, recent work by our group using long-term, multicellular human 3D scaffold-based brain models has demonstrated the ability to reproducibly model key features of injury-induced neurodegeneration in a controlled human system (Jun et al., 2025). These platforms integrate neurons, astrocytes, microglia, and vascular components within a structurally stable scaffold, enabling the application of defined mechanical injury paradigms and longitudinal tracking of pathological progression. Following injury, these systems recapitulate early mitochondrial dysfunction, sustained neuroinflammatory signaling, and progressive tau phosphorylation, while also capturing neurovascular unit disruption and impaired intercellular communication. Importantly, the ability to maintain these cultures over extended periods allows interrogation of how acute injury evolves into chronic pathological states, providing a direct experimental bridge between repetitive trauma and neurodegenerative-like phenotypes in a human biological context. Moving forward, progress will depend on combining injury-capable engineered tissues with emerging organoid and chip technologies to create models that capture both the complexity and chronicity of human CTE. Taken together, in vivo and in vitro model systems are not ends in themselves, but essential components of a broader translational pipeline linking biomechanical exposure to molecular pathology and, ultimately, clinical disease. Rodent and large-animal models provide mechanistic access to the earliest post-injury cascades and the biomechanical determinants of sulcal-depth lesion formation, while human 3D in vitro systems offer genetic fidelity, cell-type resolution, and longitudinal access to pathobiological progression in a human biological background (Liaudanskaya et al., 2023; Hinrichsen et al., 2026; Rouleau et al., 2020; Stanton et al., 2025). The biomarkers emerging from these systems, including p-tau, NfL, GFAP, and markers of TDP-43 co-pathology, are the same analytes being validated in living patient cohorts through initiatives such as the DIAGNOSE CTE project (Alosco et al., 2021b). This convergence between experimental and clinical biomarker discovery represents one of the most promising paths toward establishing the in-life diagnostic tools that the field critically lacks. As 3D human platforms become increasingly injury-capable and longitudinally stable (Liaudanskaya et al., 2023; Hinrichsen et al., 2026; Rouleau et al., 2020; Stanton et al., 2025), their potential to serve as direct preclinical testing grounds for candidate biomarkers and therapeutics, before validation in costly animal studies or clinical trials, will only grow. Importantly, no single platform captures the full spectrum of CTE, and progress will depend on integrating mechanistic insight from controlled experimental systems with biomarker validation in human cohorts.

A key emerging direction in CTE modeling is the integration of microfluidic technologies with advanced 3D human brain platforms. Current state-of-the-art multicellular systems, such as the miBrain model, which incorporates all six major brain cell types, including neurons, astrocytes, microglia, oligodendrocytes, pericytes, and brain microvascular endothelial cells derived from patient iPSCs, have demonstrated functional BBB properties, neuronal activity, and tau phosphorylation in a human genetic background (Hinrichsen et al., 2026; Stanton et al., 2025). However, these systems currently operate under static culture conditions, limiting their ability to model the dynamic hemodynamic and mechanotransductive forces that govern neurovascular unit (NVU) function in vivo. Integrating microfluidic perfusion into such platforms, creating true ‘Brain-on-a-Chip’ configurations, would enable simulation of cerebrovascular flow, shear stress, and the propagation of pressure waves through the NVU that occur during repetitive head impacts (Campisi et al., 2018). Such systems could provide a mechanistic bridge between biomechanical exposure and molecular pathology by modeling both cellular injury responses and disruption of perivascular clearance pathways.
