The rapidly evolving understanding of the long-term dangers of concussion and repeated sub-concussive impacts has created a sense of urgency towards the prevention of traumatic brain injury (TBI) and tau pathology (eg, chronic traumatic encephalopathy). One innovative proposal for protecting the brain is that of woodpecker biomimicry—a concept that gained attention following a 1976 publication by Philip May and colleagues.1

Google Scholar show that axonal injury was present in eight of ten woodpecker brains studied, with four specimens having indicators of perivascular neurofibrillary tangles; additionally, two of three woodpecker brains examined for tau protein were found to have accumulations. These findings were not present in any of the five control (non-woodpecker) birds and suggest that the woodpecker's morphological characteristics and biomechanical strategies might be insufficient to prevent brain damage. It remains unknown how closely woodpecker-specific tau pathology resembles chronic traumatic encephalopathy, and whether woodpeckers experience neurobehavioral changes and other clinical signs characteristic of human beings with TBI. Google Scholar translation of the unique structural and functional adaptations of woodpeckers into clinical applications does not seem to be a high clinical priority. Woodpeckers remain an interesting subject of study in ornithology and evolutionary biology, but woodpecker biomimicry might be a distraction to more effective strategies for preventing TBI and chronic traumatic encephalopathy in human beings.
