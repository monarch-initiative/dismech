X-Linked Intellectual Disability–Cerebellar Hypoplasia Syndrome (OPHN1 / Oligophrenin-1 Syndrome)

Comprehensive Disease Characteristics Report Target: MONDO:0010337 · OMIM:300486 · Causal gene: OPHN1 (oligophrenin-1), Xq12 Evidence sources are tagged as [Human clinical], [Mouse], [in vitro/iPSC], or [Computational/aggregated].

Summary / Direct Answer

X-Linked Intellectual Disability–Cerebellar Hypoplasia Syndrome is a rare X-linked recessive neurodevelopmental disorder caused by loss-of-function variants in OPHN1 (Xq12), which encodes oligophrenin-1, a 91-kDa Rho-GTPase-activating protein (RhoGAP) highly expressed in fetal brain (Billuart et al., 1998, Nature, 9582072). Loss of oligophrenin-1's GAP activity removes negative control of RhoA, causing constitutive RhoA/ROCK/MLC2 pathway hyperactivation plus brain-region–specific cAMP/PKA dysregulation, which impairs dendritic spine maturation, AMPA-receptor recycling and synaptic-vesicle endocytosis. Clinically this produces intellectual disability with early hypotonia, motor/speech delay, behavioral problems, seizures, strabismus, and the hallmark neuroimaging triad of cerebellar vermis hypoplasia, ventriculomegaly, and fronto-temporal atrophy (Bergmann et al., 2003, 12805098). The disorder is non-degenerative and compatible with long survival; no approved disease-modifying therapy exists, but the ROCK/PKA inhibitor fasudil reverses behavioral and anatomical deficits in adult Ophn1 mice (Meziane et al., 2016, 27146843), establishing a mechanism-based therapeutic rationale.

1. Disease Information

Overview. A monogenic (Mendelian) syndromic form of X-linked intellectual disability (XLID). Core features: non-progressive intellectual disability (mild–severe), neonatal hypotonia, delayed motor/speech milestones, behavioral disturbance (hyperactivity, attention problems), epilepsy, ocular abnormalities (strabismus, nystagmus), and distinctive brain MRI showing cerebellar (vermis) hypoplasia with enlarged lateral ventricles. Originally described as a cause of nonspecific XLID, it was later recognized as a distinct, recognizable syndrome once brain imaging was systematically applied (Bergmann 2003, 12805098; Zanni 2005, 16221952). [Human clinical]

Phenotypic spectrum note. Beyond classic syndromic/non-syndromic XLID, rare OPHN1 variants have also been reported in autism spectrum disorder and (childhood-onset) schizophrenia cohorts (20479760 30378261; "Variants of this gene have been also identified in autism spectrum disorder and schizophrenia," 27146843), consistent with a shared synaptic-plasticity pathophysiology. [Human clinical]

2. Etiology

Primary cause — genetic. Hemizygous loss-of-function variants in OPHN1 in males. "Here we report the characterization of a new gene on the long arm of the X-chromosome (position Xq12) and the identification in unrelated individuals of different mutations that are predicted to cause a loss of function" (Billuart 1998, 9582072). [Human clinical]

Genetic risk factors. - Causal variants: nonsense, frameshift (small indels), splice-site, whole/partial-gene and exonic deletions (e.g., exon 19 frameshift deletion, 12805098; exon 21 deletion, 32872024; exons 21–22 deletion, 18261018; intragenic deletions, 30534410), and rarer non-disruptive in-frame/missense variants affecting the BAR or C-terminal proline-rich domains (33638601 24105372). - Dosage sensitivity: a Xq12q13.1 duplication encompassing OPHN1 also produced syndromic MR (18512229), indicating sensitivity to both loss and gain of copy number. - Sex: male sex is the principal risk factor (X-linked recessive); heterozygous females are usually unaffected carriers.

Environmental risk factors. None established; this is a monogenic disorder. No toxin, infectious, dietary, or occupational exposure is implicated as causal.

Protective factors. In carrier females, favorably skewed X-inactivation (preferential silencing of the mutant X) is protective against manifestation; conversely, skewing toward the mutant allele can unmask disease in females (23416624). No protective pharmacologic/dietary factors are known. [Human clinical]

Gene–environment interactions. Not applicable/none documented.

4. Genetic / Molecular Information

  • Causal gene: OPHN1 (HGNC:8183; NCBI Gene 4983; OMIM 300127), Xq12, ~26 exons, encoding oligophrenin-1 (UniProt O60890; 802 aa; ~91 kDa). Protein domains: N-terminal BAR domain, PH domain, central RhoGAP domain, C-terminal proline-rich region.
  • Variant classes (ACMG): predominantly pathogenic/likely-pathogenic loss-of-function — nonsense, frameshift, canonical splice-site, and multi-exon/whole-gene deletions. Non-disruptive in-frame deletions/missense variants (BAR or proline-rich domains) are rarer and pose VUS interpretation challenges. "the identified genomic variants involve the region encoding the central RhoGAP domain … predicted to result in a complete loss of function" (33638601).
  • Functional consequence: loss of function (loss of RhoGAP catalytic activity → failure to terminate RhoA signaling). Copy-number gain (duplication) is also pathogenic (18512229).
  • Origin: germline; inherited from carrier mothers or de novo; somatic mosaicism reported (a missense variant in mosaic state, 29510240). Not a somatic/cancer gene.
  • Allele frequency: pathogenic variants are absent/ultra-rare in gnomAD; OPHN1 shows constraint against LoF. [Computational/aggregated]
  • Modifier genes: none firmly established. The main modifier of female expression is the X-inactivation ratio (epigenetic), not a trans-acting gene (23416624).
  • Epigenetic/post-transcriptional: OPHN1 mRNA undergoes A-to-I RNA editing (ADAR2) and alternative splicing during human brain development, peaking at gestational weeks 20–33 — relevant to normal regulation and potentially to phenotype modulation (Barresi 2014, 24637888). [Human/in vitro]
  • Chromosomal abnormalities: intragenic and contiguous Xq12–q13.1 deletions and one duplication; detectable by array-CGH/MLPA (18261018 18512229 30534410 32872024).

6. Mechanism / Pathophysiology

Ordered causal chain (initiating lesion → clinical manifestation)

  1. Loss-of-function variant in OPHN1 (nonsense/frameshift/splice/deletion) results in absent or non-functional oligophrenin-1 protein (loss of RhoGAP activity). [Demonstrated — 9582072]
  2. Loss of RhoGAP activity leads to failure to accelerate GTP hydrolysis on RhoA → constitutive RhoA activation. [Demonstrated/inferred from GAP biochemistry — 9582072]
  3. Constitutive RhoA leads to ROCK (Rho-kinase) hyperactivation, confirmed in OPHN1-deficient human iPSC neurons where ROCK remains elevated throughout differentiation. [Demonstrated — 27160703]
  4. ROCK hyperactivation results in increased phosphorylation of myosin phosphatase target subunit (MYPT1) and MLC2 → excess actomyosin contractility and dysregulated actin cytoskeleton. [Demonstrated — 27160703 35563851]
  5. Branch A (postsynaptic): cytoskeletal dysregulation + loss of OPHN1–Homer1b/c and OPHN1–endophilin A1 interactions lead to mispositioned spine endocytic zone, impaired AMPA-receptor recycling, and failure of dendritic spine maturation (excess immature filopodia). [Demonstrated — 24966368 19481455 17728457]
  6. Sub-branch (activity-dependent plasticity): group-I mGluR activation normally triggers rapid dendritic OPHN1 translation (mGluR1-dependent, FMRP-independent) required for mGluR-LTD and the associated persistent reduction of surface AMPARs via endophilin A2/3; loss of OPHN1 results in impaired protein-synthesis-dependent LTD — a role separable from its basal-synaptic Rho-GAP/Homer1b/c function. [Demonstrated — 22017989]
  7. Branch B (presynaptic): OPHN1 loss leads to brain-region-specific cAMP/PKA pathway dysregulation and defective synaptic-vesicle endocytosis, abolishing PKA-dependent presynaptic plasticity in hippocampus and amygdala. [Demonstrated — 24298161 19481455]
  8. Combined pre/postsynaptic dysfunction results in altered short-term plasticity (reduced paired-pulse facilitation) and impaired synapse maturation without neuronal loss. [Demonstrated, mouse — 17728457]
  9. During development, failure of Rho-GTPase–dependent control of neurite/dendrite outgrowth and migration contributes to cerebellar (vermis) hypoplasia and ventricular enlargement; neuropathology shows Purkinje-cell heterotopia in cerebellar white matter. [Demonstrated, human fetus — 23416624]
  10. These circuit- and structural-level defects manifest clinically as intellectual disability, hypotonia, ataxia, epilepsy, strabismus, and behavioral disturbance. [Human clinical — 12805098 16221952]

7. Anatomical Structures Affected

  • Organ/system: central nervous system (nervous system, UBERON:0001016); principally brain (UBERON:0000955).
  • Primary sites: cerebellum (UBERON:0002037), especially cerebellar vermis (UBERON:0004086) — hypoplasia/lower-vermian agenesis; cerebral lateral ventricles (UBERON:0002285) — enlargement; frontal and temporal lobes (UBERON:0016525 / UBERON:0001871) — atrophy; hippocampus (UBERON:0002421) — alterations with certain BAR-domain variants (24105372); cisterna magna — cystic dilatation.
  • Secondary involvement: visual axis (strabismus/nystagmus); genitourinary (hypogenitalism in a subset).
  • Tissue/cell level: neural tissue; affected cell populations include cortical/hippocampal pyramidal neurons and cerebellar Purkinje cells (heterotopic in white matter, 23416624).
  • Subcellular compartments (GO CC): dendritic spine (GO:0043197), postsynaptic density (GO:0014069), presynaptic active zone / synaptic vesicle (GO:0008021), endocytic zone / clathrin-coated pit, actin cytoskeleton (GO:0015629).
  • Lateralization: brain involvement is generally bilateral, though asymmetric cerebellar hypoplasia has been described (12805098).

8. Temporal Development

  • Onset: congenital/neonatal (hypotonia at birth; some structural anomalies detectable prenatally on fetal MRI/ultrasound, 23416624). Pattern: chronic, insidious developmental presentation.
  • Progression: the intellectual disability is static/non-progressive (a neurodevelopmental, not neurodegenerative, condition). Epilepsy may evolve and behavioral issues fluctuate; cerebellar/ventricular findings are stable structural features. Disease duration: chronic, lifelong.
  • Remission: no spontaneous remission of ID; seizures/behavior may improve with treatment.
  • Critical period: synaptic and cerebellar development (fetal–early postnatal) is the window of structural vulnerability. Importantly, mouse data show functional deficits remain pharmacologically reversible in adulthood (27146843), implying a therapeutic window beyond early development. [Mouse]

9. Inheritance and Population

  • Inheritance: X-linked recessive (OMIM 300486). Affected individuals are predominantly hemizygous males; carrier females are usually unaffected but can manifest with skewed X-inactivation (23416624).
  • Penetrance: essentially complete in hemizygous males; expressivity is highly variable, even among siblings sharing the same variant (33638601 29510240).
  • Germline mosaicism / de novo: both inherited and de novo variants occur; somatic mosaicism has been documented (29510240).
  • Anticipation: not applicable (not a repeat-expansion disorder).
  • Founder effects / consanguinity: no founder mutation; consanguinity not a driver (X-linked). Recurrent large deletions arise independently.
  • Carrier/allele frequency: pathogenic alleles ultra-rare; absent from population controls (gnomAD).
  • Epidemiology: ultra-rare; precise prevalence/incidence undefined (Orphanet: prevalence unknown). Background context: nonspecific XLMR affects ~0.15–0.3% of males, of which OPHN1 accounts for ~1%, rising to ~12% among males with ID plus cerebellar anomalies (9582072 16221952). Only on the order of dozens of families reported worldwide.
  • Sex ratio: strongly male-predominant; rare symptomatic females with skewed XCI or structural variants.
  • Geographic distribution: worldwide, no endemic clustering; reported across European, Brazilian, Japanese and other populations (24105372 30534410).

10. Diagnostics

  • Recommended approach: clinical suspicion (male with ID + hypotonia + cerebellar/ventricular MRI findings ± epilepsy/strabismus) → brain MRI → molecular genetic testing.
  • Imaging (key diagnostic): brain MRI demonstrating cerebellar vermis hypoplasia, enlarged (often rostral) lateral ventricles, fronto-temporal atrophy, and cisterna magna dilatation (12805098 24105372). Fetal MRI/ultrasound may detect ventriculomegaly and vermian hypoplasia prenatally (23416624). (RadLex imaging features; no disease-specific biomarker on labs.)
  • Genetic testing:
  • Exome/genome sequencing (WES/WGS) and XLID / epilepsy–ID multigene panels including OPHN1 are first-line for undiagnosed ID; "We emphasize the power of next generation techniques (X-exome sequencing, whole-exome sequencing and targeted multi-gene panel) to expand the phenotypic and mutational spectrum of OPHN1-related ID" (29510240).
  • Single-gene sequencing of OPHN1 where phenotype is classic.
  • Chromosomal microarray (CMA)/array-CGH and MLPA to detect intragenic/whole-gene deletions and duplications, which are a common mutation class (18261018 18512229 30534410 32872024). "we suggest that MLPA analysis should be performed as an alternative diagnostic test" (32872024).
  • Laboratory tests/biomarkers: no specific blood/urine biochemical marker; routine labs normal. Karyotype usually normal. mtDNA/repeat-expansion testing not applicable.
  • Electrophysiology: EEG for seizure characterization (e.g., myoclonic-astatic epilepsy).
  • Histopathology (research/autopsy): cerebellar Purkinje-cell heterotopia (23416624).
  • Clinical criteria / differential diagnosis: no formal diagnostic criteria; diagnosis is molecular. Differentials include other XLID-with-cerebellar-hypoplasia genes (e.g., CASK, OPHN1 vs. ATRX, ARX), Dandy-Walker/pontocerebellar hypoplasias, and syndromic ID with posterior-fossa anomalies; distinguish by gene, facial gestalt, and MRI pattern (16506132 30534410).
  • Screening: no population newborn screening (not actionable/biochemical). Cascade carrier testing of at-risk female relatives once the familial variant is known; prenatal/preimplantation testing available for known variants.

11. Outcome / Prognosis

  • Survival/mortality: not life-limiting per se; the condition is compatible with normal or near-normal lifespan. No disease-specific mortality rate established; risks relate to complications of severe epilepsy or profound disability (generic).
  • Morbidity/disability: lifelong intellectual disability is the dominant morbidity, with variable needs for educational, communication, mobility and behavioral support; epilepsy and strabismus add morbidity. ICF-level impairments in cognition, communication, and adaptive functioning.
  • Disease course: static cognitive deficit; seizures and behavior may be modifiable with treatment. Recovery of baseline cognition is not expected; early intervention improves functional outcomes (generic rehabilitation evidence).
  • Prognostic factors: severity correlates loosely with overall phenotype but there is no robust genotype–phenotype correlation, limiting individual prognostication (33638601). Presence/severity of epilepsy and degree of ID are the main functional predictors.
  • Prognostic biomarkers: none validated.

12. Treatment

No approved disease-modifying therapy exists; management is symptomatic and supportive.

  • Supportive/rehabilitative (standard of care): special education, speech/language therapy (NCIT:C15193), physical therapy (NCIT:C15327) and occupational therapy (NCIT:C15335) for hypotonia/motor delay, behavioral/psychological support. Ophthalmologic management of strabismus (corrective lenses/surgery).
  • Pharmacotherapy (symptomatic): antiseizure medications for epilepsy (agent chosen by seizure type, e.g., valproate/levetiracetam; NCIT:C264 anticonvulsant class); stimulants/behavioral agents for ADHD-type symptoms as clinically indicated. Pharmacogenomics: no disorder-specific PGx guidance; standard ASM PGx (e.g., HLA-B for carbamazepine) applies generically.
  • Mechanism-based experimental therapy — ROCK/PKA inhibition (fasudil): the leading translational candidate. "chronic treatment in adult mouse with Fasudil, is able to counteract vertical and horizontal hyperactivities, restores recognition memory and limits the brain ventricular dilatation observed in Ophn1" (27146843); fasudil "fully rescued" hippocampus/amygdala learning deficits (24298161) and corrected OPHN1-deficient iPSC neurons (27160703). CHEBI: fasudil (CHEBI:75172); target NCIT: Rho-associated kinase inhibitor. Human trials specific to OPHN1 are not yet reported. [Mouse / in vitro]
  • Gene/cell/RNA-based therapy: none in clinical development; conceptually plausible given defined LoF mechanism, but no vectors/ASOs reported for OPHN1.
  • Surgical/interventional: none disease-specific (standard epilepsy or strabismus surgery as indicated).
  • Treatment strategy: multidisciplinary (neurology, genetics, developmental pediatrics, ophthalmology, rehabilitation); personalized to seizure type and developmental needs. Combination (pathway inhibitor + symptomatic care) is the envisaged future model.

13. Prevention

  • Primary prevention: not possible (genetic); risk reduction centers on reproductive counseling.
  • Genetic counseling: carrier mothers have 50% transmission risk to each son (affected) and 50% of daughters are carriers (X-linked recessive). Cascade testing of maternal relatives once the variant is identified.
  • Secondary prevention (reproductive options for known-variant families): prenatal diagnosis (CVS/amniocentesis) and preimplantation genetic testing (PGT-M); prenatal imaging may detect ventriculomegaly/vermian hypoplasia.
  • Tertiary prevention: seizure control, early developmental intervention, behavioral and physical therapies to limit secondary complications and maximize function.
  • Immunization / public-health / environmental interventions: not applicable.

14. Other Species / Natural Disease

  • Orthologous gene: mouse Ophn1 (NCBI Gene 94190; MGI); rat Ophn1 (NCBI Gene 309989). Protein is highly conserved; RhoGAP function conserved across vertebrates.
  • Taxonomy of experimental hosts: Mus musculus (NCBITaxon:10090); Homo sapiens (NCBITaxon:9606) iPSC-derived neurons.
  • Natural disease in other species: no naturally occurring OPHN1 disease reported in companion animals or wildlife (OMIA: none established); veterinary relevance is limited to engineered models.
  • Comparative biology: mouse Ophn1 KO recapitulates ventricular enlargement, spatial-memory and social deficits, hyperactivity, and dendritic-spine immaturity, confirming evolutionary conservation of the RhoGAP–synapse mechanism (17728457).
  • Transmission/zoonosis: not applicable (genetic disorder).

15. Model Organisms

  • Mouse (primary mammalian model): constitutive and conditional Ophn1 knockouts (Mus musculus). Phenotype recapitulation: enlarged lateral ventricles, impaired spatial memory, impaired social behavior, hyperactivity, reduced paired-pulse facilitation, and reduced mature dendritic spines — "we generated a mouse model of ophn1 deficiency and showed that it mimics the ventricles enlargement without affecting the cerebellum morphoanatomy" (17728457). Model limitation: the mouse does not reproduce the human cerebellar hypoplasia, an important divergence. Models also revealed presynaptic cAMP/PKA deficits (24298161) and enabled fasudil rescue proof-of-concept (27146843). [Mouse]
  • Human iPSC-derived neurons (in vitro cellular model): OPHN1 loss-of-function iPSC neurons show sustained ROCK hyperactivity, impaired neuronal differentiation/morphology, and rescue by fasudil/ROCK inhibition (27160703). Useful for human-specific neurodevelopmental mechanisms and drug testing. [in vitro]
  • Primary neuronal cultures / rat hippocampus: used to dissect OPHN1–Homer1b/c (AMPAR recycling; 24966368) and OPHN1–endophilin A1 (SV endocytosis; 19481455) functions, and postnatal developmental changes in synaptic transmission/plasticity (35563851).
  • Model types available: knockout, conditional/floxed (region- and stage-specific), knockdown; patient-derived iPSC lines. Resources: MGI (mouse), IMPC/IMSR, Cellosaurus (iPSC lines).
  • Research applications: synaptic plasticity mechanisms, dendritic spine biology, Rho-GTPase signaling in cognition, and preclinical ROCK-inhibitor pharmacology.

Supported vs. Refuted Hypotheses

Supported: - OPHN1 LoF causes the disorder via RhoA/ROCK hyperactivation (9582072 27160703). ✔ - Disease reflects synaptic dysfunction (pre- and postsynaptic) rather than neurodegeneration (17728457 24298161 19481455 24966368). ✔ - Cerebellar hypoplasia is variable/non-obligate; phenotype spans non-syndromic to syndromic (29510240 33638601). ✔ - ROCK/PKA inhibition (fasudil) reverses deficits in adulthood in models (27146843 24298161 27160703). ✔

Refuted / not supported: - That OPHN1 causes purely non-syndromic MR — refuted once imaging revealed consistent cerebellar/ventricular signatures (12805098 16221952 24105372). - A tight genotype–phenotype correlation — not supported; expressivity varies even within families (33638601). - An environmental/infectious contribution — none found; disorder is monogenic.

Limitations and Future Directions

  • Ultra-rare disorder: epidemiologic parameters (precise prevalence, incidence, natural-history cohorts, QoL metrics) are undefined.
  • No human therapeutic trials; fasudil evidence is preclinical (mouse/iPSC) — translation and a human treatment window remain to be established.
  • Model gap: mouse lacks cerebellar hypoplasia, limiting study of that key human feature; new models (e.g., cerebellar organoids) are needed.
  • Variant interpretation of non-disruptive (BAR/proline-rich, missense) variants is challenging and would benefit from standardized functional assays.
  • Roles of RNA editing/alternative splicing and X-inactivation as modifiers warrant further study.
