| **Foundational human reports** | Biallelic **HERC1** variants were reported by Ortega-Recalde et al. (2015) in syndromic overgrowth and intellectual disability. Nguyen et al. (2016) reported a nonsense variant associated with intellectual disability, megalencephaly, thick corpus callosum, and cerebellar atrophy. Aggarwal et al. (2016) reported a splice-site variant with macrocephaly, facial dysmorphism, and syndromic intellectual disability. These details were recovered from later bibliographies rather than the complete original articles. (pqac-00000022, pqac-00000024) | Not applicable. | The retrieved evidence does not provide reliable syndrome-wide prevalence, penetrance, or phenotype percentages. It also does not establish a uniform molecular consequence for every HERC1 variant. |
| **Direct human evidence: 2024 case** | A 2-year-old boy had developmental delay, seizures, macrocephaly, hypotonia, coarse or dysmorphic facies, speech and cognitive impairment, unilateral hypertrophy, thoracic scoliosis, and a normal MRI at 6 months. WES reported homozygous **HERC1 c.1280dup** and a heterozygous **PMP22** exon 1–5 duplication. The HERC1 variant was absent from the population databases examined and classified as likely pathogenic. The reported notation, **c.1280dup (p.Ile469Aspfs*33)**, is internally inconsistent and requires transcript-level validation before knowledge-base deposition. (pqac-00000008, pqac-00000009, pqac-00000011) | The reported frameshift was predicted to cause premature truncation or nonsense-mediated decay, but this was not tested directly in patient cells. (pqac-00000007, pqac-00000013) | The pathogenic PMP22 duplication confounds attribution of every neurologic or musculoskeletal feature to HERC1. The child was also too young for some CMT1A manifestations. This single case does not establish population frequency, prognosis, or treatment response. (pqac-00000004, pqac-00000005) |
| **Human cellular mechanism: 2020** | Schwarz et al. reported a homozygous HERC1 gain-of-function variant in MDFPMR. The study was identified through a later bibliography because its complete primary text was not retrieved. (pqac-00000024) | The variant was reported to cause **mTORC1 hyperactivation** and **reduced autophagy during cellular catabolism**, indicating that at least one MDFPMR allele may act through gain of function rather than simple HERC1 deficiency. (pqac-00000010, pqac-00000024) | The study does not demonstrate mTORC1 hyperactivation for all MDFPMR variants, particularly truncating alleles, or establish that mTOR inhibitors are safe or effective in affected people. |
| **Herc1-knockout mouse: 2023** | The model provides biological plausibility for skeletal abnormalities reported in some patients but is not patient-outcome evidence. | HERC1 deficiency increased C-RAF abundance and ERK and p38 activation during osteogenic differentiation, altered osteoblast and osteocyte transcription, increased the RANKL-to-OPG ratio and osteoclastogenesis, and produced osteopenia in adult mice. Young females showed particular susceptibility associated with reduced androgens. (pqac-00000015, pqac-00000016, pqac-00000019) | The model does not prove that human scoliosis, elongated digits, or other skeletal manifestations arise through the same pathway. Murine sex effects cannot yet be extrapolated to humans. |
| **Tambaleante mouse: 2018** | No direct patient-level inference is possible; the spontaneous murine **Herc1 Gly483Glu** allele is not an established human MDFPMR allele. | Mutant mice showed delayed action-potential propagation, compact-myelin abnormalities, altered terminal Schwann cells, and increased phosphorylated Akt-2, supporting roles for HERC1 in peripheral myelination and neuromuscular function. (pqac-00000006) | The model does not establish peripheral neuropathy as a universal MDFPMR feature and does not reproduce the complete human syndrome. |
| **Tambaleante mouse extension: 2025** | This study supplies recent functional context but not clinical validation. | Mutant brain and muscle showed no detected change in measured proteasomal activity. Muscle p62 expression and clathrin and synaptophysin levels were altered, while pharmacologic autophagy inhibition impaired evoked neurotransmitter release. The authors concluded that the allele disrupts autophagy and synaptic-vesicle recycling without measurably impairing proteasomal function. (pqac-00000010, pqac-00000020) | The findings do not establish a disease-specific biomarker or treatment and should not be generalized automatically from this murine allele to all human loss-of-function or gain-of-function variants. |
| **Overall evidence judgment** | Human evidence supports an **autosomal-recessive, biallelic HERC1-associated neurodevelopmental syndrome**, designated MDFPMR, OMIM 617011, and MONDO:0014863. The small, heterogeneous case literature prevents robust phenotype-frequency estimates. Open Targets associates this disease specifically with HERC1 rather than HERC2. (pqac-00000000, pqac-00000001, pqac-00000025) | Convergent evidence implicates ubiquitin-dependent protein regulation, membrane and synaptic-vesicle homeostasis, autophagy, mTORC1, RAF–ERK and p38 signaling, myelination, and bone remodeling. The dominant mechanism may depend on the allele and tissue. | **HERC2-related Angelman-like neurodevelopmental disorder is distinct** and must not be merged with HERC1-associated MDFPMR. No retrieved evidence establishes a curative therapy, an approved molecularly targeted treatment, or an MDFPMR-specific clinical trial. (pqac-00000002) |


*Table: Evidence hierarchy separating direct human genetic observations from cellular and mouse-model mechanisms in HERC1-associated MDFPMR. It highlights variant-specific uncertainty, the 2024 PMP22 confound, and findings that should not be generalized to HERC2 or all HERC1 alleles.*
