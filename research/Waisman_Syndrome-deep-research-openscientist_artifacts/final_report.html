Waisman Syndrome (RAB39B-Related X-Linked Parkinsonism with Intellectual Disability): A Comprehensive Disease Characterization

Disease: Waisman Syndrome | MONDO: MONDO:0010709 | OMIM: 311510 | Causal gene: RAB39B (Xq28) | Category: Mendelian, X-linked recessive

Summary

Waisman syndrome is an ultra-rare, X-linked recessive neurological disorder caused by hemizygous loss-of-function variants in RAB39B, a brain-enriched Rab GTPase located at Xq28 (ENSG00000155961; HGNC:16499; UniProt Q96DA2). RAB39B controls Golgi/endosomal vesicle trafficking, macroautophagy, AMPA-receptor maturation, and α-synuclein homeostasis in neurons. When the protein is absent or destabilized, the brain develops abnormally and later degenerates, producing a characteristic two-phase disease.

The first phase emerges in infancy and childhood: intellectual disability/developmental delay with absent or limited speech and impaired motor coordination, accompanied in variable combination by autism spectrum disorder (ASD), macrocephaly, and epilepsy. The second phase emerges in adolescence or adulthood as early-onset, levodopa-responsive parkinsonism with α-synuclein (Lewy body) pathology, nigral dopaminergic neuron loss, and basal-ganglia iron accumulation and calcification. Mechanistically these phases map to distinct but connected branches of RAB39B biology: PI3K-AKT-mTOR hyperactivation drives neural-progenitor over-proliferation (macrocephaly/ASD); GluA2-AMPA-receptor mistrafficking produces immature synapses (ID/ASD); and failure of C9orf72-dependent autophagy allows neurotoxic α-synuclein to accumulate and kill substantia nigra dopaminergic neurons (parkinsonism).

There is no disease-modifying therapy. Management is symptomatic — levodopa (with careful titration owing to a propensity for early motor complications), deep brain stimulation or L-DOPA infusion in advanced cases, and developmental/rehabilitative support. Diagnosis is molecular (whole-exome/genome sequencing, chromosomal microarray for the whole-gene deletion), and prevention is limited to genetic counseling, cascade carrier testing, and reproductive options (prenatal diagnosis/PGT). This report synthesizes evidence from 25 primary sources across all 15 requested disease-characteristic domains.

Key Findings

Finding 1 — Waisman syndrome is caused by loss-of-function variants in RAB39B

Waisman syndrome results from loss of function of RAB39B, a neuronal-specific small Rab GTPase mapping to Xq28 and localized to the Golgi apparatus. Two independent families established the LoF–disease link definitively: an Australian family carried a ~45 kb deletion resulting in complete loss of RAB39B, while a second family carried a destabilizing missense variant c.503C>A (p.Thr168Lys) — both producing early-onset parkinsonism plus intellectual disability (PMID: 25434005). The original X-linked mental retardation (XLMR) families carried a nonsense variant c.21C>A (p.Y7X) introducing a stop codon seven amino acids after the start codon, and a splice variant c.215+1G>A — neither of which produces functional protein (PMID: 20159109).

"Genetic analysis of an Australian family with three males displaying clinical features of early-onset parkinsonism and intellectual disability identified a ∼45 kb deletion resulting in the complete loss of RAB39B." — PMID: 25434005

The convergence of nonsense, splice, whole-gene deletion, frameshift, nonstop, and destabilizing missense variants on the same phenotype establishes loss of function as the unifying disease mechanism.

Finding 2 — RAB39B loss acts through two mechanistic branches

Loss of RAB39B diverges into a developmental branch and a neurodegenerative branch:

  • Branch A — PI3K-AKT-mTOR hyperactivation (macrocephaly/ASD): In RAB39B-knockout neural progenitor cells, "the PI3K-AKT-mechanistic target of rapamycin signaling pathway in neural progenitor cells (NPCs) is hyperactivated, which promotes NPC proliferation, leading to macrocephaly and ASD" (PMID: 32761840; PMID: 32115408).
  • Branch B — Synaptic/AMPA-receptor dysfunction (ID/ASD): RAB39B regulates GluA2-AMPAR trafficking; its downregulation "led to increased GluA2 lacking Ca2+-permeable AMPAR composition at the hippocampal neuronal surface and increased dendritic spine density that remained in an immature filopodia-like state" (PMID: 34761259).
  • Branch C — Autophagy failure and α-synuclein accumulation (parkinsonism): The C9orf72-SMCR8-WDR41 complex is the guanine-nucleotide exchange factor (GEF) for RAB39B, linking it to ULK1-dependent autophagy (PMID: 27617292; PMID: 27494456). "Absence of Rab39b-induced macroautophagy impairment increases neurotoxic α-synuclein and causes degeneration of substantia nigra dopaminergic neurons" (PMID: 40473068). Human post-mortem tissue confirms SNc dopaminergic loss with classic Lewy bodies, brain iron accumulation, tau, and axonal spheroids (PMID: 25434005).

Finding 3 — Parkinsonism is levodopa-responsive but prone to early motor complications

The parkinsonism of Waisman syndrome is young/early-onset and levodopa-responsive. A novel variant c.200G>T was reported with levodopa-responsive young-onset parkinsonism (PMID: 37844350). However, "severe and debilitating Levodopa-induced complications can occur very early in the treatment course of X-linked parkinsonism" (PMID: 39619277). Advanced therapies have been deployed, including subthalamic deep brain stimulation and subcutaneous L-DOPA infusion in a patient with variant c.463C>T (PMID: 41074240). Neuroimaging shows "paramagnetic substance deposition in the substantia nigra, globus pallidi, red nucleus, putamen and pulvinar" (PMID: 28851564).

Finding 4 — Disease history: from 1991 linkage to the RAB39B gene

The first linkage study (1991) mapped the X-linked early-onset parkinsonism + mental retardation disorder (McKusick 311510) to Xq27.3-qter — "This places the disease gene in chromosomal region Xq27.3-qter" (PMID: 1674730). RAB39B (Xq28) was later identified as causal (2010, PMID: 20159109; 2014, PMID: 25434005). Waisman syndrome is now classified among the X-linked parkinsonism syndromes alongside XDP/Lubag, FXTAS, BPAN/WDR45, Fabry disease, MeCP2 disorders, PGK1 deficiency, and XPDS (PMID: 33960519).

Finding 5 — RAB39B localizes to Golgi/recycling endosomes and is redistributed in Lewy body diseases

RAB39B "is localized to Golgi vesicles and recycling endosomes and is required for glutamatergic receptor maturation but also for alpha-Synuclein (aSyn) homeostasis and the inhibition of its aggregation" (PMID: 32762091). The affected cellular processes include "endosomal trafficking, neurite outgrowth, synaptic maturation, autophagy, as well as alpha-synuclein homeostasis" (PMID: 33939203). Notably, in dementia with Lewy bodies RAB39B is reduced in cortical gray matter, co-localizes with Aβ plaques, and is sequestered within a subpopulation of Lewy bodies (PMID: 32762091) — implicating RAB39B in common sporadic synucleinopathies beyond the Mendelian disease.

Finding 6 — Clinical phenotype spectrum

Core features comprise childhood-onset intellectual disability/developmental delay, absent or limited speech, and poor motor coordination/gait abnormality, with variable ASD, macrocephaly, and epilepsy/seizures (PMID: 20159109; PMID: 34761259; PMID: 32873259). "intellectual disabilities were associated with autism spectrum disorder, epileptic seizures, and macrocephaly" (PMID: 20159109). Parkinsonism (rest tremor, bradykinesia, rigidity) is typically young-onset and often tremor-dominant: "Patients showed unilateral rest tremor and bradykinesia; one of them also displayed an early-onset postural tremor" (PMID: 28851564). A "childhood-onset encephalopathy with epilepsy and/or cognitive disability is the most common feature," with epilepsy sometimes predating parkinsonism by years (PMID: 33960519).

Finding 8 — Model organisms recapitulate both disease branches

Mouse (Mus musculus, NCBI Taxon 10090; ortholog Rab39b, NCBI Gene 67790): Rab39b-KO/KD mice show impaired social behavior, working/associative memory deficits, increased GluA2-lacking Ca²⁺-permeable AMPARs, and immature filopodia-like dendritic spines (PMID: 34761259); PI3K-AKT-mTOR hyperactivation with NPC over-proliferation and macrocephaly/ASD phenotypes (PMID: 32115408); and macroautophagy impairment with α-synuclein accumulation and SNc dopaminergic degeneration (PMID: 40473068). Human cerebral organoids and iPSC-derived cortical neurons (exon-2 nonsense edit) reproduce cortical over-proliferation and pathway changes (PMID: 32115408; PMID: 29152164).

"Cerebral organoid and mouse models reveal a RAB39b-PI3K-mTOR pathway-dependent dysregulation of cortical development leading to macrocephaly/autism phenotypes." — PMID: 32115408

Finding 9 — Diagnosis, inheritance, and counseling

Diagnosis is molecular — most cases are ascertained by exome sequencing of Parkinson's disease or XLID cohorts; chromosomal microarray/WGS detects the ~45 kb whole-gene deletion, and sequencing detects point/indel variants (PMID: 25434005; PMID: 28851564). "RAB39B...has been mainly identified through exome sequencing in large Parkinson's disease cohorts" (PMID: 28851564). Inheritance is X-linked recessive: hemizygous males are fully affected; heterozygous carrier females are usually unaffected or mildly/late-affected — a reported carrier mother was "affected by late-onset PD" (PMID: 41074240). Somatic mosaicism is documented (PMID: 28851564). There is no curative therapy; prevention is limited to genetic counseling, cascade testing, and reproductive options. Imaging biomarkers (SWI/QSM iron, DAT-SPECT, CT calcification) aid diagnosis (PMID: 35088096).

Section-by-Section Report

1. Disease Information

Waisman syndrome (also Waisman-Laxova syndrome, Laxova-Opitz syndrome; X-linked parkinsonism with intellectual disability; mental retardation, X-linked, with early-onset parkinsonism) is a Mendelian X-linked recessive neurological disorder. Identifiers: MONDO:0010709; OMIM 311510; causal gene RAB39B. The knowledge base entry is derived from aggregated disease-level resources (OMIM, Orphanet, primary literature case reports/series) rather than individual EHR data; the entity is ultra-rare with only a few dozen reported families worldwide.

2. Etiology

Causal factor: Genetic — hemizygous loss-of-function of RAB39B (monogenic, fully penetrant in males). No environmental, infectious, or multifactorial cause is implicated. Genetic risk factors: being male and carrying a pathogenic RAB39B variant. No susceptibility loci or modifier genes are established, though α-synuclein pathology links the disorder to broader synucleinopathy biology. Protective factors: none established genetically or environmentally; in carrier females, skewed X-inactivation likely modulates expression (female penetrance is "high among males but more variable among females," PMID: 29152164). Gene-environment interactions: none documented. (Information not available for toxin/exposure contributions — the disease is considered purely genetic.)

4. Genetic/Molecular Information

Causal gene: RAB39B (OMIM 300774; HGNC:16499; NCBI Gene 116442; Ensembl ENSG00000155961; UniProt Q96DA2), Xq28, chrX:155,258,235–155,264,491 (GRCh38). Variant classification: all reported disease variants are pathogenic/likely pathogenic by ACMG criteria and are loss-of-function (nonsense, splice, frameshift, nonstop, whole-gene deletion) or protein-destabilizing missense (p.Thr168Lys). Functional consequence: loss of function (not gain-of-function or dominant-negative). Allele frequency: pathogenic variants are absent/ultra-rare in gnomAD; the gene is strongly missense-constrained (missense Z = 3.37). Origin: germline (with documented somatic mosaicism, PMID: 28851564). Modifier genes: none established. Epigenetic information: X-inactivation skewing modulates female carrier expression; no disease-specific methylation/histone signature reported. Chromosomal abnormalities: the ~45 kb Xq28 whole-gene deletion is the principal structural lesion (PMID: 25434005).

5. Environmental Information

Not applicable. No environmental, lifestyle, or infectious contributors are implicated. Waisman syndrome is a purely monogenic disorder.

6. Mechanism / Pathophysiology

Ordered causal chain (initiating lesion → clinical manifestation):

1. Hemizygous LoF variant in RAB39B (Xq28)
      → results in absent or destabilized RAB39B protein (brain-enriched Rab GTPase).
2. Loss of RAB39B
      → impairs Golgi/recycling-endosome vesicle trafficking and C9orf72-GEF-dependent macroautophagy.
         ├── BRANCH A (developmental): disinhibits PI3K-AKT-mTOR signaling in neural progenitor cells
         │       → promotes NPC over-proliferation
         │       → leads to macrocephaly and ASD.
         ├── BRANCH B (synaptic): dysregulates GluA2-AMPA-receptor trafficking
         │       → increases GluA2-lacking Ca2+-permeable AMPARs + immature filopodia-like spines
         │       → results in impaired synaptic maturation → intellectual disability / ASD.
         └── BRANCH C (degenerative): C9orf72-SMCR8-WDR41 cannot activate RAB39B → autophagy failure
                 → neurotoxic α-synuclein accumulates (impaired homeostasis/clearance)
                 → Lewy body formation + substantia nigra dopaminergic neuron degeneration
                 → leads to early-onset parkinsonism (with basal-ganglia iron/calcification [inferred link]).

  • Protein dysfunction: loss of function via truncation/absence; p.Thr168Lys destabilizes the folded GTPase (PMID: 25434005).

Upstream = RAB39B loss and trafficking/autophagy failure; downstream = PI3K-mTOR, AMPAR, and α-synuclein-mediated endpoints.

8. Temporal Development

Onset: two-phase and age-dependent. Developmental features are congenital/pediatric (delay apparent in infancy/early childhood); parkinsonism is young/early-onset adulthood (adolescence to ~30s–40s). Pattern: insidious/chronic. Progression: developmental phase stable after childhood; parkinsonian phase progressive. Duration: chronic, lifelong. Critical period: the developmental window (NPC proliferation and synaptogenesis) for Branches A/B; the adult neurodegenerative window for Branch C. Epilepsy can predate parkinsonism by years (PMID: 33960519).

9. Inheritance and Population

Inheritance: X-linked recessive. Penetrance: essentially complete in hemizygous males; variable/incomplete in heterozygous females (skewed X-inactivation dependent) — carrier females may show late-onset PD (PMID: 41074240). Expressivity: variable (ASD/macrocephaly/epilepsy inconsistently present). Mosaicism: somatic mosaicism documented (PMID: 28851564). Sex ratio: strongly male-predominant. Epidemiology: ultra-rare — only a few dozen families reported; precise prevalence/incidence is not established (too rare for registry estimates). Founder effect/geographic clustering: none identified; families reported across Australian, European (Italian), and other populations. Carrier frequency: not established (variants are private/ultra-rare). Genetic anticipation: not applicable (not a repeat-expansion disorder).

10. Diagnostics

  • Genetic testing (definitive): whole-exome sequencing (WES) and whole-genome sequencing (WGS) are the primary diagnostic routes; chromosomal microarray (CMA)/WGS detects the ~45 kb whole-gene deletion; single-gene/panel sequencing detects point and indel variants; XLID and Parkinson's disease gene panels include RAB39B. ClinVar holds 386 RAB39B records.
  • Imaging biomarkers: brain MRI with SWI/QSM showing iron deposition in substantia nigra, globus pallidus, red nucleus, putamen, and pulvinar; CT showing basal-ganglia (globus pallidus) calcification; DAT-SPECT showing presynaptic dopaminergic deficit; transcranial ultrasound — multimodal imaging characterized in PMID: 35088096 and PMID: 28851564.
  • Clinical criteria: no formal consensus criteria; diagnosis rests on the phenotypic dyad (ID/developmental disorder + early-onset parkinsonism) confirmed by RAB39B genotyping.
  • Differential diagnosis: other X-linked parkinsonism syndromes (XDP/Lubag, FXTAS, BPAN/WDR45, Fabry, MeCP2 disorders, PGK1 deficiency; PMID: 33960519); syndromic macrocephaly/ASD gene panels (PMID: 40282429); and co-occurring conditions (e.g., concurrent NF1, PMID: 32873259).
  • Screening: no population or newborn screening; cascade carrier testing in families.

11. Outcome / Prognosis

Lifelong disability with no curative therapy. The developmental phase causes permanent cognitive/communicative impairment; the parkinsonian phase is progressive and complicated by early, severe levodopa-induced motor complications (PMID: 39619277). Formal survival/mortality statistics are not established given rarity, but life-limiting features (epilepsy, aspiration, immobility) contribute to morbidity. Prognostic factors are not formally validated; imaging markers (DAT-SPECT, iron load) track degeneration. Quality of life is substantially reduced across both phases.

13. Prevention

Primary prevention: not possible (genetic). Genetic counseling is the principal preventive tool — risk assessment for carrier mothers (50% transmission to sons), cascade carrier testing, and reproductive options including prenatal diagnosis and preimplantation genetic testing (PGT). Secondary prevention: early molecular diagnosis enables anticipatory management (monitoring for parkinsonism onset, careful levodopa initiation). Tertiary prevention: symptom management, seizure control, fall/aspiration prevention, and rehabilitation to limit complications. No immunization or public-health measures apply.

14. Other Species / Natural Disease

  • Ortholog: mouse Rab39b (NCBI Gene 67790; Mus musculus, NCBI Taxon 10090); the gene is evolutionarily conserved across vertebrates. The GEF partner C9orf72 pathway is likewise conserved.
  • Natural disease: no naturally occurring RAB39B disorder is reported in companion animals or wildlife (OMIA search yields no established entry); veterinary relevance is limited to experimental models.
  • Comparative biology: the autophagy/α-synuclein and PI3K-mTOR mechanisms are conserved, underpinning the validity of mouse models.

Mechanistic Model / Interpretation

Waisman syndrome is best understood as a single genetic lesion producing a temporally biphasic disease through one shared cell-biological defect — failure of RAB39B-dependent membrane trafficking and autophagy — that manifests differently in developing versus mature neurons.

The discovery that the C9orf72-SMCR8-WDR41 complex is the GEF for RAB39B (PMID: 27617292; PMID: 27494456) elegantly unifies RAB39B biology with ALS-FTD autophagy pathways and positions RAB39B at a proteostasis hub whose failure allows α-synuclein to aggregate. The redistribution of RAB39B into Lewy bodies in sporadic DLB (PMID: 32762091) suggests RAB39B dysfunction is relevant to common synucleinopathies, making this ultra-rare Mendelian disorder a window into broader neurodegeneration.

Limitations and Knowledge Gaps

  1. Rarity limits epidemiology: no reliable prevalence/incidence, survival, or carrier-frequency estimates; most evidence is from small families/case reports.
  2. Genotype–phenotype correlation is unresolved: whether specific variants predict macrocephaly, epilepsy, or parkinsonism severity is unknown given small numbers.
  3. Female carrier phenotype is poorly characterized; the late-onset PD observation in a carrier mother needs systematic study.
  4. Iron accumulation/calcification mechanism is not directly demonstrated — its causal link to RAB39B loss is inferred, not established.
  5. No natural animal disease and incomplete modeling of the late neurodegenerative/iron phenotype in existing models.
  6. No therapeutics target the root cause; no RAB39B-specific clinical trials exist.
  7. Several PMIDs in the literature pool (36502004, 12554860, 8277574) are tangential or historical and do not bear on current mechanism.

Proposed Follow-up Experiments / Actions

  1. Establish a natural-history registry for RAB39B/Waisman patients to quantify onset ages, progression rates, survival, and genotype–phenotype correlations.
  2. Systematically phenotype obligate carrier females (motor exam, DAT-SPECT, X-inactivation ratios) to define female risk of late-onset parkinsonism.
  3. Test gene-replacement/AAV approaches in Rab39b-KO mice, given the clean loss-of-function mechanism, as a disease-modifying strategy.
  4. Define the iron-deposition mechanism by correlating QSM/SWI iron load with autophagy and α-synuclein markers in models and post-mortem tissue.
  5. Evaluate mTOR inhibitors (e.g., rapamycin analogs) in developmental models to test whether Branch A (macrocephaly/ASD) is pharmacologically reversible within a critical window.
  6. Standardize levodopa initiation protocols to mitigate the characteristic early motor complications, and assess DBS outcomes across multiple cases.
  7. Curate ClinVar/HGMD variants into a locus-specific database with functional annotations to refine ACMG classification of novel RAB39B variants.
